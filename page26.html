Anthropic rival says companies can (mostly) skip the pricey models

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On the same day that Anthropic went public in the largest IPO on record, with investor enthusiasm for the AI lab through the roof, far away at the Slush startup conference in Helsinki, the topic du jour was open-source everything.

That’s the enormous opportunity being chased by companies like Fireworks AI, as enterprises seek ways to avoid paying frontier prices for their AI needs.

Fireworks runs AI models for other businesses. It specializes in open-weight models, which anyone can download, and helps customers tune them on their own data so they run faster and cheaper than the models of Anthropic, OpenAI and other heavyweights and are harder for rivals to copy. Customers have included Uber, Shopify and Notion. Co-founder Chenyu Zhao and I spoke on stage toward the end of the day.

Zhao and many others believe companies are gravitating toward platforms like his because frontier models are pricey and, in most cases, he says, unnecessary unless you need the very latest and greatest generalized intelligence. There are also growing concerns that frontier labs absorb too much of their customers’ know-how, even if only through the prompts used to get answers, a worry Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella raised in July.

Fireworks has also gotten its first black eye, having disclosed two security incidents this fall. In one, hackers reached API keys and tokens stored as secrets for a limited number of accounts; in the other, attackers breached its cloud provider, made their way into a development system, and nabbed customer data, including billing records and some datasets.

Notion says AI chat transcripts from fewer than 1% of its workspaces were exposed, but it has disconnected from Fireworks. Uber says hackers stole the names, addresses, birth dates and driver’s license numbers of about 150,000 drivers through a vendor that TechCrunch identified as Fireworks. Fireworks will have to win back the confidence of customers like them.

Fireworks, assigned a $17.5 billion valuation by its investors in summer, is reportedly in “early talks” for yet another round, though Zhao declined to comment.

There’s money chasing everyone in the category, seems like. Rival companies Together AI and Baseten have announced massive rounds in recent months. On Wednesday, Palo Alto Networks said it will buy Applied Compute, which, like Fireworks, helps companies train and run custom AI models on their own data. The deal for the startup, founded just a year ago by former OpenAI researchers, is $4.5 billion, all of it in stock.

Here’s some of what Zhao had to say. This conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

You spent a decade at Google, including managing a 50-person engineering team on Google Cloud Vertex AI. Why leave when you did?

When you meet great co-founders, it’s obvious within five minutes that they’re really smart. I was sold after talking to Lin [Qiao] and Dima [Dzhulgakov]. It’s also important to find people who are genuinely nice, people you’ll enjoy working with.

How do you describe Fireworks to a CEO who has never heard of it?

I describe Fireworks as a specialized intelligence platform. OpenAI and Anthropic are building general intelligence. We use open models, which are often smaller, cheaper and faster, and let companies add their own data to specialize them. That way you own your AI rather than being beholden to giant AI conglomerates. If you’re just using Opus or Sonnet, that’s not a differentiator.

How far behind are open models right now?

A few years ago I’d have said a year, a year and a half. Then came DeepSeek and a waterfall of fantastic open models. These days it’s at most six months behind, probably closer to three.

If the models are so close behind, why are businesses slow to switch?

Frontier models are a household brand, and before you find product-market fit, using the best model is low risk. But once you pass 10,000 or 100,000 users and you’re spending $10,000 to $100,000 a month on tokens, it’s time to think about open source.

In July, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella warned that companies using proprietary AI models pay twice, once in money and again in know-how. Do you think that warning had a material effect?

We think we’ll live in a two-model world, with frontier and open models in parallel. You want the best intelligence for high-level planning, but you don’t need a frontier model for basic arithmetic. That’s why there’s so much interest in routing, and why people now think about cost per task, not just cost per token.

Anthropic went public today. What do you think of its valuation? There’s one scenario in which it just continues to cement its place in this sort of oligopoly; there’s another where it starts to lose traction.

On the record: I’ll probably call my broker tonight and buy a few shares. This is a long-term bet. In 10 years AI usage will be at a completely different scale, and that takes an incredible amount of capital, which is why these companies are going public.

Fireworks has had a couple of security breaches recently. What do you tell customers?

Every company is probably seeing an unprecedented frequency of attacks. For our incident, a lot of the motivation was LLM jacking: hunting through providers for API keys and secrets to sell tokens on illicit marketplaces. Our promise is zero data retention for inference traffic, and there’s no indication that has been compromised.

Notion has disconnected from Fireworks. Did you lose other customers?

I don’t think I can publicly name the specific customers that were impacted. Some, such as Notion and Cursor, have gone public already. It was a small subset of our customers that had this data impact.

How did that customer data get exposed, given your zero-retention promise?

Notion was using batch prediction, where you upload a dataset that’s stored at rest, so it’s not a zero-retention product by nature. We’re moving those services to an online flow so we keep data only as long as we need it.

Jensen Huang left Nvidia last week, and Nvidia has backed Fireworks since 2024. Does that affect you?

It’s been a blow to the industry, and we feel for Jensen and his family. But Nvidia has always been a strong supporter of open source, and I don’t see that changing. We expect it to keep investing in Nemotron, and we’re excited to keep partnering with them.

PCT Weeks 21 & 22: Aftermath

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

It was nice to be in a new town. Leavenworth is modeled on a Bavarian village, so I woke up knowing there’d be plenty to check out today. We walked into town to grab breakfast at a cafe that served expensive but delicious waffles, before venturing out to see the sights. It was a Saturday, and with Oktoberfest starting today and a marathon on tomorrow, the town seemed to buzz with a low thrum of energy.

We meandered along the river, and then the streets of the main part of town, which grew increasingly busy as the day went on. We had bratwursts for lunch, and were dismayed when we jumped online and found out that Oktoberfest tickets had sold out. A little dejected, we headed back to the KOA for an afternoon rest. Well, I rested. Sass planned a little DIY Oktoberfest featuring stops at the breweries in town. In the early evening we headed into town to get started. We were still dressed in our trail clothes, so we stuck out like sore thumbs – as soon as we arrived in town we were picked out by locals as PCT hikers. They seemed pretty excited to see us, maybe because most hikers hadn’t made it to Leavenworth this year thanks to the wildfire trail closures.

At the second brewery we went to, we instantly spotted a table of dirtbags. As we waited in line to order, we discussed in hushed tones whether they were thru-hikers or not. Drinks in hand, we walked past their table and discovered they were looking at us with the same curious eyes. Then I recognised a hiker I’d last seen in Big Bear, and all of a sudden we were sitting with them, exchanging news. This has been one of the best parts of trail life: being instant friends with other hikers. They were just finishing their drinks before heading into Oktoberfest. My FOMO was real.

Once they’d gone, and Sass and I had finished our drinks, we decided to at least go and ask if there was a walk-up ticket. And we were in luck! Soon we had cleared security and were on the hunt for our next drink. We paused by one stage where a stein-holding competition was in full swing. The winner really went for it – not satisfied with outlasting his competition, he held that thing up for minutes longer, clearly testing himself. Then we checked out a tent where live music was playing, where we found our friends from earlier and were quickly invited to join them. It was a fun evening of exchanging more trail news, people watching, and singing along to Bavarianised songs. It felt very appropriate that one of them was ‘I’m Gonna Be (500 Miles)’ by the Proclaimers – basically the unofficial anthem of thru-hikers everywhere.

Sass and I had planned to thru-hike the Enchantments Traverse today, but when we woke up we were feeling the consequences of Oktoberfest. So we decided to postpone until tomorrow, instead. We were in no rush; we were planning to meet Catch in Bend in another week or so, and had plenty of time to kill. Plus, the PCT rate at the KOA was easily the cheapest accommodation we were going to find anywhere.

We spent the morning crawling through coffee shops, then headed to the library to research hire cars to get us down to Bend in a couple of days. Then lunch, and back to the KOA for a few hours of rest before grabbing dinner and turning in early, ready for tomorrow’s hike.

📍 Enchantments Traverse | 🥾 35.65 km | ⛰️ +2,392m / -1,733m

We were awake bright and early, heading into town to pick up a coffee and then trying to hitch up to either of the trailheads for the Enchantments Traverse. Bizarrely, we got a lift with someone we knew! It was a couple we’d run into a couple of times on trail, and who’d been at the terminus with us. They’d been in town last night with friends, but had flights to catch later today so they could only drive us to the trailhead closest to town. Fine by us!

The trail started easily as we climbed well-maintained switchbacks. We made excellent time, and were confident we’d be done with the trail sooner than we’d planned, with plenty of daylight left to get a hitch back to town.

How wrong we were. After passing our first few lakes the trail began to enter tougher territory. Soon we were clambering over boulders, and in a couple of spots we lost the trail for a few minutes, but managed to find our way back. It got steeper and steeper. At the top of every climb I thought: this must be the top of the pass. Wrong. Every time.

At least, once the toughest bit of climbing and navigating was out of the way, the scenery was sublime. The larches were in full, golden splendour, and the clear alpine lakes were glimmering in the sunlight. There wasn’t a cloud in the sky.

Sass cracked his head on a tree and ended up with a pretty decent open wound. Hikers streamed past in the other direction, telling us that Aasgard Pass had been pretty hectic. We brushed them off; we’d just done the PCT, how hard could it be?

Bloody hard, it turns out. There was clearly a reason most people hiked this trail in the opposite direction to us. Aasgard was a steep slope covered in both boulders and loose scree. It was a nightmare to downclimb. It took us at least two hours to get down to Colchuck Lake as we moved slowly, keeping our bodyweight low and trying not to twist an ankle or go sliding with the scree. About a quarter of the way down Sass realised that he’d lost his phone. How frustrating, especially since he had no idea where, and we hadn’t exactly been following a trail since it didn’t really exist amongst the rocks. The chances of finding it were slim. Daylight was fading, too; we didn’t have our overnight gear with us, so had no choice but to keep moving. I suggested we could come back tomorrow to look for it, but he said there was no way he was climbing back up this heinous pass. He just hoped his terminus photos had uploaded to the cloud.

We finally finished the pass and I was ready to rip out the final miles past the lake and down to the trailhead. Only the terrain remained rocky and difficult, and progress was much slower than I’d have liked. We ended up hiking for an hour in the dark, hoping against hope that this wouldn’t stop us from getting a hitch at the trailhead. We were glad to see that the carpark was still pretty much full when we arrived. Surely someone would come by soon. We were in luck: a car approached and Sass waved it down, giving the driver his sob story about his lost phone and head injury, and she immediately offered us a lift to town, which we gratefully accepted.

Today we were headed to Wenatchee, a larger town that had a Verizon store where Sass could get a replacement phone, and we could pick up a hire car. We’d originally planned to head to Mount Rainier and hike the open sections of the Wonderland Trail, but yesterday’s hike had beaten us to a pulp. So now we’d switched plans and were just going to grab the car for a day so we could get to Bend.

Sass had no trouble getting a new phone – and his terminus photos had made it to the cloud! – but the hire car was a different story. Neither of us had credit cards, and none of the hire places in town would rent to us without one. We spent several hours trying to figure out a new plan. Eventually, we found a place in Seattle that would let us hire with a debit card. So all we had to do was get to Seattle. We decided the easiest way would be to get the bus back to Leavenworth tomorrow, and hitch from there, since the locals are pretty used to PCT hikers.

We were up in the dark to catch an early bus to Leavenworth. There, we grabbed a coffee and then headed up to the roundabout on the western side of town to try and hitch. We got a ride up to Stevens Pass pretty quickly, but then we were stuck there with our thumbs out for a long while. Eventually I headed up to the cafe to grab some cardboard and make a hitching sign; but while I was there Sass managed to score us a lift and we were on our way to Seattle.

Our hitch dropped us at a Costco on the outskirts, and from there we took an uber to the car hire place. As we drove along the busy highways I could feel myself growing a little sad. I didn’t like being back in this kind of civilisation. It was too much.

We picked up the car and, too scared to attempt driving on the wrong side of the road, I forced Sass to drive us the 6 hours to Bend. And as I doomscrolled in the passenger seat, I came across a post in the Enchantments hiking Facebook group: someone had found Sass’s phone! What were the chances? I put them in touch, then sat back and enjoyed the views of the mountains we’d hiked by only a couple of weeks ago.

The days we spent in Bend were much the same. Our mornings were spent in one coffee shop or another, catching up on journal entries, going through photos, and doomscrolling. We’d head back to the motel for a supermarket lunch. We’d discovered the Bend Ale Trail app, and in the afternoons we made out our mission to visit as many as we reasonably could. When we grew tired, we headed back to the motel and turned on the tv, catching up on all the releases we’d missed while on trail.

Catch arrived in the evening of Day 150. He’d shaved and changed his clothes, and I hardly recognised him. We spent a good hour catching up on what we’d each been up to for the last week or so, then plotted our moves for this final section up to Government Camp and headed to bed early, eager to be getting back on trail.

📍 Mile 2,025.6 | 🥾 37.89 km | ⛰️ +1,293m / -1,037m

I was not excited about going back to trail today. It was mostly because of the weather. The whole time Sass and I had been waiting around in Bend, the weather had been perfect – up to 27 degrees Celcius. But now, we were walking into a cold snap. Still, my desire to finish these miles won, and soon we were using a combination of Uber and hitching to get to the trailhead.

As we drove, the weather only deteriorated. As we passed through Sisters we were subjected to thick sleet. I decided that if conditions were like this at the trailhead, I’d hitch back to town and never mind the miles. But in the end the weather at the trailhead was ok, if a little windy.

We’d already done the first ten miles previously, but with a light layer of snow on the ground the landscape looked entirely different. I’d been hoping to catch a better view of Three Fingered Jack, which had been largely obscured by smoke last time. But this time it was completely socked in by thick white clouds. We began to descend and were brutalised by the wind and lightly falling snow until we reentered treeline. We chatted from time to time, but it was so chilly that conversations didn’t last long. My lips wouldn’t move properly because of the cold. Our lunch break was only a quick pause to dig out our food, which we ate on the move to keep warm.

I loved walking through the snow, it made everything so delicately beautiful. But I was too cold to bother trying to grab my phone out for photos. As the afternoon started to fade into evening I spotted the snowline on the trees, and began hoping that the campsite we were aiming for would be below it. And I was in luck. Although the spot wasn’t as tree protected as I’d have liked, it had to be warmer than camping in the snow.

📍 Mile 2,058.1 | 🥾 53.08 km | ⛰️ +1,446m / -1,894m

Last night’s temperature hadn’t actually been that bad. The night between days 135 and 136 as we headed to the Canadian border had definitely been worse. It seemed like we were going to have much nicer weather today, too. I could already see glimpses of blue sky as we walked beneath the trees.

Mid-morning, the views started to open up and we caught great sights of Mount Jefferson, clouds dancing around its summit and eventually disappearing in the warm morning sun. Suddenly, I was very glad we’d decided to come back and do this section after all.

The climb up to the border of the Mount Hood Wilderness felt tough, though. I’d expected this section to be flat, as I remembered much of Oregon had been. Once we made it to the top, we scouted out some sun-warmed boulders where we paused briefly for lunch. Unfortunately, the sun wasn’t strong enough to keep us warm for long when we weren’t moving, so it wasn’t long before we were on trail again.

We split up for the afternoon, which I enjoyed. I quite like having the trail to myself. And, at the back of the pack, it was really nice to know it was extremely unlikely that any other hikers would come along behind me. I was free of having to watch out for potential tailgaters.

There was a long burn zone, which was surprisingly rocky and boulder-y underfoot. Just as I was thinking it was a miracle I hadn’t tripped yet – I hadn’t even done one of the usual little toe stubs on a tiny rock – all of a sudden, I was falling. I struggled to regain my balance, but a bush was in the way of my feet and soon I had toppled completely. I just managed to save my face from smashing into a rock, but in the process I fell strangely on my wrist, pinning it underneath me. Ouch was an understatement. It was the same pain I’d felt when I rolled my ankle in NorCal, so I thought I’d probably sprained it. Suddenly, the solitude of the trail at this time of year didn’t seem so nice. I was thankful I had my Garmin inReach. Not that I needed it right now, but it definitely gave me some peace of mind.

I struggled to my feet and continued on, trying my best to keep my wrist above my heart. It slowly began to swell to the size of a grape. Catch and Sass were ahead of me, but I hoped that at some stage they’d have stopped to let me catch up. I’d have liked their help getting my wrist compression wrapped. Worst case, we’d already agreed on a campsite, so I’d see them there.

The rest of the day’s miles were pretty tough. My wrist was searing with pain, and walking with it against my chest wasn’t natural or comfortable. Just after I passed the road into Olallie Lake Resort – which had closed for the season – a car drove past. I seriously considered backtracking to the road and trying to hitch literally anywhere, but in the end stubbornness to finish this section won out and I kept hiking.

I arrived at the campsite in the dark. Catch and Sass had been wondering where I was, so I filled them in, got my wrist strapped, and took a dose of ibuprofen. Then we sat together for a quick dinner before retreating to bed.

📍 Mile 2,089.7 | 🥾 51.59 km | ⛰️ +1,172m / -1,359m

My wrist was still painful in the morning, which I’d anticipated. At least today was our final day on trail and I could look forward to sleeping indoors tonight. Breaking camp took me longer than usual, and I had to ask the guys to help stuff my tent into my pack so I could close it.

Today’s trail was nothing particularly special. We stayed beneath the canopy for pretty much the entire day, so the audiobooks came back out as I plodded along. It was extraordinarily flat, though: I could see now why this section was popular for the 24 hour challenge. I tried not to take too many breaks, eager to be done with trail ASAP. And I ended up walking the last couple of miles at dusk, catching a couple of gorgeous glimpses of a rosy Mount Hood through the trees as I approached the trailhead where Catch had left his car.

It was such a nice change to finish the day with a car waiting at the trailhead. We threw our packs in the back and drove to Government Camp, where we had enough service to search for an Airbnb. In the car, I realised I was properly sick. Sass had gotten a little sick on our last day in Bend, and I’d felt the usual warning sign scratchiness of a sore throat on trail. But it was only once I sat in that car, knowing we were done, that I felt the full force of it. I let the guys take the reins on booking the Airbnb, and soon we were inside, cosy and warm. We hadn’t bothered resupplying – the general store was already closed for the day when we arrived, and we didn’t want to drive further afield – so dinner was trail food. Sass handed me a NyQuil, saying as he did so that they never made him drowsy. But within 20 minutes of starting a movie, I was struggling to keep my eyes open, and had to retreat to bed early.

All three of us were sick as we rotted in front of the tv at our Airbnb for three nights. Although trail was really over now, I don’t think any of us had the energy to actually process it. It was kind of nice to have a good reason not to have to move from the couch, too. We spent our time watching a lot of endurance sports documentaries, venturing into Mount Hood Village only to resupply, and later into Government Camp for the sole purpose of grabbing another of the massive donuts from the Huckleberry Inn. Although on the way we also popped our head into the visitor centre and museum, which ended up having some pretty cool exhibits.

On our last morning together Catch kindly drove us to Portland, where we wandered around a used bookstore to pick up copies of our next book club reads, and then enjoyed a final coffee together. I hadn’t really planned what I was going to do next, and after thinking about it on the drive, I decided to book a flight back to Australia for the next day. I knew I wouldn’t enjoy spending time – or money – in cities on the West Coast after being in the backcountry for so long. So in the afternoon we said goodbye to Catch who was heading off to meet another friend, then Sass and I grabbed a quick dinner and stuffed our checked-luggage-only items into cardboard boxes, ready for our respective flights.

The next morning Sass left early to catch his flight. Mine didn’t leave until 7.30pm, so I hung around the motel until checkout, then ventured into town. I didn’t enjoy it. The hustle and bustle was too much for me, especially on a busy weekend, so I soon made my way back to the motel, collected my pack from behind the counter, and headed to the airport early.

The flight was about as good as any long-haul flight can be. I read a little, watched plenty of movies and tv shows, and had a few decent naps. Then there was a 3.5 hour train, followed by a 2 hour drive home. It was only then that I realised how tired I was, and practically collapsed into bed.

A week passed, and I finally gave into my parents’ nagging and went to the GP about my wrist. She sent me for an x-ray, and I discovered I’d fractured it. Unbelievable. I’ve never broken a bone in my life – unless you count my little toe, which I don’t, really. Earlier on trail we’d all discussed what our worst ever injuries had been, and I hadn’t had any good stories to tell. Now, I do.

Bill Hader‘s first film as a director has found its distribution partner. Black Bear has acquired North American distribution rights to “They Know,” Hader’s upcoming psychological horror film that he co-stars in with Linda Cardellini. The growing distributor, which just released “Wicker” nationwide, acquired the rights from financier MRC in what was described as a competitive situation.

Hader co-wrote “They Know” with Duffy Boudreau and produced alongside Bob Graf, with Alyssa Donovan co-producing.

Hader is a three-time Primetime Emmy winner for his work on “South Park” and “Barry.” He co-created the HBO comedy “Barry” with Alec Berg and began directing episodes in Season 2, gradually working his way up to directing the entirety of Season 4. He has long been open about his cinematic influences and his desire to transition into feature filmmaking after the show ended.

Hader recently shared some more details about directing his first feature while speaking at IndieWire’s Los Angeles Future of Filmmaking Summit. Experimental compound burns fat without sacrificing muscle

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new approach to weight loss could help the body burn more fat while preserving muscle, potentially overcoming one of the major drawbacks of existing obesity treatments. Researchers at the University of California, Berkeley, have identified a compound that increases the amount of energy the body uses rather than simply reducing how much food a person eats.

In experiments with mice, the compound improved blood sugar regulation, reduced harmful blood fats, and helped obese animals lose fat without a significant reduction in lean muscle mass. It also increased energy expenditure by up to 18%, even though the mice did not become more physically active or experience an increase in body temperature.

The findings, published in Science Advances, suggest a promising new direction for treating obesity, diabetes, and fatty liver disease. The experimental compound also appeared to enhance the benefits of popular weight-loss medications such as Ozempic and Wegovy when the treatments were used together. However, its effectiveness and safety have not yet been established in humans.

A Different Approach to Weight Loss Than Ozempic

Over the past five years, medications known as GLP-1 drugs have transformed the treatment of obesity and other metabolic conditions, including diabetes and fatty liver disease. Drugs marketed as Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound have helped many patients achieve substantial weight loss while improving their ability to regulate blood sugar.

Despite these benefits, the medications have important limitations. Nausea and other digestive problems can make treatment difficult for some patients. Because these drugs reduce appetite and food consumption, they can also increase the risk of inadequate nutrition and loss of muscle mass. Over time, losing too much muscle can contribute to weakness, frailty, and other health complications.

Berkeley researchers wanted to investigate a fundamentally different strategy. Rather than focusing on reducing calorie intake, they explored whether it would be possible to increase the amount of energy the body burns.

This process, known as energy expenditure, includes the calories used to maintain basic bodily functions, regulate metabolism, and support everyday activities. Increasing energy expenditure could potentially help people lose weight without relying exclusively on eating less.

"Body weight responds to two levers: taking in fewer calories, or spending more energy," said Anders Näär, a professor of metabolic biology and nutrition at UC Berkeley and senior author of the study. "GLP-1s work almost entirely on the first, so we went after the second."

A Compound That Burns Fat While Preserving Muscle

The researchers focused on a molecule called 5-tetradecyloxy-2-furoic acid (TOFA), which has an unusual combination of effects on the body's metabolism.

TOFA interferes with the production of lipids, a broad category of fatty substances that includes cholesterol and triglycerides. These molecules serve important functions, but excessive levels of certain lipids can contribute to metabolic disorders and cardiovascular disease.

At the same time, TOFA activates genes involved in taking up and burning fat for energy. This combination allows the compound to influence both how the body produces fat and how it uses fat as fuel.

In mice, treatment with TOFA produced several encouraging results. The animals became more sensitive to insulin, the hormone that helps move glucose from the bloodstream into cells. Improved insulin sensitivity makes it easier for the body to control blood sugar, an important benefit for people with diabetes or insulin resistance.

The compound also lowered triglyceride levels and improved characteristics of fatty liver disease, a condition in which excess fat accumulates inside liver cells and can eventually interfere with liver function.

Perhaps most notably, obese mice treated with TOFA lost weight primarily through reductions in body fat, without experiencing significant losses of lean muscle mass. This distinction could be especially valuable because maintaining muscle is important for strength, mobility, and long-term metabolic health.

Although TOFA was originally discovered in the 1970s, the new research uncovered additional properties that could make it particularly useful for treating metabolic disease.

The compound belongs to a group of substances known as ACC inhibitors. These compounds interfere with enzymes that help the body manufacture fatty acids, effectively slowing the production of certain fats.

Other ACC inhibitors have previously advanced to mid-stage clinical trials, but none has been approved to treat metabolic diseases. One major obstacle has been an unexpected side effect: many of these compounds increase triglycerides in the blood, potentially raising the risk of heart problems.

The Berkeley team discovered that TOFA does more than inhibit ACC. It also activates two cellular receptors called PPARα and PPARδ, which help regulate the genes responsible for fat metabolism.

These receptors function as molecular switches. When activated, they stimulate processes that allow cells to absorb fatty acids and break them down to produce energy. In effect, TOFA simultaneously reduces the production of certain fats and encourages the body to burn fat that is already available.

In mice, this activity increased energy expenditure by as much as 18%, without requiring additional physical activity or raising body temperature. Unlike other ACC inhibitors, TOFA also did not cause triglyceride levels to rise, potentially because of its additional effects on fat metabolism.

"TOFA appears to engage a coordinated metabolic response," said study first author Justin Y. Lee, a postdoctoral student at UCSF who conducted the research as a Ph.D. student at Berkeley. "It is not simply blocking lipid synthesis. It is also activating energy expenditure pathways that may help the body handle excess lipid and glucose more effectively."

To understand whether these effects could be reproduced with separate treatments, the scientists gave mice a combination of two different compounds. One blocked lipid production, while the other stimulated energy expenditure.

Surprisingly, this two-compound approach was less effective at improving overall metabolic health than TOFA alone. The finding suggests that TOFA's ability to influence multiple metabolic processes through a single molecule may be an important part of its effectiveness.

Could TOFA Make Ozempic and Wegovy More Effective?

The researchers also investigated whether TOFA could improve the results of existing GLP-1 medications.

They tested the compound alongside semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, as well as tirzepatide, which is marketed as Mounjaro and Zepbound.

These medications primarily help people lose weight by reducing appetite and food intake. TOFA, by contrast, targets the other side of the energy balance equation by increasing how much energy the body expends.

When the researchers combined TOFA with these medications in mice, the results were more favorable than those produced by either treatment alone. The combinations led to greater improvements in body weight, blood glucose regulation, insulin levels, and triglycerides.

This suggests that treatments targeting calorie intake and energy expenditure could potentially work together to produce stronger metabolic benefits.

"In our combination experiments, TOFA worked additively or synergistically with the GLP-1 appetite-suppressing drugs, so we view it as complementary rather than as a replacement," Näär said.

In other words, the researchers do not necessarily envision TOFA replacing medications such as Ozempic or Wegovy. Instead, it could eventually become an additional treatment that helps address obesity and related metabolic conditions through a different biological mechanism.

A Promising Discovery, but Human Trials Are Still Needed

Despite the encouraging results, the researchers emphasize that TOFA remains an experimental compound. All the findings so far come from animal studies, and scientists have not yet established whether it is safe or effective in humans.

Further research will be necessary to determine whether the same metabolic improvements can be achieved in people and whether the treatment could offer meaningful advantages over existing medications.

To help move the research toward potential clinical applications, the Berkeley scientists have established a biotechnology company called ReRx Therapeutics. The effort has received support from the university's life sciences entrepreneurship network, including Nucleate and Berkeley SkyDeck.

If the findings eventually translate to humans, TOFA could represent an important addition to the growing range of treatments for obesity and diabetes. By encouraging the body to use more energy while helping preserve muscle, the compound points toward a potentially different way to achieve healthier weight loss.

What a city’s sewage can reveal about the health of the people upstream

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Eight Ohio cities' sewage carried gut bacteria whose mix lined up with local obesity, blood pressure, and stroke rates, a pilot study finds.

During the pandemic, health departments learned to read a city’s sewage for the virus behind COVID-19.

The same water carries something else from every household that flushes into it: the bacteria living in people’s guts.

In eight Ohio cities, the mix of those gut bacteria in the sewage lined up with how the cities scored on chronic disease.

Cities with more of one group of bacteria that produce hydrogen sulfide had higher rates of obesity, high blood pressure, stroke, and several other conditions.

The study rests on 27 samples collected over seven weeks in May and June 2022, and the authors wrote that the links do not show cause.

What they show is that a sample from a treatment plant can carry a readable signal about the health of the people upstream.

Emma Lancaster, then a doctoral student in the College of Public Health at Ohio State University, led the study with Jiyoung Lee, professor and chair of environmental health sciences.

Lancaster is now an environmental health consultant in Boston.

“During the pandemic, the world learned how to use wastewater to track infectious disease. We asked, what else can it tell us?” Lancaster said.

Sewage arriving at a treatment plant is a blend of everything a community has flushed.

One earlier study the authors cite found up to 97% of the microbes in human stool in untreated sewage.

So the water is a pooled sample of a whole population’s gut bacteria, collected without asking anyone to take a test.

Earlier sewage studies elsewhere had tied a community’s bacterial profile to its rates of obesity, high blood pressure, and smoking.

The Ohio team set out to build full gut bacteria profiles for a set of cities and test them against a wide range of health and social measures.

The team collected a liter of untreated wastewater from each of nine treatment plants, on three days in May and June 2022. Two of the plants serve Columbus, the state capital.

The rest serve smaller cities in central and southeastern Ohio: Zanesville, Marysville, Newark, Lancaster, Athens, Circleville, and Marietta.

Together the plants handle sewage from more than 1.3 million people.

The smallest serves about 15,000 and the largest nearly 548,000. So the cities range from small towns to a major metro area.

Four of the cities count as rural under the federal definition the team used, with fewer than 50,000 residents. The other four are urban.

Each sample was processed the same day. The team sequenced a marker gene to identify the bacteria present. Then they kept only those known to live in the human gut, about 37% of what they read.

For the health side, they pulled public estimates for the census tracts inside each plant’s service area, 329 tracts in all.

The CDC’s PLACES database supplied rates of 23 health conditions and behaviors.

The agency’s Social Vulnerability Index supplied the social and economic measures, such as poverty and unemployment.

Each city’s sewage had its own bacterial profile, and the profiles differed enough that the cities could be told apart.

Several of those differences lined up with health. Cities where smoking was more common had more of one large group of bacteria, the Firmicutes.

Cities with less physical activity had more of two genera, Desulfovibrio and Tolumonas.

Desulfovibrio, a genus of bacteria that produce hydrogen sulfide, appeared again and again. Its abundance was associated with eight health measures.

They were high blood pressure, obesity, stroke, mobility disability, chronic kidney disease, coronary heart disease, chronic lung disease, and cancers other than skin cancer.

Studies of patients have found more Desulfovibrio in people with colorectal, stomach, and breast cancers than in healthy people, the authors wrote.

Researchers suspect its hydrogen sulfide may damage cells.

Asthma ran the other way. Cities with more asthma had a less even mix of gut bacteria and less of a genus called Akkermansia, which is widely treated as a marker of a healthy gut.

Social conditions showed up too. Cities with more unemployment had less diverse gut bacteria overall and more Firmicutes.

The four rural cities carried more Desulfovibrio, more Akkermansia, and more of a major group called Bacteroidota than the urban ones.

They also generally had higher rates of the chronic conditions in the dataset.

“In many cases, poor health outcomes are worse in rural areas and to see these distinct indicators of poorer gut microbiome health in these rural areas offers us clues to some of what is driving that,” Lancaster said.

Marysville, an urban city, sat at the healthy end of nearly every measure, with the lowest rates of smoking, depression, unemployment, and poverty among the eight.

“Wastewater offers a unique way to understand the health of whole communities, not just the people who show up in clinics,” Lee said.

Eight cities give eight data points. The authors wrote that their results should be read as exploratory, not as reliable estimates for any one city.

Columbus alone accounts for most of the 1.3 million people, which could tilt comparisons.

The health figures are also modeled estimates for census tracts, not counts of diagnosed patients. And the samples cover seven weeks of one year.

A deeper problem is in the pipes. Desulfovibrio and Acetobacterium live in sewers as well as in guts, and their numbers in wastewater can rise or fall with how long sewage stays in a pipe.

The authors wrote that future studies should record sewer conditions to separate the human signal from the plumbing.

A larger study could test whether the same patterns hold across more cities and over longer periods while accounting for diet, access to health care, and sewer conditions.

Wastewater already tracks viruses such as those behind COVID, flu, and mpox, Lee said.

Tracking a community’s gut bacteria the same way, she said, could eventually show whether a nutrition program is improving its gut health.

The full study was published in the journal Environmental Research.

Sierra Club Submits Comments Urging SEC to Preserve Shareholder Rights & Climate Accountability

WASHINGTON, D.C. — On Thursday, Sierra Club submitted two comment letters opposing the Securities and Exchange Commission’s (SEC) proposal to rescind Rule 14a-8, which allows shareholders to put proposals addressing climate risks and other corporate issues before fellow investors for a vote, and amend Rule 14a-4(c), which governs how companies may exercise discretionary voting authority over shareholder proposals. The Sierra Club submitted both a technical comment and a grassroots comment, reflecting concerns from over 1,100 of its members and supporters.

Together the submissions urge the SEC to withdraw its proposed changes and preserve investors’ ability to raise concerns directly with corporate management and press companies to address material risks, including climate-related risks, that can affect their long-term financial interests.

Jessye Waxman, Campaign Advisor on Sierra Club’s Sustainable Finance Campaign, released the following statement:

“Climate change and other systemic risks have real consequences for companies, investors, and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors an important way to bring those risks to management’s attention, push for meaningful action, and hold companies accountable when they fail to act. The SEC’s proposals would weaken those tools, making it harder for investors to protect their long-term financial interests and leaving corporate management with less accountability for the risks its decisions create. The SEC’s proposals would shield corporate management from meaningful shareholder scrutiny, while undermining the very investor protections the Commission is charged with upholding.”

Rule 14a-8 establishes when public companies must include qualifying shareholder proposals in the materials they send investors ahead of shareholder meetings. The SEC’s proposed repeal would leave decisions about shareholder proposals to state law and individual companies’ governing documents, eliminating a federal framework that investors have used for more than 80 years.

Sierra Club’s technical comments explain that material risks, like climate change, can both have significant financial consequences for companies and create systemic financial risks that extend across investment portfolios and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors a way to bring these concerns to company leadership and fellow shareholders, including by surfacing risks that management may overlook or have limited incentives to address. The letter emphasizes that, for decades, this process has served the broader market by helping investors collectively identify and address financially material risks.

The letter warns that replacing a consistent federal framework with varying state laws and corporate bylaws could make shareholder engagement less predictable and push disputes over proposals into costly litigation. It points to the increase in shareholder lawsuits challenging proposal exclusions during the 2026 proxy season as an indication that disputes do not disappear when the SEC steps back from administering the process; instead, they can shift to more costly and uncertain avenues.

The submission also opposes proposed amendments to Rule 14a-4(c), which would expand company management’s discretionary authority to vote shareholders’ proxies on proposals excluded from company proxy materials, unless shareholders affirmatively opt out. Sierra Club’s letter warns that these changes could allow management to vote on proposals that investors have not had an adequate opportunity to evaluate.
