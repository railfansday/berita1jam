Anthropic’s Backers Have Another Big Bet: Americans Staying Renters

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic’s stock market debut on Thursday is expected to value the artificial intelligence company at $1.5 trillion, putting some of Wall Street’s biggest investment firms in line to cash in from its rapid rise.

In its pitch to investors, the maker of Claude offers a vision far grander than the 2,800 millionaires the IPO is expected to mint. AI, it says, could “significantly accelerate global growth and dramatically improve quality of life for people around the world.”

But some of its backers are making another bet on a future where renting dominates the housing market.

Among them are Blackstone and Brookfield, two investment giants whose portfolios extend from emerging technologies to the nation's housing stock.

“Amidst continued macroeconomic uncertainty, we believe rental housing is the number one thing you want to own,” reads a 2025 shareholder update from Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust, known as BREIT.

Under a heading touting Blackstone Real Estate’s “Best Ideas,” the fund pointed to record homeownership costs, sluggish construction, and a national shortage of 4 million to 5 million homes. It called those conditions “powerful tailwinds” for its rental housing investments.

To housing advocates, though, those tailwinds are precisely the headwinds they’re working to reverse, says Jordan Ash, housing director at the nonprofit watchdog Private Equity Stakeholder Project.

The investment thesis anticipates a future in which “there are many more people who are just going to be renters for life,” Ash tells Realtor.com®.

“The renter is not their customer,” he says of large private equity landlords. “They're accountable to their investors.”

And now, the rental market is showing signs of turning in those investors’ favor.

Median asking rents rose 0.1% in October—the first annual increase since June 2023—across the 50 largest metros, according to Realtor.com research. The increase was small, but it came as the apartment construction boom that helped hold rents down began to fade.

Meanwhile, households that might otherwise leave the rental market face a steep price for becoming homeowners.

Mortgage rates averaged 7.33% in October, roughly double their 3.69% level in October 2019, notes Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu.

“Elevated mortgage rates are one of the key barriers to homeownership, because they significantly raise the monthly cost of buying,” she explains. Higher ownership expenses have added to the burden.

That financial hurdle is apparent in Dallas–Fort Worth, TX, where Blackstone owns rental homes through Tricon, the operator it acquired in 2024.

Across the metro, the median monthly cost of buying a starter home was $2,741 in October, compared with $1,471 to rent, according to data from Realtor.com. That means making the leap to homeownership would involve finding an additional $1,270 in the monthly budget—after also coming up with a down payment.

It's easy to understand the bet then—with a gap that wide, who would ever be able to afford the jump?

Washington has tried to make that leap easier by limiting buyers’ competition from Wall Street. Following President Donald Trump’s January executive order, Congress enacted restrictions on additional single-family home purchases by institutional investors controlling at least 350 such homes. The ban takes effect on Jan. 7, 2027, with exceptions that include qualifying build-to-rent projects.

But limiting competition from investors does not, by itself, close the monthly gap between renting and owning. And while that gap gives households a reason to keep renting, BREIT expects declining construction to give landlords more room to raise rents.

“Ultimately, lower supply should support rent growth and real estate values over time,” the fund wrote in its Nov. 4 shareholder update, which did not address the institutional investor ban.

Of course, that outcome isn’t assured. Although the multifamily construction pipeline has shrunk from its peak, it remains large by pre-pandemic standards, Xu says. Projects already underway could continue giving tenants more options.

Even so, “If new multifamily deliveries cannot keep pace with rental demand, we expect rents to rise,” she says.

But even buying a home doesn't always end the relationship with a landlord.

Ash points to Brookfield’s acquisition of YES Communities, an operator of manufactured-home communities where residents can own their houses while paying rent for the land beneath them.

Residents’ complaints about rising costs and neglected maintenance predated Brookfield’s purchase. And as the deal approached, some appealed directly to pension funds invested with the firm, seeking assurances that a new owner would improve their circumstances.

“The rent just keeps going up, but no improvements have been made,” Michigan resident Abbie Pedrotte told University of California regents, according to Ash’s reporting.

By October, those concerns had reached Congress.

In an Oct. 13 letter, Rep. Rashida Tlaib (D-MI) and two colleagues pressed Brookfield about its plans. They asked whether it intended to raise home and lot rents by more than 3% annually and whether it would cooperate with residents seeking to purchase their parks. Written answers were requested by Nov. 20.

For its part, Brookfield’s published strategy calls for increasing resident homeownership and expanding tenant programs to “boost operating income and retention.” Owning the house can give a resident a stake in the community while preserving the landlord’s income from the land.

“We own the appreciating part of real estate. It’s fantastic,” Lowell Baron, CEO of Brookfield’s real estate business, told investors in September.

Some of the firms betting on continued demand for rentals are also financing a construction boom of their own.

BREIT had deployed more than $9 billion into data center developments in 2026 by its November shareholder update, helping expand the infrastructure supporting AI and cloud computing.

Blackstone has already identified a potential cost for other real estate—including homebuilding.

On its October earnings call, President Jon Gray said rising construction costs and limited new supply were being intensified by higher interest rates and “crowding out from the AI infrastructure build.”

A construction industry survey offers evidence of that competition. Among firms reporting on data center projects’ workforce effects, 58% cited increased competition for skilled workers, while 49% reported greater wage pressure, according to the 2026 AGC/NCCER Workforce Survey.

Xu cautions against treating those findings as a measure of the impact on homebuilding.

“The survey points to labor pressure where data centers are concentrated, but it does not isolate AI investment from factors like immigration enforcement and tariffs, nor does it directly measure residential construction costs,” she explains.

And to their credit, the private equity firms are also moving to help finance additional housing. Blackstone launched a homebuilder lending platform in May that it expects to support more than 50,000 new for-sale homes annually.

Buying existing homes changes ownership without adding to the housing stock, Xu explains.

“Financing new construction, by contrast, increases the stock, which is the key to expanding supply and improving affordability for both groups,” she says.

Anthropic’s backers have capital to help build the future they describe. Households waiting for a more affordable home need some of that future to include them.

2 Monster Stocks to Hold for the Next 10 Years

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Both of these stocks have turned $10,000 into roughly $70,000 or more over the past decade, and their businesses are still built to keep compounding.

By John Ballard – Nov 19, 2026 at 4:45AM EST

The best stocks don't just beat the market for a year or two. They compound wealth for a decade or more, and that kind of return almost always traces back to a business that keeps growing its customer base, revenue, and profits.

MercadoLibre (MELI -0.95%) and Amazon (AMZN +3.82%) have both delivered monster returns, and their businesses are still growing, making them quality stocks to hold for 10 years.

A $10,000 investment in MercadoLibre 10 years ago would be worth about $94,000 today, a roughly 25% annualized return. However, the stock trades more than 40% below its June 2025 high because profits have stalled.

That is a deliberate choice, as management is prioritizing investments to strengthen the business for long-term growth. Management has lowered Brazil's free-shipping threshold several times since 2017, while ramping up credit card issuance and expanding fulfillment space nearly 20% last quarter.

It's short-term pain for long-term gain. Operating margin fell to 6.3% from 9.8% a year ago, even as revenue climbed 45% year over year. On the Nov. 4, 2026, earnings call, CEO Ariel Szarfsztejn said the company has "accepted a lower margin" to deepen customer relationships.

Those relationships are showing up in other metrics. Gross merchandise volume (GMV) rose 38% to $23 billion, unique active buyers grew 22% to 93.9 million, and purchase frequency hit a record.

The risk is that competition from Shopee and others keeps profits down longer than investors expect. Still, the economics are improving. Brazil's cost per shipment is down 14% since Q1 2025, and high-margin advertising grew 58%.

Analysts expect earnings per share (EPS) to jump 45% to $52.62 in 2027, according to Yahoo! Finance. At $1,491 as of Nov. 13, 2026, the stock trades at 28 times that estimate. That's a fair valuation for a quality e-commerce stock with excellent long-term growth prospects.

A $10,000 investment in Amazon 10 years ago would be worth about $70,000 today, a roughly 22% annualized return. The engine behind those gains is growth in operating income, which reached $104 billion over the past year, up 37% year over year, excluding foreign currency effects.

Amazon Web Services (AWS) is a fast-growing, high-margin business, with revenue up 39% to $46 billion last quarter, and a backlog of $578 billion. CEO Andy Jassy expects AWS to grow more than 40% in both 2027 and 2028. Amazon also plans to sell its Trainium AI chips outside AWS starting late 2027.

Amazon's core retail business is still growing, too. North America and international margins have each expanded nearly 10 percentage points over the past few years, and Amazon is still finding ways to lower costs that could make its online store even more profitable.

The risk is the spending required to support growth, particularly the addition of more data center capacity for AWS. Capital expenditures will reach about $220 billion in 2026 and rise "meaningfully" in 2027. But trailing-12-month operating cash flow still grew 29% to $168 billion, and Jassy says Amazon has a "clear line of sight to attractive financial returns."

Analysts expect EPS to increase from $7.17 in 2025 to $10.69 in 2027. At $266.66 as of Nov. 13, 2026, Amazon trades at about 25 times 2027 estimates. The stock should continue to compound in value as the business grows over the next 10 years.

Bitcoin’s Current Drawdown Ahead of Anthropic IPO Mirrors SpaceX Listing — Why a $90K Rebound Could Be Coming by February

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Bitcoin’s latest price decline may be setting the stage for a familiar market pattern, and history could give bulls reason to be optimistic. The benchmark cryptocurrency has suffered a sharp drop ahead of Anthropic’s upcoming red-hot stock market debut, echoing its 19% plunge in the 50 days leading up to SpaceX’s IPO earlier this year. However, if the historical pattern repeats, Bitcoin could be gearing up for a significant recovery, with a potential rebound toward $90,000 by February now drawing attention.

Strive CEO Matt Cole has observed an interesting historical parallel between Bitcoin’s current price action and its performance ahead of SpaceX’s record-breaking IPO. In the 50 days leading up to SpaceX’s $75 billion public debut on June 12, Bitcoin plunged 19%.

Now, with Anthropic preparing for a potential $64.4 billion IPO next week, BTC has posted a remarkably similar 18% decline over the same period, Cole noted in a post on X.

At press time, Bitcoin was trading at $64,665, according to CoinGecko, extending its recent losses after breaking below the $70,000 mark on November 13. The cryptocurrency continued its downward slide over the weekend, falling beneath $67,000 as bearish pressure intensified.

The Strive chief noted that overlaying the two price movements reveals an almost identical pattern, raising the possibility that Bitcoin could be repeating its pre-SpaceX sell-off ahead of another historic market debut.

Bitcoin extended its decline after SpaceX’s market debut, reaching its lowest point 18 days later before reversing course. The subsequent rally lifted BTC 28% above its IPO-day closing price within 100 days.

“If Anthropic follows the same pattern, most of the drawdown is behind us, and it could reverse quickly over the next couple of months,” Cole opined.

If history repeats itself, Bitcoin could climb toward $90,000 by late February, Cole suggested.

Binance co-founder Changpeng “CZ” Zhao also weighed in on the chart comparison, sharing the analysis on X and describing Cole’s findings as “interesting.”

However, the historical comparison comes with important caveats. Bitcoin’s sell-off ahead of the SpaceX IPO unfolded alongside market concerns over potential BTC sales by Michael Saylor’s Strategy, suggesting that factors beyond the anticipated listing may have contributed to the decline. As a result, attributing the entire downturn to IPO-related sentiment would overlook other pressures weighing on the market at the time.

Pfizer Will Pay Its 352nd Straight Quarterly Dividend on Dec. 7. Here's What It Would Take to Break the Streak.

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Pfizer's dividend streak spans 88 years, and it would take a perfect storm to end it.

By Keith Speights – Nov 19, 2026 at 4:44AM EST

Pfizer (PFE -1.44%) shareholders should mark Dec. 7, 2026, on their calendars. The giant drugmaker is scheduled to pay its 352nd consecutive quarterly dividend on that date.

That payout is a sure thing for investors who owned shares as of the close of business on Nov. 6, 2026. But what would it take to end Pfizer's impressive dividend streak?

No company can continue paying dividends indefinitely without generating enough cash to fund operations while funding the dividend program. Pfizer's free cash flow currently covers its dividend payouts. However, the big pharma company faces a patent cliff that could upset the status quo.

But Pfizer has a cushion that could allow it to pay quarterly dividends even if its free cash flow declines sharply. The drugmaker sits atop a cash stockpile of $14.3 billion (including cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments). That's enough to fund dividends for well over a year. If Pfizer uses a big chunk of its cash to finance an acquisition, though, this cushion could disappear.

Importantly, Pfizer is counting on its newer drugs and promising late-stage pipeline candidates to help it successfully navigate the patent cliff. Multiple major clinical setbacks could cause management to reevaluate the company's growth prospects and potentially realize that continuing to pay dividends isn't viable.

It isn't too far-fetched to envision one of these scenarios causing Pfizer to cut its dividend. However, A reduced dividend would still keep the streak alive. I think that all three scenarios would have to unfold for Pfizer to eliminate its dividend.

Could this triple-whammy for Pfizer happen? It's possible, but not probable. In my view, Pfizer is much more likely to navigate the looming patent cliff relatively well, while continuing to generate sufficient free cash flow to fund the dividend at current levels. This big pharma stock should remain an attractive alternative for investors seeking higher income.
