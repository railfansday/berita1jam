While most AI-powered peripherals record only phone calls or in-person chats, the Ear Note is designed to capture every conversation.

Phone-maker Nothing Technology is breaking away from its typical retro-inspired designs with a new pair of AI-powered earbuds, as reported earlier by Bloomberg. The company confirmed via an X post that its Ear Note wireless earbuds will be available starting Dec. 8.

The Ear Note earbuds are designed primarily to maximize the recording functionality. While other AI earbuds have already hit the market, the Ear Note is the first to record both phone calls and in-person conversations.

The earbuds can capture and transcribe phone calls, while the charging case has its own microphone to record in-person conversations. To mitigate privacy concerns, the case only records with a physical button press — a tell that could reveal when audio is being captured around the Ear Note user.

Nothing shared that the Ear Note will work with multiple popular AI agents, but the full list of compatible software has not yet been released. The Ear Note works in tandem with an app that stores recordings from the earbuds and case, creating transcriptions and summaries similar to those created by the popular Otter AI app.

The Nothing brand has developed a cult following for combining lifestyle and fashion trends in its suite of smartphones, headphones, speakers and watches.

The full product lineup is built with modern hardware, but mimics warm, nearly retrofuturist design sensibilities. The blocky Ear Note charging case continues this trend while taking the company’s tech in a new direction.

In the lead-up to the Ear Note’s reveal, Nothing CEO Carl Pei described the earbuds in an X post as “a tool, but one built with the personality and care that have come to define a Nothing product.”

In a Bloomberg interview, Pei indicated that Nothing isn’t trying to create AI agents or software, but that the company has a “really interesting opportunity to be the hardware layer to AI.”

It’s currently unclear how much the Ear Note earbuds and case will cost when they’re released next month. According to Bloomberg’s Mark Gurman, the company hasn’t settled on a price just yet. The most expensive earbuds currently available on the Nothing store cost $149.

Whether you’re covering your eyes, plugging your ears or slapping a timepiece on your wrist, there’s now an ever-present option to record the world around you. Multiple companies have created their own versions of smart glasses, earbuds and watches with augmented reality capabilities.

Now that AI is the flavor du jour, these existing features are being enhanced by new ways to scan and interact with the surrounding environment. That means a lot of visual and auditory data is being recorded by wearable devices, and very rarely with the consent of all parties involved.

The most immediate risk factors are associated with video recording – public distrust of smart glasses is so widespread that they’ve colloquially been coined “pervert glasses,” as folks fear they fundamentally break an unspoken social contract by obscuring public recording from other people.

But audio recording, enabled by Apple’s latest smartwatches, the Amazon Bee, Plaud earbuds and the new Ear Note, is also raising concerns, not least because many states require all-party consent before you can record conversations.

The Ear Note’s ability to record every bit of audio, from phone calls to in-person conversations, means it will be handling more sensitive information than other AI-powered earbuds. The company will need to face new challenges to ensure security measures are in place to prevent private chats from leaking.

At the very least, it seems as though Nothing Technology is aware of these privacy risks. According to Bloomberg, the company created a data and security team to address concerns that cropped up during the Ear Note’s development. The company has also “obtained the same privacy certifications as competing recording products and worked with law firms to assess potential risks and safeguards.”

Pei called wearables with recording functionality a sensitive space, and said the design team was “extra careful to not take any risks.” The Ear Note earbuds and case are largely controlled by user commands, including tactile button presses that begin the recording process.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/hhs-medicaid-state-directed-payment-savings-primarily-stem-slowing-growth" hreflang="en">HHS: Medicaid state-directed payment savings 'primarily' stem from slowing growth</a>

📅 2026-11-23  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
HHS: Medicaid state-directed payment savings 'primarily' stem from slowing growth" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Rural hospitals claim a relatively small portion of the state-directed payments being squeezed in coming years by 2025’s H.R. 1, with much of the legislation’s projected spending reductions the result of holding funds for those hospitals from growing beyond fiscal year 2025 levels, Department of Health and Human Services policy heads wrote in a new report.

The analysis, released Monday, paints a softer picture of the rate limits Republicans imposed on a supplemental Medicaid payment stream in last year’s broad budget reconciliation law, also referred to as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act or the Working Families Tax Cut Legislation.

Hospitals and other healthcare industry groups, as well as advocates and Democrats, have said the law’s billions in projected savings—from state-directed payment changes as well as other policy areas like Medicaid work requirements—are likely to devastate providers already operating at thin or negative margins.

Specifically, the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE)’s analysis showed that an estimated 7.8% of Medicaid state-directed payments reached rural hospitals in FY2025, or $11.5 billion out of $148.3 billion. That rate is slightly above rural hospitals’ 7% share of total Medicaid hospital spending for that same fiscal year, as estimated by the CMS Office of the Actuary.

Scaling from that baseline in line with H.R. 1’s multi-year payment rate phasedown, with some cumulative adjustments, showed that total nominal cumulative spending on rural state-directed payments would decline by 42.9%, or $81.6 billion, from 2026 through 2035 under the bill. This rate is again slightly higher than the CMS Office of the Actuary’s 40.8% estimate for all affected hospitals, which ASPE said is the result of the higher average dollar weighted payment rates rural hospitals currently receive, “which means that rural providers have more room to reduce rates to meet the new limits of 100% to 110% of Medicare.”

The ASPE report’s bottom line, however, is that $75.2 billion of the $81.6 billion reduction rural hospitals face, or 92.1%, reflects projected increases over the FY2025 baseline of $11.5 billion. The remainder, about 7.9% of the projected change, is a reduction below FY2025’s estimated state-directed payments to rural hospitals.

HHS, in a release highlighting the report’s findings, said that the language used in government reports describing the full $81.6 billion 10-year spending reduction as “savings” misses the government’s primary goal of capping future growth.

“Counting every dollar of projected savings as a cut below existing funding gives the public the wrong picture. Most of these savings are not reductions below what the program spent in 2025,” HHS Chief Economist and Chief Regulatory Officer Casey Mulligan, who was one of the report’s coauthors, said in the release. “They reflect future spending increases that will no longer occur. Our analysis makes that distinction clear.”

H.R. 1’s multi-year scale down in state-directed payment rates is set to begin in 2028, and are expected to slash some hard-hit states’ Medicaid spending by up to a quarter of their total annual Medicaid spending, per one analysis. Hospitals have broadly criticized the policy changes while spending much of this year adjusting their operations to contend with the future limits supplemental revenue, expectations of increased uncompensated care costs and year-to-year expense pressures like wage and supply inflation.

Additionally, CMS earlier this year proposed state directed payment caps substantially more extensive than what was outlined in H.R. 1 and used as the basis for Monday’s ASPE report. That proposal would extend rate limits beyond the bill’s four outlined service areas (inpatient hospital services, outpatient hospital services, nursing facility services and qualified practitioner services at an academic medical center) to “all [state-directed payments]s for all services in all states.” Hospital groups have flatly opposed the proposal, which more than triples the 10-year estimated savings.

Lawmakers last year, when seeking votes, blunted H.R. 1's funding reductions for rural hospitals by inserting a five-year, $50 billion rural health fund into the legislation. Those funds have come with usage restrictions for states and providers, with critics also noting transparency concerns and the fund's relatively small scale compared to the bill's total projected impact on hospitals.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/unpacking-implications-cms-latest-proposed-medicare-advantage-changes" hreflang="en">Unpacking the implications from CMS' latest proposed Medicare Advantage changes</a>

📅 2026-11-23  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Unpacking the implications from CMS' latest proposed Medicare Advantage changes" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Late last week, the Trump administration released a proposed rule that includes a slew of technical changes in Medicare Advantage for the 2028 plan, citing enhanced competition as a key goal.

Analysts dove into the beefy regulation, and project that changes to MA's star ratings program would likely cull more than $100 billion in federal spending over the next decade.

Per a note from Leerink Partners, estimates from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services suggest that the changes would cut federal spending by $11.5 billion in 2031 and $107 billion through 2037.

The Leerink analysts estimate that about 58% of Medicare Advantage contracts would see lower scores using the newly proposed methodology, with about 20% of contracts losing quality bonus payments, which are tied to star ratings performance.

In the rule, CMS proposed moving to a set of 15 measures to determine the star ratings scores, which are focused on member experience and outcomes. The proposed rule would also eliminate the Reward Factor, and in tandem, these changes "would remove a cushion for contracts sitting at the critical QBP threshold, while concentrating performance across fewer measures," according to the Leerink report.

The analysts said that the potential impact of these proposals "reminds us just how many plans have been living at 4 stars for years with the hugely important Reward Factor."

The report estimates that the changes to the star ratings, if finalized, would have an uneven impact on insurers. For example, Humana would see the number of contracts earning four or more stars decline from 95% to 82.9%. Clover Health, meanwhile, would likely maintain 100% of its contracts earning at least four stars.

UnitedHealthcare, by comparison, could see a drop from having 67.9% of its contracts in plans with at least four stars in 2027 to just 8.8% under the new methodology. For Aetna, the Leerink analysts project a decrease from 70% to 0%.

Given the scale of the changes, the Leerink analysts write that the rule sets the stage for a significant overhaul of the policies and rules that govern Medicare Advantage. The proposal is "almost overwhelming in size, scope and the impact on the industry."

Molly Turco, a policy analyst at TD Cowen, wrote in a note Monday that the proposed rule "is more significant than expected, in both revenue impact and scope and touches most core aspects of MA."

"It appears CMS aimed to reform long-standing concerns, especially Stars, in a way that takes money out of the program, while also loosening regulation on things like agents/brokers, seamless enrollment, network adequacy, etc. to help the program grow," Turco wrote. "However, we think these growth/deregulation policies are unlikely to offset the headwinds for MA."

She noted that while most of the most painful changes wouldn't impact plans until 2031 at the earliest, the delay does suggest that CMS may be comfortable with including more significant short-term changes to risk adjustment in the forthcoming 2028 Advance Notice, which is expected in January.

Ryan Langston, an equity research analyst at TD Cowen, wrote in the note that the proposed rule would likely be net negative for insurers with large, multi-state contracts and more people enrolled with plans earning at least four stars.

The delayed timeline of at least four years does grant plans time to adapt, Langston wrote, but the financial impacts would likely force benefit changes. The proposed rule could also spook investors concerned about what's included in the 2028 rate notice, he said.

"The combination of a smaller measure set, elimination of reward factors and reduced improvement measure protection likely lower Star ratings across the industry and force plans to mitigate lost revenue via benefit revisions," Langston wrote.

The Leerink analysts wrote that health plans are almost certain to fight the regulation. Lawsuits have become commonplace in the stars space, with multiple payers suing the feds over the most recent round of scores.

"We believe a lot of what has been proposed will change and/or not be implemented at all," they wrote. "Expect industry litigation."

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/florida-sues-leapfrog-group-misrepresenting-its-hospital-safety-grades-consumers" hreflang="en">Florida sues The Leapfrog Group for 'misrepresenting' its hospital safety grades to consumers</a>

📅 2026-11-23  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Florida sues The Leapfrog Group for 'misrepresenting' its hospital safety grades to consumers" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Florida’s attorney general has filed a lawsuit against hospital safety watchdog The Leapfrog Group alleging that it misled consumers about how it determines the grades assigned to hospitals, particularly those that declined to voluntarily submit data to the group.

The complaint, which was filed late last week and lists President and CEO Leah Binder alongside her organization, follows a similar suit brought by Tenet hospitals in the state that convinced a judge that nonparticipating hospitals were being “unfairly” penalized in Leapfrog’s methodology.

The group was ordered to withhold safety grades for those hospitals and in grade releases since has voluntarily chosen not to award ratings to hundreds of others that did not submit recent quality data to its surveys. Leapfrog appealed that decision in September, and has said it is reviewing a potential methodology change for upcoming gradings to incorporate non-submitting hospitals.

The Florida Office of Attorney General James Uthmeier’s lawsuit cites that case. It argues that Leapfrog’s poor scores for “at least seven Florida hospitals” misled patients, and outlined specific cases in which patients had declined care over the perceived poor performances of those hospitals.

“The Leapfrog Group told Florida patients its hospital grades were peer-reviewed and backed by experts. That was a lie,” Uthmeier said in a Friday release announcing the litigation. “When hospitals declined to fill out its survey, Leapfrog handed them the worst scores in the country without a shred of data, and patients refused surgery because of it. Leapfrog and its CEO should expect to be held accountable.”

The office requested that the court permanently bar Leapfrog from “misrepresenting to Florida consumers … the data, sources, methods or expert approvals underlying any hospital grade or score they publish,” or from representing the scores as peer reviewed. It also seeks to limit Leapfrog’s solicitation of charitable contributions, make corrective disclosures, and pay out other relief to providers, patients or other affected individuals and groups.

Leapfrog, in a statement to Fierce Healthcare, said the office’s case “poses the specter of outright government censorship,” and was “piggybacking” on the Tenet hospitals’ case “with old allegations Leapfrog strongly refuted.”

“Leapfrog looks forward to answering in court and defending our First Amendment rights and most importantly, patient safety,” the group’s statement reads. “Too many patients are harmed or killed by medical errors and infections in hospitals, and Leapfrog will always stand up for their lives as we have for 26 years.”

The methodology change at the heart of the cases came ahead of Leapfrog’s fall 2024 safety grade publication. It more heavily weighed “imputed” scores for four of Leapfrog’s 32 safety measures that were assigned when recent safety data for the two preceding years were unavailable. The Tenet hospitals, in their case, highlighted internal communications and other testimony that questioned the validity of the changes. Both lawsuits suggest the changes were made to maximize hospital participation in Leapfrog’s survey, which would bring downstream increases to revenues it collects from allowing hospitals to advertise their grades.

Uthmeier’s office has also filed a friend of the court brief to the appellate court now considering Leapfrog’s appeal, where it similarly argues that Leapfrog’s conduct is a violation of Florida’s consumer protection laws and not protected by the First Amendment.

The new complaint comes the same week as Leapfrog’s latest hospital safety grade release, which, similar to the spring release, withheld grades for 447 hospitals.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/provider-groups-file-lawsuit-against-hhs-over-new-ryan-white-funding-rules" hreflang="en">Court strikes down anti-trans Ryan White funding rules 'based in animus, loathing and discrimination'</a>

📅 2026-11-23  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Court strikes down anti-trans Ryan White funding rules 'based in animus, loathing and discrimination'" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Medical associations and providers notched a legal victory over the federal government in a court ruling vacating federal funding requirements for Ryan White clinics involving gender-affirming care.

Those restrictions would have required recipients of Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program funds to not acknowledge the identities of transgender patients or use funds for gender-affirming care.

It was challenged by the HIV Medicine Association of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (HIVMA), the American Academy of HIV Medicine and the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care in June (see that coverage below), who argued that the policy was contrary to Congress' intent when establishing the program under law in 1991, lacked sufficient explanation and discriminated against transgender people in violation of the First and Fifth Amendments.

The Nov. 19 decision from Judge William G. Young, of the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts, prohibits the government from opposing its conditions in regard to all federal funding and permanently blocks any similar policies from being implemented by associational plaintifs' members, "which covers virtually every HIV care provider in the United States," said Lambda Legal, which acted as co-counsel for the plaintiffs.

"The ruling is a decisive and important victory for transgender people with HIV and protects the ability of physicians and other health care professionals to offer evidence-based care without political interference," Philip Bolduc, M.D., chair of HIVMA, said in a statement reacting to the decision.

Young's opinion and order opened with an acknowledgement that there are "sincere and deeply felt views" regarding transgender identity, and that the issue at question was of legal regulation rather than "the propriety of individual gender expression."

Still, the judge concluded his opinion by writing that the administration's conditions "are based in animus, loathing and discrimination. They do not stand for justice—in fact, they defy it. They are contrary to the laws of this nation and cannot continue to stand."

A group of medical associations and providers has filed a lawsuit challenging new federal funding requirements for Ryan White clinics involving gender-affirming care.

The new requirements, issued in a recent HRSA Notice of Funding Opportunity, require recipients of the Ryan White HIV/AIDS Program funds to not acknowledge the identities of transgender patients or use funds for gender-affirming care. The restrictions undermine the very intent of the program, the plaintiffs say, and violate anti-discrimination components of the Fifth Amendment.

“The Ryan White Program was created as a public health intervention to address the needs of low-income, vulnerable populations who otherwise would not have access to life-saving care,” Melanie Thompson, M.D., co-chair of a primary care guidance panel for the HIV Medicine Association, said in a press release. “It is crucial that Ryan White clinicians be allowed to continue to deliver the most scientifically appropriate treatments for all patients, including transgender individuals, without discrimination if we are to improve health outcomes for all and end the U.S. HIV epidemic."

The lawsuit alleges (PDF) that HHS and HRSA violated the Administrative Procedure Act by exceeding their statutory authority and imposing restrictions that conflict with the Ryan White statute. The plaintiffs ask the court to block enforcement of the funding restrictions.

The case was filed by Lambda Legal and HSF Kramer on behalf of the American Academy of HIV Medicine, the HIV Medicine Association and the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care, plus two providers.

A preliminary hearing will likely be scheduled in a few weeks. Lambda Legal is “cautiously optimistic” about the future of the case, José Abrigo, senior attorney and HIV project director at Lambda Legal, told reporters on a webinar on Thursday afternoon. He called the administration’s actions “unlawful,” and said gender-affirming care in this context includes all types of care, including primary care and mental healthcare.

Specifically, the HRSA Notice of Funding Opportunity reads: “Discretionary awards shall not be used to fund, promote, encourage, subsidize, or facilitate … denial by the recipient of the sex binary in humans, or the belief that sex is a chosen or mutable characteristic.”

Some providers are already preemptively complying with the restrictions, Abrigo said, particularly in Tennessee and Kentucky. It is unclear how many other providers will also stop gender-affirming care. In certain cases, it’s up to individual states or departments of health to enforce the restrictions. It’s possible that medical records could be audited and reviewed for indications of pronouns, per Thompson. “It does matter what someone’s gender and pronouns are listed as,” Thompson said on the webinar, explaining that medical records are shared between providers and help ensure affirming care.

If a clinic is found to be non-compliant, the repercussions are steep, Thompson added. “The penalty for being found out is severe, and puts an entire program’s funding in jeopardy,” she said on the webinar.

Forty percent of American Academy of HIV Medicine members rely on Ryan White funding, of which 40% voluntarily report providing gender-affirming medical care. For low-income trans individuals with HIV, there aren’t many alternative options, per Abrigo. They may seek care through private insurance or potentially through Medicaid, but typically the population relies on Ryan White clinics for services.

Gender-affirming care restrictions under the Trump administration have been commonplace. Over 40 hospitals have reportedly stopped some gender-affirming care services for minors in response to pressure. Last week, the Cleveland Clinic promised the Department of Justice (DOJ) to commit $2 million to detransitioning services and a “decades-long” agreement not to perform gender-affirming care on minors. Delivering most of these services is currently illegal for minors under Ohio state law. Last month, DOJ struck a similar deal with Texas Children’s.

For months, experts have been sounding the alarm about the U.S. HIV response slipping into dangerous instability. In April, the International Association of Providers of AIDS Care (IAPAC) released a data brief finding that 61% of providers have reported at least one HIV service disruption and 68% have seen operational changes such as staffing reductions, service cutbacks and program closures. This was attributed to a combination of policy decisions, funding cuts and social determinants like loss of insurance coverage.

“It’s a catastrophe in the making," José M. Zúñiga, AIPAC's president and CEO, told reporters on the webinar. "It was already happening. This adds fuel."

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/wtw-global-health-insurance-costs-set-rise-102-next-year" hreflang="en">WTW: Global health insurance costs set to rise 10.2% next year</a>

📅 2026-11-23  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
WTW: Global health insurance costs set to rise 10.2% next year" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Global health insurance costs are set to rise by double digits in 2027 for the second straight year, according to a new survey.

WTW released its latest Global Medical Trends Survey, which estimates that the average gross cost for medical benefits will increase by 10.2% in 2027. This follows a 10.1% increase in 2026 and a 9.8% increase in 2025, according to the report.

In the United States, the cost for medical benefits is expected to rise by 10.3% on average, on par with 2026 and up from a 9.4% increase in 2025. Across regions, the highest increases are expected in the Asia-Pacific, averaging 12.6% in 2027.

The next-highest region was North America, with a projected 10.5% average increase.

The survey was conducted in June and August 2026 and includes responses from 393 insurers offering coverage across 92 countries. Just under half (49%) of those surveyed said they anticipate elevated medical and pharmacy costs to impact them over the next three years, and likely persist beyond that window.

The WTW analysts said this likely means that insurers will remain focused on cost containment and sharing measures in the near future.

"In the US, healthcare costs remain under sustained pressure from new medical technologies, specialty pharmaceuticals, and the rising use of GLP-1 therapies, alongside higher provider costs and the ongoing demands of chronic and complex conditions," said Courtney Stubblefield, strategy and specialty leader in Health & Benefits at WTW. in an announcement.

More than three quarters (76%) of the surveyed insurers named new medical technologies as the leading cost drive for both medical and pharmaceutical costs. Sixty-five percent cited provider fee increases, and 45% named pharmaceutical advancements.

Cancer was the leading condition driving costs, the survey found, with 62% naming it as the top cost condition. Sixty-one percent of those surveyed said they have seen an increase in cancer diagnoses among individuals under age 40 when compared to the past two years.

However, when looking at the conditions where costs are growing the most quickly, cardiovascular disease rises to No. 1, cited by 54% of the surveyed insurers. Cancer was second on this ranking, and named by 51%, while diabetes was third at 28%.

"To manage this, employers need a multiyear, multifaceted strategy that addresses pharmacy spending and high-cost conditions—such as cancer, cardiovascular disease and diabetes—while keeping care affordable and accessible for employees," Stubblefield said.

By 2040, 51% of People Living with HIV Could Be Over 50

Advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have helped people living with HIV live longer, contributing to a growing population of older adults with the virus.

In fact, a recent report published in The Lancet HIV projects that approximately 51% of people living with HIV worldwide will be over age 50 by 2040. An estimated 96% of these older adults will live in low- and middle-income countries.

The report’s authors emphasized the “pressing need” for HIV prevention and screening services tailored to older adults, who face additional age-related challenges when accessing care.

These challenges include stigma related to both age and HIV, a higher burden of chronic health conditions, taking multiple medications, delayed diagnoses, physical limitations, and increased financial and social vulnerability.

Factors like these “influence HIV risk for older adults in complex and dynamic ways,” according to the report authors.

Many adults over age 50 who are living with HIV acquired the virus during young adulthood in the late 1990s and early 2000s. During that period, some experienced prolonged periods of uncontrolled HIV and exposure to older ART medications with more severe side effects, which may have contributed to long-term inflammation and other health complications.

William Schaffner, MD, professor in the Division of Infectious Diseases at Vanderbilt University, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline, “That older persons with HIV are on track to outnumber younger persons with HIV infection is a double-barreled success story.”

“First, the effective treatment of persons with HIV infection has resulted in the expectation of normal or near-normal lifespans; thus, more older persons living with HIV infection,” he said. “This is a great success; both in creating effective, easy-to-use medicines as well as developing medical care systems that can reach such patients and provide lifetime care.”

Schaffner said the second success has been educating younger people about HIV prevention, helping reduce new infections.

Older adults also account for a growing proportion of new HIV diagnoses. They are more likely to receive a diagnosis later in the course of infection, which can delay treatment and increase the risk of serious health complications and death.

Mathematical models project that the average age of people living with HIV worldwide will increase from 41 in 2025 to 50 by 2040, largely driven by demographic shifts in low- and middle-income countries.

While the World Health Organization (WHO) does not specify an upper age limit for HIV testing in its published overview, U.S. guidelines focus on specific age groups.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends routine HIV testing for people ages 13 to 64, and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends testing for people ages 15 to 65.

However, these age limits don’t mean older adults shouldn’t be tested for HIV, as both U.S. organizations also recommend testing older adults who may be at risk.

Older adults can still be sexually active and have a chance of acquiring HIV. Certain physical changes that come with age, such as thinning of vaginal tissue, may also increase the risk of HIV infection.
