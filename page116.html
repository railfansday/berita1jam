Star Alliance carrier Air New Zealand is excited to expand its South Island long-haul network, with the airline already having introduced service from Christchurch (CHC) to Singapore (SIN) in October. This November, the airline will expand further, with the addition of Tokyo Narita (NRT) and Perth (PER) on a seasonal basis.

Historically, the airline has served these routes before. However, they were ultimately canceled, with services to Japan cut in 2015 from the Garden City, and Perth was shelved in 2019.

Air New Zealand will resume its nonstop service to Japan's largest city, Tokyo, on November 28. The route will be served with the airline's Boeing 787-9 aircraft, which is equipped with three classes of service: business, premium economy, and economy. The airline's Skycouch is also available on these flights, allowing passengers to book a full row to themselves, with the seats converting to a small bed, including a mattress topper, duvet, and pillows.

The flights to Tokyo will operate three times a week, with an afternoon departure from Christchurch, for a late evening arrival into Narita. Return flights will depart from Tokyo in the evening for an overnight flight south back to New Zealand's South Island.

For South Island travelers, the opportunity to now fly on this long-haul international route from Christchurch saves the need to transit via the country's busiest airport, Auckland (AKL), where the majority of all long-haul Air New Zealand services operate. Tokyo Narita will join Singapore (SIN) as the only two long-haul destinations by Air New Zealand from Christchurch, for just a couple of days; read more about that below.

Just two days after Air New Zealand adds Tokyo Narita, the airline will also resume its service to Perth (PER) in Western Australia. The airline already serves Perth year-round from Auckland; however, this will be the first time the carrier has flown from Christchurch since 2019.

The airline will face direct competition on the route, with Jetstar having already launched a seasonal service on the route recently with its Airbus A321LR. Air New Zealand will be the only airline from the South Island providing premium cabins on the route, with the service operated by the airline's Boeing 787-9. These flights will operate seasonally through May.

A nonstop flight from Christchurch to Perth takes just over seven hours gate-to-gate, while return flights surpass six hours. The return of nonstop flights to Western Australia is bound to be popular for travelers on both sides of the Tasman, and saves the unnecessary stops in either Auckland, Brisbane, or Melbourne for connecting flights.

Fleet resurgence is fueling bold nonstop links from Auckland and Christchurch, with Air New Zealand adding Western Sydney and Tokyo services.

On October 28, Air New Zealand resumed nonstop flights from Christchurch to Singapore. The last time the carrier operated on the city pair was in 2019, and through the airline's long-standing joint venture with Singapore Airlines, it provides seamless transfers through SQ's network at Changi (SIN).

Air New Zealand initially planned to operate the route seasonally; however, it has already announced that the route will now be operated year-round, complementing Singapore's year-round daily service to Christchurch, which increases to ten weekly across the Southern Hemisphere summer season.

Nvidia’s tutorial on genuine good governance, needed by tech bros and governistas alike

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jeffrey Sonnenfeld is the Lester Crown Professor in Management Practice and Senior Associate Dean at Yale School of Management. Steven Tian is the director of research at the Yale Chief Executive Leadership Institute. Stephen Henriques is Senior Research Fellow at the Yale Chief Executive Leadership Institute and former McKinsey consultant.

Charles de Gaulle said, “The graveyards are filled with indispensable men.” That rings especially true despite the pathetic cries of the ‘Bring Jensen Back’ bros.

Nvidia’s strong earnings release today proves once and for all that Nvidia does not need Jensen Huang to return to flourish as a strong American national champion. Interim CEO Colette Kress deftly raised forecasts moving forward, pointing to the underlying strength of the business, which is far greater than any one individual, while winning plaudits for running the earnings call with command, presence, and impressive candor. And indeed, new board chair Harvey Jones definitely closed the door on Jensen Huang’s return as CEO amidst news that the SEC is now probing Huang and Nvidia.

All this makes the unresolved governance questions and lessons from the Nvidia debacle even more important.

In the aftermath of Jensen Huang’s ouster from Nvidia this week, a tiny band of tech bros, perhaps intoxicated by their own sudden wealth and prominence, have cast good governance principles to the wind by unrealistically advocating for the return of Jensen Huang, decrying the “woke” politically correct management policies which they believe led to Huang’s exit. At the same time, a chorus of finger-pointing good governance advocates has joined the fray, throwing every pet theory of organizational structure and regulatory restraints on risk-seeking entrepreneurs. Both camps ignore the real collapse of board oversight, which is the tragically poor judgement of the CEO matched with the faulty character and faulty process of board oversight and not its clunky bureaucratic mechanics.

The tech bro cheerleaders rooted for a messianic return of Huang, despite the ongoing investigations into his wide array of apparently corrupt actions and character collapses, such as his blatant violations of Nvidia’s code of conduct and unresolved national security concerns. The arrogant, naïve fantasies in the chants of these tech bros is encapsulated by Telegram founder Pavel Durov’s X post declaring, “Jensen made a rookie mistake. He had a board”, apparently forgetting that any executive who is entrusted with other people’s money owes a fiduciary duty to their shareholders first, above their own self-enrichment. They are happy to snag the money but then to dodge accountability.

Then, on the other end of the critics’ spectrum, good governance advocates, self-declared governistas, have rushed to offer a smattering of bad solutions for Nvidia’s governance woes. These proposed solutions are as ludicrous as they are trite, and as irrelevant as they are ineffectual.

For example, Charlie Elson at the University of Delaware somehow baselessly managed to blame a favorite bogeyman of many governistas, dual-class shares, for Nvidia’s woes — even though Nvidia has a single-class stock structure and not dual-class shares at all, a rather inconvenient fact which nevertheless did not detract from Elson launching into his tiring tirades. Elson bewilderingly argued in Directors & Boards that “in the end, this may spark, and I hope it does, a real reconsideration of the whole notion of dual-class stock in publicly traded companies”, as if that would be a panacea for all of Nvidia’s board woes, an argument which The New York Times also haplessly repeated.

Similarly, governance professor Richard LeBlanc of York University proposed an independent chair as the panacea to all Nvidia’s governance woes, even though Nvidia already had an independent lead director who was the former CEO of Cooley Law Firm, Stephen Neal.

All of these proposed governista solutions are irrelevant cliches offering insufficient and misguided recipes.

Between clumsy New York Times op-eds, delusional Silicon Valley social media campaigns, and even an ideologically charged, anti-management Delaware Chancery Court judge, it appears that both anti-oversight tech bros and scolding governistas need to relearn some basic lessons about corporate board diligence.

Here’s what these self-anointed governance experts miss and what the real lessons for boards from the Nvidia crisis are:

All of the misguided proposed solutions from governistas tend to draw from the same wrongheaded playbook that ISS and Glass Lewis have preached for decades, recited on cue by the governistas: split the chair and CEO roles, encourage more so-called independent board members, tally the committees, and count board diversity. As we have argued in Fortune, this tired and trite playbook has never been able to tell a good board from a bad one.

Consider how Nvidia was an ISS and Glass Lewis box-checker’s showpiece. Every director but Huang was technically independent. It had an independent lead director who was a former CEO of a respected law firm; independent audit, compensation and governance committees and committee chairs; annual elections for every board position without any staggered terms; and the ‘one vote per share’ principle so beloved by governistas. Huang was not even chairman of his own board: Nvidia had no chairman at all until he left, so Nvidia even fit the technical separation of roles box with a separate lead independent director. In June, shareholders re-elected all ten directors, the CEO with 99% of the vote, with ISS supporting the entire director slate this year, as it has each year for many years now.

But the Nvidia crisis reminds us all just how misleading are the metrics of ISS and Glass Lewis’ checklist. This ‘check-the-box’ approach does absolutely nothing to actually weed out genuinely responsible boards from boards asleep at the switch operating on a bedraggled autopilot. Indeed, it is ironic that many of the worst corporate governance crises this century have been at companies which were darlings of the governista community.

For example, Enron had 12 independent directors among its 14 directors, split the chairman and CEO roles in its final year, and had a former dean of Stanford’s business school, an accounting professor, chairing its audit committee, winning widespread praise from governance advocates with one governance publication even naming the Enron board one of America’s five best boards in 2000 – right before Enron blew up, exposed as a fraud. Similarly, Silicon Valley Bank had an independent chair and a board that was entirely independent other than its CEO, and 10 of Lehman Brothers’ 11 directors were independent.

Indeed, when Stanford researchers tested ISS’s then-flagship governance rating against five measures of how companies actually fared, including restatements, shareholder lawsuits and stock performance, they found no significant correlation with any of them.

The first author wrote in Harvard Business Review in 2002, “In the wake of the meltdowns of such once great companies as Adelphia, Enron, Tyco, and WorldCom, enormous attention has been focused on the companies’ boards … And yet a close examination of those boards reveals no broad pattern of incompetence or corruption. In fact, the boards followed most of the accepted standards for board operations: Members showed up for meetings; they had lots of personal money invested in the company; audit committees, compensation committees, and codes of ethics were in place; the boards weren’t too small, too big, too old, or too young.”

Alas, the reliance upon such banal bromides of supposed good governance, lead to the recitation of platitudes in publications but worse yet miss the targeted need entirely – much the way the Goodhart’s Law of economics warns us to not confuse the metrics of the tool with the assessment of the underlying phenomena itself. This is also parallel to intelligence testing, where the test is an indicator of performance against the unidimensional artificial metrics of the intelligence test as an instrument itself and misses the many other untested dimensions of intelligence as Harvard psychologist Howard Gardner has pointed out in his classic Frames of Mind analysis.

Another favorite bogeyman of the governance community tends to be dual-class shares, and this long-held bias is once again rearing its ugly head with governance commentators rushing to blame dual-class shares for the Nvidia crisis – even though, ironically and inconveniently, Nvidia only has one class of stock, and Nvidia has never actually had dual-class shares at all.

Indeed, ISS now generally universally recommends against directors at companies with unequal voting rights, without exception, reflecting how widely held this erroneous strain of thinking is entrenched in some circles of the governance community.

By way of full disclosure, we are sometimes wrongly identified as an ‘advocate for dual-class structures’, in the words of Vice Chancellor Travis Laster of the Delaware Chancery Court, who has been overturned by the Delaware Supreme Court no less than 35 times in his idiosyncratic and tortured readings of the law.

But we are not the universal dual-class share advocate Laster misleadingly portrays us as. Our view is much more nuanced and captured in the conclusion of my Harvard Law Corporate Governance Forum piece: “In evaluating the outperformance of dual-class shares and in evaluating common scenarios where dual-class shares work especially well, balanced against common pitfalls, the evidence is clear that for companies, there is no single right answer between dual-class, multi-class, or single-class shares. It is all situational, dependent on a company’s circumstances, the demonstrated caliber of their leadership, and the environment in which they operate. Just as dual-class shares are not the right structure for all companies; dual-class shares can make sense for certain companies, in certain scenarios. These crucial nuances are often lost in what has increasingly become the single-minded zealotry of ‘one share, one vote’ advocates, seeking to impose single class structures on all companies irrespective of circumstance.”

Indeed, what critics of dual-class shares like Laster and Elson miss is that some of the worst corporate collapses and frauds of the modern era had ‘one share, one vote’ share structures, ranging from Enron, Worldcom, Tyco, Arthur Andersen, Bear Stearns, Lehman Brothers, Wirecard, FTX, Silicon Valley Bank, and First Republic Bank, all of which share a single commonality: they did not have dual-class shares. Thus, critics like Charlie Elson who blame dual-class shares for Nvidia’s woes are committing the sin of governance by non sequitur. It is like prosecuting a car company for making potential getaway cars for a robbery which was actually committed on foot. Clearly, a formula that indiscriminately, automatically scores Berkshire Hathaway’s share structure as a vice and Nvidia’s as a virtue, without human discernment or space for an iota of discretion, is measuring based on the wrong yardstick.

What failed at Nvidia was not any of the factors identified above; rather, it was the caliber of the directors and the culture amongst the board that proved problematic.

As the first author wrote in the seminal Harvard Business Review article in 2002, ‘What Makes Great Boards Great’, great boards are social systems before they are governance structures. They build enough trust that management brings bad news early, with open and candid discussions and no secrets kept. Great boards treat dissent as a duty, and promote cultures where questioning authority is not only welcome but expected. They rotate roles so that no director is typecast as a permanent cheerleader or scold.

As my former colleague, the former Yale School of Management Dean and former SEC Chairman Bill Donaldson said at a Yale CEO Summit many years ago, “On the subject of board governance… corporate governance has become a cottage industry with lots of opportunists, and there’s some very superficial thought out there about what a board really is…..The least examined is that it is a social entity. It has human beings on it. Nobody talks much about that. Everybody says, ‘Oh, you ought to have certain people on the audit committee because they’ll be independent, etc., etc.’

“There hasn’t been nearly enough work done on the social contract on the board and the recognition of what is really going on, on that board. And a lot of times, what’s really going on bears the seeds of horror stories. Perhaps there’s a group that’s aligned improperly, and that group dominates either through tenure on the board or whatever and allows bad things to go on because nobody really recognizes what those interpersonal relationships are.”

Donaldson emphasized that great boards hold each director accountable and grade their own performance as hard as the CEO’s, and this gets to the caliber of the board as well as the culture of the board. None of this works without directors of the stature to look a monarch in the eye. As I wrote in The Hero’s Farewell, monarchs are larger than life CEOs who would leave office only by death or palace revolt. With a visionary founder, a board’s job is to retain and contain: keep the genius, check the hubris.

But Nvidia’s board lacked the stature to contain or check Huang’s impulses; they could only retain and worship. Evaluating the board members individually, no Nvidia director has ever run an S&P 500 company and none has national security experience, at a company whose chips are a national security asset. Industry peers Microsoft, IBM, and Dell each have at least four current and former big-company CEOs on their board; Nvidia had none. And apparently, the board was asleep at the switch on succession planning, which is one of the primary functions of a board. Nobody thought to groom even an emergency successor for a CEO with more than 60 direct reports and no president or chief operating officer beneath him, and a system of doing business through handshake deals to centralize all power in himself. And when the crisis came, the board’s first instinct was to vouch for him before its own investigation had begun before finally surrendering to the facts of the situation.

Similarly, it was plainly inadequate and insufficient for Nvidia’s board, to simply defer to the professional judgment of their advisors, such as their external accountant PwC or their external law firm Cooley. PwC signed off on financial reports with what many experts consider to be questionable accounting, obfuscating the geographic origins of Nvidia’s revenues and the end users of sensitive Nvidia chips in ways that could possibly open the board up to legal claims of failure of oversight if not even perhaps fraud in the eyes of many experts.

Even today, there still seems to be a latent expectation amongst some in tech circles, including apparently from Huang himself, that a weak Nvidia board will just collapse and bring Huang back in deference to the founder if enough pressure is brought to bear. Huang himself put out an unusual statement this morning, saying that “If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, our employees, the company, and America, I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO”, even though Huang is now the target of no less than 4 ongoing government investigations across congressional oversight hearings and federal probes as well as multiple shareholder lawsuits. Certainly, Marshal Philippe Petain of Vichy France; the deposed Emperor Napoleon, Enron’s disgraced Jeff Skilling, Worldcom’s deposed Bernie Ebbers, and other deposed monarchs dragged out kicking and screaming would have likewise enthusiastically volunteered to return after being thrown out by their constituents. Bewilderingly, even some proxy ratings firms like Egan Jones seem to be lending implicit support to the ‘Bring Jensen Back’ movement, declaring that “It is not entirely clear the board acted in the best interest of shareholders” by removing Huang, looking past the evasion of sanctions, national security, and accounting integrity concerns to say the board should not have removed Jensen on the basis of shareholder returns from prior, possibly misleading financial reports.

What Nvidia’s board woes highlight is that contrary to the outdated ideas of some – for example, how Travis Laster would define ‘good faith’ as checking hot tempers at the door – the measure of genuine board diligence is the willingness to foster board cultures of open dissent and genuine partnership with CEOs over blind deference.

The latter challenge is especially salient when it comes to working with larger-than-life founders. The right answer is not for boards to go to war with these creative geniuses by trying to suppress them, or to lose trust by trying to fire them over foot faults. Rather, it is up to the board to develop governance practices that show they know how to partner with the genius they are privileged to have among them.

The boards of Microsoft, Amazon, Dell, Starbucks and, ultimately, Apple figured out how to work with Bill Gates, Jeff Bezos, Michael Dell, Howard Schultz and Steve Jobs, forging alignment between the needs of their founder visionary CEOs and the shareholders. These boards helped their founders appreciate and address their human frailties, whether with non-accusatory accountability (as with Gates through Microsoft anti-trust litigation) or during health crises (Jobs’ battle with cancer and need for credible succession plan).

It is not easy to regulate genius and creative founders do not resemble rational people as they challenge the daunting odds of success that most people wisely avoid. They are also often obsessed and single minded about their visions – which seem to many to be delusions seeking a mirage of unattainable entrepreneurial success. Yet their innovations can disrupt and transform society on the few occasions where they succeed.

Sigmund Freud reminded us that society is changed by its discontents. Painter Robert Motherwell confessed that as an artist there is never satisfaction, because creative joy is mixed with the anguish of what could be. But at the same time, boards must recognize that they are not dealing with an artist painting in a private studio on their own canvas, but rather an artist who is using other people’s resources, painting on a public canvas, and so must listen to other voices.

Only by forging genuine partnerships with their CEO built on trust and mutual candor, can boards strike the right balance between blind deference and overbearing suffocation, particularly when it comes to working with bigger-than-life visionary founders. The lesson for boards, and what both sides of the critics’ spectrum misses, is that the solution is not related to the structure of the board; rather, it is the professional courage to do their jobs fully, and nobody can legislate performance through checklists or rulebooks.

Sweatt said that pressure means defense tech companies will likely consolidate in “a tranche of deals” in the next six months. Sweatt and Steele said the Pentagon’s lengthy approval processes and outdated systems could keep new tech from reaching troops quickly enough. Sweatt described the Pentagon as an “Industrial Age organization” bogged down with “big long bureaucracies” and “antiquated technology production lines” that will have to change.

“We’re at the beginning of this journey, not the end of this journey,” Steele added. “It will take time because things move slowly; the technology is not moving slowly.”

Sweatt and Steele’s concerns echo those of a Government Accountability Office (GAO) report published in July, which found the average time needed to deliver a capability across the Pentagon’s major weapon acquisition programs rose to over 12 years, and the watchdog predicted this average “will likely increase in the future.”

“It takes a minute for that tail to really reach the tooth,” he said, referring to the lag between investment in defense tech and its actual implementation in the battlefield.

The White House pulled the nomination for Heidi Overton, M.D., Ph.D., to lead the FDA just hours before the Senate was scheduled to vote on her confirmation.

The move hardly comes as a surprise after Overton’s confirmation hearing before the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions (HELP) Committee drew contention.

The news was first reported by Axios on Tuesday evening. The White House confirmed the news to Fierce the following morning.

In September, the HELP committee questioned Overton in a hearing marked by tightrope-walking on issues like vaccines, abortions, flavored vapes and whether or not President Donald Trump is capable of being wrong.

Overton conceded multiple times during the hearing that vaccines approved by the FDA, as well as the “abortion pill” mifepristone, are safe and effective. But she stopped short of admitting that Trump has been wrong when dispensing medical information out of line with what scientific evidence shows.

Overton’s nomination faced mounting pushback and uncertainty from senators ahead of a planned HELP Committee vote on Nov. 18. Fierce reached out to Sen. Ed Markey, the veteran Democratic lawmaker from Massachusetts, ahead of the vote, and he told Fierce that he would “vote against her nomination, and I urge my colleagues to do the same.”

“The FDA needs a leader who will stand up for science and for our kids’ safety, not one who stood by while both were undermined,” he added.

Republican Sen. Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, who chairs the Senate health committee, and other Republicans had also raised questions about Overton's nomination.

As support for Overton looked thin, Republicans were discussing Sen. Roger Marshall, a Kansas Republican, as a potential replacement, Semafor reported today. Health Secretary Kennedy reportedly met with Marshall in recent days to discuss filling the role, according to Politico, but the White House told the publication that it is not considering him.

Robert Steinbrook, M.D., health research group director for Public Citizen, commented today urging the HELP committee to reject Overton’s nomination: “The FDA needs a strong commissioner who stands for science and the integrity of the agency,” Steinbrook said. “Overton is the wrong choice for FDA Commissioner and has done nothing to dispel the well-founded reasons to oppose her candidacy.”

Analysts from Citi shared that the White House’s decision to pull the nomination has a negative impact on biopharma, “extending uncertainty around permanent agency leadership.”

“We view the withdrawal as a modest sentiment negative, but not a material near-term change to the regulatory environment facing most drug and biologics sponsors,” the analysts said in the note.

The withdrawal of Overton's nomination leaves a gap at an FDA that has already faced significant upheaval. The White House only shifted its focus to Overton in August, after the agency had spent three months without a permanent leader.

Since former FDA commissioner Marty Makary, M.D., departed the agency in May, Kyle Diamantas, who hails from the agency’s food regulation division, has been at the helm in an interim capacity.

Why You Should Never Underestimate Mule Deer

If you head north and east out of Salt Lake City toward the Wyoming state line you end up in some of the most stunning country in the United States. At first glance, it looks like a semi-arid range of mountains with the exposed shelves of red rock, patches of sage, and grassy benches typical of the Intermountain West at this latitude.

But once you get past the view from the winding highways that snake through this land and gain some elevation on the dirt roads that take you back to more remote areas, you’ll notice something else. These mountains give birth to a surprising number of springs, providing water that ends up supporting a vast amount of life.

The wet country reveals itself in the stands of aspen that grow thick in places you wouldn’t expect, with waist-high grass that contrasts with the blanket of drought-resistant scrub oak that covers much of the terrain.

This almost contradictory mix of moisture, cover, food, and remoteness provides a recipe for great elk and mule deer, and is the reason I’ll return to this country whenever I can, as happened a few weeks ago this fall. I was there on an invitation from Winchester Ammunition to try its newest offering, Supreme Long Range, on my favorite North American game.

There are many reasons mule deer have such a strong claim on my psyche. I love the wild places they call home. The hours spent glassing them is about the most rewarding way to pass time. And I find it infinitely fascinating to watch and study their behavior.

You see, mule deer are a study in contradiction, too. Are their numbers in decline? In many places, without a doubt. They’ve certainly struggled to maintain their hold in much of their traditional range. Housing and energy development disrupt their winter grounds and migration routes. Diseases like CWD have taken their toll, especially with older bucks. We know that blossoming herds of elk and whitetails can outcompete mulies for food and water, putting more pressure on their numbers.

But they are also resilient and, in some areas, thrive. Good numbers of mule deer live in suburban areas throughout the mountain west. And you can find them in some of the most inhospitable desert regions of North America, and tucked away in other unlikely places.

Though they can seem more docile than their high-strung white-tailed cousins, I have witnessed mule deer put on incredible displays of strength, aggression, and John-Wick-caliber willpower — another contradiction.

Years ago in Colorado, I watched a hunter fail to follow up his shot on a mule deer. The high-shoulder shot dropped the buck where it stood, knocking it senseless. The hunter started celebrating, thinking he had killed it, and when the deer regained its feet the hunter wasn’t ready — and the buck disappeared into the alders. I helped track the deer for several hours through the snow, following a substantial blood trail. We kept going as long as we could after dark but eventually had to call it quits.

The hunter left for the airport and I kept looking for the deer with my good friend Cody while trying to fill my own tag. Two days later, Cody and I spotted the buck tending his does, despite the grievous wound in his upper chest and one ruined leg. He was 500 yards away, skylined on the ridge, and I couldn’t get a shot off before he fled over the mountain.

There was no doubt in my mind the buck was going to die from that injury, but procreation was his sole preoccupation. Despite the obvious pain, and the imminent certainty of death, he would not be deterred.

That buck spurned death, but I’ve also seen mule deer inflict it. I was hunting in southeast Montana a few years back in mid-November when the rut was in full swing. Bucks were bird-dogging does, scent-checking the ladies, cruising in search of mates, and squaring up against potential rivals.

Usually when two bucks face off they will posture a bit and, after sizing each other up, one will back off before things get too heated. Sometimes they will fight — those antlers aren’t just for decoration — and the vanquished male will make his excuses to be elsewhere.

In this case, the smaller of the deer lost his footing during the scuffle and fell back, rolling on his side. The larger buck lowered his head and drove his antlers into the other’s exposed belly.

The deer on his side flailed his legs in a desperate attempt to get up and escape, but through my binocular I could see the spreading circle of red in the snow as the dominant buck continued the savage attack, never letting up.

After several minutes — it felt like an eternity from my vantage — the buck’s fury was spent and he lifted his head and walked off as the other deer lay still, surrounded by his own gore.

I relate this because it is one thing to enjoy hunting, which I do, but it is another to, on occasion, experience the wild at its most raw and profound.

So, when I got this latest opportunity to test my legs and lungs in the high-country in Utah, I had hopes of encountering a mature buck, and perhaps more.

I was joined on the trip by Kyle Masinelli, the vice president of manufacturing operations at Winchester Ammunition, and a friend and work colleague of mine, Ed Friedman, the editor-in-chief of American Rifleman. Kyle was one of the architects of Winchester Supreme Long Range and a great source of information about it.

As the name implies, Supreme Long Range (SLR for short) is geared toward big-game hunting where longer shots might be in the offing, which is often the case when mule deer hunting. This new ammo uses what Winchester calls their “BC Max” bullet, which is a heavy-for-caliber cup-and-core design with an oversized polymer tip to initiate rapid expansion.

Over the summer, I shot several boxes of this new ammunition, chambered in 6.5 Creedmoor, with excellent results in terms of its precision and consistency.

I gathered 20 shots of data with three different rifles, shooting four 5-shot groups with each. Those twelve 5-shot groups ranged in size from .53 inches to 1.10 inches. The overall average group size was .818 inches.

In a curious coincidence, the mean radius value for each of the three rifles was .32 inches, which is very good for hunting ammunition.

The three rifles I used to gather the data were Proof’s MTR Elevation FDX, which won an Editor’s Choice for best hunting rifle of the year; Seekins Precision’s latest PH3 NRL Hunter, which won an Editor’s Choice for best crossover rifle; and a Seekins Precision SIC Short Action, which won’t be officially launched until early 2027.

For the hunt itself, I went a different route, taking a Winchester XPR chambered in 6.8 Western. The XPR has been a perennial choice of ours for an entry-level hunting rifle. It is a no-frills bolt-action that delivers good performance. In its most basic black synthetic stock trim it can be had for less than $600 new.

While I’ve shot plenty of XPRs over the years, I hadn’t had as much time with the 6.8 Western. I shot this souped-up .270 Winchester a bit when it was first rolled out in 2021, but had never done a deep dive on the cartridge or taken it hunting.

I didn’t have as much trigger time with the XPR and the ammunition as I would have liked before rolling into camp, but I did establish a solid zero prior to making the drive from Montana to Utah and knew that the 175-grain 6.8mm SLR bullets were exiting my 22-inch barrel right at 2,700 fps.

We were hunting with the R&K Hunting Company, which has a base camp in Coalville, Utah, and I confirmed my dope on targets they had there. With three shots, I checked my point of impact at 200, 500, and 700 yards, the bullets ringing the center of the steel each time.

Our hunting camp was another hour’s drive into the mountains, most of it off-road, and much of it pitted with craters like small bomb impacts.

But the camp itself was an alpine Shangri-La. The main lodge was a cozy wood structure where the hunters stayed, with a series of small cabins for the guides, and other out buildings, all overlooking a post-card scene of a small lake surrounded by golden-leafed quakies and distant mountains.

Kyle and I spent the first day with our guides, two young brothers named Keston and Kandon Mosher, glassing the heights. In the afternoon, we spotted two excellent bucks engaged in pre-rut posturing.

From nearly a mile away we watched the bucks through our optics. One of the bucks appeared to have a larger, darker body and a heavier rack. The other buck was mature as well, seemingly younger, but with larger antlers. Both were stunning.

We watched them circle each other a bit in a small clearing between stands of alders. We needed to move quickly, because the cover around them was thick, it was getting late, and we were losing light.

We grabbed our packs and guns and hoofed up the mountain as fast as we could. It took about half an hour of hard climbing to get to the knob we wanted to set up on. That put us within 800 yards of where we’d last seen the deer.

Assuming we spotted them again, we’d make a final plan. But that didn’t happen. We glassed the ridge until dark. We could see many of the other deer we’d been watching, but the bucks had vanished. As has happened to me so many times while going after big mule deer, they’d given us the slip.

We returned to the base of that mountain ahead of sunrise, peering into the cover as the light came up. With each passing minute, we took in more ground.

The mountain was actually a long, fairly flat ridge running north to south that topped out at 9,200 feet, and encompassed five separate large drainages. From end-to-end it spanned over three miles. The middle section seemed to hold the most animals, but we frequently spotted elk and deer at the northern and southern edges too.

Early on, Keston caught a flash of antler in an alder thicket not far from the top of the ridge. We all trained our optics on the spot and after a bit the lighter-colored buck stepped into the open. We easily identified him based on his coloration and the picture-perfect four-point frame he carried above his head.

He was feeding on his own about 350 yards from where we’d seen him the previous afternoon. About 30 yards below him was a doe and two fawns, also munching on grass. The other big buck was nowhere to be seen.

There was no way to get to him from below. Even if we could approach from that direction without busting him he’d be out of sight until we stepped into the same clearing. That clearly wouldn’t work. Plus it would take well over an hour and a half to hike up there at which point he’d certainly be gone even if we were as stealthy as ninjas.

Instead, we hiked back down to our side-by-side and took a long looping route to the top of the ridge. From this new vantage, the terrain presented more of a challenge than I would have guessed. Looking down, I could see benches and pockets of cover that were invisible from lower down. This included small natural ponds and abundant feed. An animal could move through that all day and never reveal itself to a hunter glassing from below.

We parked well away from where we saw the buck last and snuck along the spine until we were above his feeding spot. We crept toward the edge and peered down the slope.

At this point the wind shifted so it was coming from directly behind us. We saw the doe and her fawns scoot out of the pocket, having clearly caught our scent.

But the buck should have been between us and them, and if he followed a similar escape route, I would get a shot. We held tight for a quarter hour but saw no sign of him. He’d obviously slipped away in a safer, more hidden direction.

I wasn’t surprised. Mule deer get a bad rap for acting dumb during the rut. And it’s true that some bucks seem to throw caution to the wind when love is in the air. But in my experience I’ve seen more evidence of their clever and well-honed survival instincts preserving them from eager hunters, especially when it comes to truly mature bucks.

That afternoon we returned to the base of the ridge. We set up beneath the knob from the previous night because we didn’t want to overcommit and potentially bust the bucks in case they had moved lower down the mountain.

It wasn’t long before we spotted a new buck working its way down a drainage to our right. He was a handsome 4×4 with eyeguards, and Kyle wanted to take him.

He set his .300 Win. Mag. in a tripod and shot the buck at 350 yards from a kneeling position. The bullet was perfectly placed and the buck ran a few steps and tumbled over. Kyle, Keston, and Kandon (which sounds not unlike a law firm) took off to recover the buck while I stayed behind to keep glassing.

By the time Keston returned we had about an hour left of shooting light. As luck would have it, we caught sight of the light-colored deer trying to sneak off to the south.

With the pressure we’d applied to the north half of the ridge that morning and afternoon (Kyle’s deer was in the same drainage as the bench where we spotted the buck after first light) I wasn’t surprised to see the older deer trying to put some distance between us and him.

Once again it was a race against the falling light. Keston and I took off up the mountain to get within range before dark.

We hiked for 20 minutes to an exposed point that gave us a good view. Keston found the deer again, walking to our left and down toward a thick stand of aspen in a steep draw, where he was about to disappear.

I ranged the deer at 616 yards and got prone, deploying my bipod and setting the butt of the XPR on my shooting bag. The wind, which had been howling earlier in the day, was calming down, but still needed to be accounted for.

I dialed elevation into my scope, held left .4 mil for the wind, and broke the shot. The deer dropped as if poleaxed, kicked a bit, and then lay still. I watched him through my scope with a fresh round chambered until I was sure he was dead.

By the time we got to the vicinity of the deer it was pitch black. Finding an animal under these conditions can be difficult. And while technology is often a two-edged affairm in this case it was a godsend.

For the last year, I’ve been using Leupold’s BX-6 rangefinding binocular in shooting competitions and while hunting. I won’t go into all its virtues here, but one feature that’s a game changer is its ability to range a spot and have it drop a pin into a map, like onX Hunt.

That pin led us right to the buck, saving us who knows how much time stumbling around the dark.

Every hunting bullet has the same end goal, which is to inflict massive tissue damage so that the animal quickly bleeds out, shutting the central nervous system down. (Alternatively, a bullet can strike the CNS directly, with a brain or spinal shot, killing the animal that way.)

How a bullet accomplishes this depends heavily on its construction. Bonded bullets, for instance, are meant to mushroom to a degree, while retaining weight and penetrating deeply.

The BC Max bullet takes a different approach. The large polymer tip on the projectile initiated quick and violent bullet upset. The entrance hole, in the upper portion of the chest, was about two inches across and busted two ribs on the way in.

I could tell the bullet fragmented significantly based on three things: that large entrance wound, the massive tissue damage observed on the deer’s lungs, and the fact that the bullet didn’t penetrate the off side of the ribcage.

We quartered the deer and took backstraps, loins, and neck meat without gutting it, so I didn’t try to recover what was left of the bullet, though I doubt it would have been much.

Of all the big mule deer I’ve shot over my career, this one comes closest to the Platonic ideal. He’s a large, clean four-point, with deep, nearly identical, forks front and back, has a tall boxy frame, carries good mass throughout his rack, and has nice eye guards to boot. If one were to draw an idealized symmetrical mule deer, it would look a lot like this one.

I never take an animal like this for granted. He was a wily deer and had given us the slip a couple times already, and was about to make it a third. And, of course, there was his older cousin, that darker, heavier brute, who only showed himself once.

We were the last deer hunters on that mountain this season. And my fingers are crossed that the other big buck, and his kin, make it through the winter.

I don’t say that because I expect to hunt that spot again. There’s very little chance that will happen. But I do hope some other hunter, who loves these animals the way I do, at least gets to encounter that buck and possibly take him home.
