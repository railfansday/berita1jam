Dungeons & Dragons Unveils Brand-New Species You Can Play For Free

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Dungeons and Dragons has just officially revealed a brand-new species that you can play for free during your adventures and campaigns, without the need to purchase a book supplement. The TTRPG has just seen the early access release of the World of Warcraft expansion for D&D, a new supplement that adapts elements from Blizzard’s MMORPG. The book comes with new classes, like Death Knight and Demon Hunter. There are also new subclass options in D&D: World of Warcraft, like the Oath of the Holy Paladin and the revised Swashbuckler Rogue. On top of that, the expansion offers 16 playable species.

Despite the fact that a whole lot of content has just been released as part of the World of Warcraft expansion, Wizards of the Coast is still treating players who haven’t necessarily purchased it. As part of the November Drop for D&D Beyond members, several freebies were announced, including new weapon options, monsters to fight, feats to pick, and one new species. All of the new content in the November Drop shared on D&D Beyond is centered around the themes of Ysgard and the Upper Planes, which is why a CR 9 Valkyrie is highlighted as a new monster option.

Free species for D&D players has become something new since September, when Wizards of the Coast made the Duskling free for Beyond members. This time around, however, the species in question is the Bariaur. This centaur-like creature’s type is Celestial, as Bariaurs are either born in Ysgard or have ancestors there. Instead of having the lower body of a horse, however, they have the body of a sheep, while the upper part resembles a humanoid – this can be human, elf, or dwarf, for instance.

The Bariaur has a Medium size and a base Speed of 35 feet. This new species also has Darkvision with a range of 60 feet. Playing a Bariaur gives you proficiency in Animal Handling, Arcana, Nature, or Survival, as part of the Wanderer’s Instinct trait. With Ram, when you move 10 feet or more straight toward a creature and hit it with an Unarmed Strike as part of the Attack action on your turn, you can use both the Damage and Shove options, once per turn. This makes the Bariaur species work well with Monks in Dungeons and Dragons.

The Bariaur also has the Stable Stance trait. With it, the character has Advantage on saving throws to avoid the Prone condition. This is not necessarily a broken new species in D&D like the Dracthyr from the World of Warcraft expansion, but it is certainly a nicely balanced one that gives players the option to not play as an often tiresome Humanoid. As a Medium character, Small characters can even ride them, potentially creating a fun dynamic between two characters.

This is not the first time this species is playable, but it is the first time it can be played in 5.5e. The Bariaur was introduced as a playable race back in 2nd Edition, and last appeared as a character option in 3.5e. It’s a major release that newer players may not even be aware of, and a fun little nod to veterans who have been playing Dungeons and Dragons for decades.

Spotted a dazzling light in the eastern sky before sunrise? It's Venus, and the planet is only going to get brighter as it heads toward its spectacular peak later this month.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

Early morning risers heading to work or school before sunrise are likely to notice a very bright, star-like object appearing low in the east-southeast sky just before sunrise. What is somewhat amazing is that just a couple of weeks ago this object wasn't there at all, but now it literally stands out with such brilliance as to almost command the attention of commuters who might be traveling in an easterly direction to "Look at me!"

The object in question is, of course, the planet Venus, which is currently amid one of its most spectacular forays into the morning sky, literally "vaulting up" into prominence.

On Oct. 24, Venus came to inferior conjunction, passing between the Earth and sun and shifting from the evening into the morning sky. And the view is only going to get better in the days and weeks to come!

In terms of morning visibility, Venus is now in a literal and figurative sense "rising to the occasion." It's now rising before the break of dawn, more than 90 minutes before sunrise. From here on, it will rise three minutes earlier each day, so that by Dec. 17 it will rise 3 hours and 50 minutes before sunrise and blaze in a completely dark sky for more than two hours before the first vestige of morning twilight.

It will reach peak brilliance on Nov. 29, when it will shine at an eye-popping magnitude of -4.9. That's 12 times brighter than its nearest planetary competitor, Jupiter and a full 25 times more dazzling than Sirius, the brightest of all stars. If you live in a rural location with no light pollution to interfere, you might be able to make out that it is casting a visible shadow and if you follow it right on through sunrise, it will be evident to your naked eye in full daylight: a tiny white speck plainly evident against the blue daytime sky.

In a way, we could even regard Venus as this year's version of a "Christmas Star," glowing with stunning radiance ". . . in the east" like the biblical quote mentioned in St. Matthew.

After reaching its high point in terms of morning visibility in mid-December, Venus will start a long, slow slide back toward the sun, rising about 1½ minutes later each morning from New Year's Day through late March. By then, Venus will be coming up as dawn is breaking, roughly 100 minutes before sunrise. Venus will continue to get gradually lower and rise progressively later during the spring and by late June it will be coming up less than an hour before sunrise, ultimately vanishing into the bright dawn twilight by July. Venus will then go on a "summer sabbatical" of sorts and will not reappear again until about this time next year when it will begin a very fine evening apparition.

So, if any of your friends or relatives remark to you that they saw a "strange UFO" on the way to work or school recently, you now can set them straight.

A farmer in Mexico saw the ground split open in his cornfield, then watched a volcano grow to 1,391 feet tall

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In 1943, Mexico's Parícutin volcano erupted from a farmer's cornfield, growing rapidly and burying two villages during nine years of activity.

On February 20, 1943, farmer Dionisio Pulido watched a fissure open in his field near the village of Parícutin, in the Mexican state of Michoacán. Smoke rose from the crack, along with a hissing sound and a smell of sulfur.

Within a week, the new cone stood more than 490 feet (150 meters) tall. When the eruption ended in 1952, it had risen 1,391 feet (424 meters) above the cornfield where it began.

Lava buried two villages along the way. Parícutin was the first volcano whose eruption modern scientists observed in detail from its birth, according to the International Union of Geological Sciences (IUGS).

Starting around February 5 to 7, 1943, local witnesses felt about two weeks of earthquake activity before the eruption.

Felipe Cuara Amezcua, then municipal president of San Juan Parangaricutiro, later reported that the tremors grew until more than 200 shook the area in a single day.

On February 20, Pulido went to his farm to prepare the fields for the spring sowing. He had owned the land for 31 years.

Smithsonian mineralogist William F. Foshag and Mexican geologist Jenaro González R. later collected his account and his wife’s. A 1956 U.S. Geological Survey bulletin printed them in English translation.

At about 4 p.m., Pulido left his wife, Paula, and went to burn a pile of branches. She had been hearing underground noise and thunder.

“About 4 p. m., after talking to my husband, I heard a kind of loud whistle,” Paula Pulido recalled.

“I saw that it was a kind of fissure that had only a depth of half a meter,” Pulido said.

He then saw the ground swell by about 6.6 to 8 feet (2 to 2.5 meters), while gray smoke or dust rose from the crack.

“Immediately more smoke began to rise, with a hiss or whistle, loud and continuous,” he recounted.

Frightened, he mounted his mare and galloped to the village where his family was waiting.

Accounts from Pulido and other witnesses gave “the first adequate account of the birth of a new volcano,” Foshag and González wrote.

One of the first photographs of the eruption, taken at 6 p.m. on February 20, shows an ash plume rising from the new vent. By midnight, the cone was about 20 feet (6 meters) high.

On the morning of February 21, a strong earthquake caused panic. People from Parícutin fled to San Juan Parangaricutiro, and many from San Juan went on to Angahuan or Uruapan.

A photograph from February 21 shows the cone about 100 feet (30 meters) tall, just rising above the treetops. After six days of activity, it reached 548 feet (167 meters).

The first lava flow, called the Quitzocho flow, moved north over cornfields prepared for planting. Mexican geologist Ezequiel Ordóñez estimated that the early lava advanced about 10 feet (3 meters) an hour.

Ash fell too. It piled up deeper than 6 inches (15 centimeters) over an area of 116 square miles (300 square kilometers) around the volcano.

When the first phase of activity ended on October 19, 1943, the original vent had built a cone about 1,200 feet (365 meters) high.

Pulido’s home village lay only 1.9 miles (3 kilometers) northwest of the new cone. Its 733 residents had to abandon it for good four months after the eruption began, and lava later flowed over the site.

Starting in January 1944, a new phase sent out the Taquí lava flows, which spread over about 7 square miles (18 square kilometers) by 1945.

In July 1944, lava surrounded the church of San Juan Parangaricutiro. Its tower still stands in the middle of a lava flow, the IUGS notes.

In all, the volcano’s lavas reached up to 2.5 miles (4 kilometers) from the vent and formed a field of about 9.7 square miles (25 square kilometers).

The Mexican government gave displaced residents new land in Caltzontzín, 17 miles (27 kilometers) to the southeast.

Parícutin was “the first well-documented example of an eruption of a monogenetic volcanic cone in historical time,” according to a 1993 USGS film report.

A monogenetic cone forms during a single eruptive episode. Parícutin, active for about nine years, gave scientists an unprecedented chance to follow such a volcano through its full life cycle.

An international group documented the activity in real time. Celedonio Gutiérrez, a resident of San Juan who kept a diary from the first day, worked with U.S. scientists throughout the eruption.

In 1990, volcanologists I. Yokoyama and S. de la Cruz-Reyna analyzed the precursory earthquakes of the 1943 eruption, a type of signal that comes before eruptions.

In 1985, decades after the eruption stopped, a fumarole at Parícutin still measured 883°F (473°C). The cone is one of more than 1,400 vents in the Michoacán-Guanajuato volcanic field.

The information was obtained from the Smithsonian Institution’s Global Volcanism Program.

‘To Hold a Mountain’ Took on the Government — Now It’s Montenegro’s Oscar Submission

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

For one of the most important screenings of “To Hold a Mountain,” the filmmakers had to bring their own electricity. In July, co-directors Biljana Tutorov and Petar Glomazić hauled a tent, a generator, and a DCP up to the Montenegrin highlands where they had spent years filming.

The creative couple, also partnered in life, wanted the shepherds who lived and worked in Sinjajevina to see the movie they had made possible. But by then, Montenegro had already resolved their story’s central conflict — scrapping the government’s plan to turn these people’s ancestral pastures into a military training ground after joining NATO in 2017. “To Hold a Mountain” won the World Cinema Grand Jury Prize for documentary at Sundance, and Montenegro’s prime minister and president even showed up to the shepherds’ screening with their families, unannounced. When the film opens at New York’s Film Forum on December 16, American audiences will see the feature’s updated conclusion.

“We reopened the film to continue shooting and then a second end happened that was very empowering and powerful,” Tutorov said at IndieWire’s Art of the Doc series.

During our conversation, the filmmaker recalled arriving in Sinjajevina with Glomazić on the first day of protest. They wanted to help the community and make a movie, and agreed early on that those were two different jobs.

“We will separate our activist mission from our filmmaker’s mission,” she said. “That was a very good decision because it allowed us to be very bold in both roles.”

Onscreen, the fight over who would get to lay claim to the mountain centered on two people who already did: Gara, a shepherd and community leader, and Nada, the young girl she was raising. As the threat to their home brought a history of violence in their own family to the surface, the directors had to reckon with how much of Nada’s private life might become public.

“We really wanted it to be an empowering experience for [her],” Tutorov said.

Nada was twelve and a half when the filmmakers began to understand what the story would ask of her, and Tutorov said they decided to keep filming until she turned eighteen — precisely so Nada could decide for herself whether she wanted to be part of it. The crew logged more than 230 shooting days, though some yielded only half an hour of footage.

Gara and Nada had work to do, too. The directors and cinematographer Eva Kraljević shared a small shepherd’s shed, assisting with various chores including cows that had to be milked twice a day. Over successive summers, their routine became a framing device. Tutorov, who studied art history, thinks of the mountain as a kind of chorus bearing witness to living things on it.

Last July, just as the filmmakers were wrapping up, Montenegro’s government canceled the training-ground plan. Tutorov believes “To Hold a Mountain” and how it told the existing community’s story influenced that decision. She also pointed out that a different government was in power by 2025, following years of organizing by Sinjajevina locals.

At the shepherd’s screening a year later, Prime Minister Milojko Spajić said the film reinforced his view that canceling the plan had been the right call. Then Montenegro chose “To Hold a Mountain” as its official submission for the international feature Oscar. Tutorov said there was irony in realizing that, after years of fighting the government, she and Glomazić would be proudly representing the country on a global stage.

“It feels like they really kind of destroyed our strategy,” she said, warmly.

It’s a good joke, and an even better reason to remember that rough-and-tumble showing. For an evening, the people who’d spent years making their case to the government got to host its leaders on the very land they’d fought to keep. Tutorov recalled what Montenegro’s president told the filmmakers after seeing the movie: “Thank you guys for teaching us democracy.”
