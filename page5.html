Warren Buffett Said This 1 Mistake Cost Berkshire About $10 Billion. Here’s How Investors Can Avoid It.

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Buffett said that hesitating over a small price gap on Walmart cost Berkshire about $10 billion. Here’s how to avoid the same trap.

Warren Buffett never hid from his investment mistakes. Instead, he wanted to learn from them. One of his most expensive blunders wasn't a bad purchase, but one he never completed.

At Berkshire Hathaway's (BRKA +0.47%)(BRKB +0.53%) 2004 annual meeting, Buffett confessed that his worst mistake was Walmart (WMT +1.51%). While he initially wanted to buy 100 million shares of the retail giant, Berkshire bought only a small amount because, as the price went up, Buffett waited for it to come back down, which never happened. His reluctance to pay a little more turned out to be a $10 billion mistake.

The mistake wasn't buying a bad stock. It was getting too anchored to a price on a good one. That's a trap almost every investor falls into, and it's easy to avoid once you know what to look for.

Walmart was exactly the kind of business Buffett wanted to own. It was a dominant low-cost leader. He set his sights on buying 100 million shares at a pre-split price of $23. Buffett bought some shares, but then the price rose slightly. He thought it might come back down, so he waited. That pullback never came. In hindsight, he said paying a little more would have been inconsequential compared to the gains the stock would go on to deliver.

Buffett did try to fix this mistake. Berkshire built a new Walmart stake starting in 2005, which was worth nearly $6 billion by 2016, the year it sold most of it.

Berkshire's missed gains on Walmart show why anchoring can be so costly. If you think a $50 stock has the potential to gain 50% over the next few years, paying 5% more ($52.50) than your desired price would yield a 43% return. That's not a bad return. Waiting for the price to come back down to your target, on the other hand, risks the entire upside if it never does. A small premium costs you a little in the long run, while a missed position costs you the whole gain.

The lesson here isn't to pay any price for a stock. Buffett's Walmart mistake served as a valuable lesson because Walmart's an exceptional business, so paying a few percentage-point premium was small relative to its upside. However, chasing a stock that has already run up, or paying a premium for an average business, are different mistakes. It's fine to pay a little bit more than you'd like for a high-quality business, but don't go overboard.

Patience and price discipline are good habits, but Buffett's Walmart miss shows how they can backfire when a small price gap keeps you out of a great business. Decide what a stock is worth before you buy, set your purchases in advance, and weigh any premium against the upside you expect. That way, your discipline protects you from overpaying without costing you the opportunity.

This Tractor Supply Mini-Excavator May Not Be The Smallest, But It Is One Of The Cutest

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Remember playing with your Tonka trucks as a wee kiddo? If you grew up before tablets displaced little metal keepsakes, you ruled an imaginary work site, moving clumps of sand, dirt, and mud from one place to other spots. Granted, those other places were often your hair, shirt, and pants pockets. Oh, those were the days. Fret not, forever child — Yardmax has a tool that bridges the gap between practical equipment and a rideable Tonka-style toy.

It's the Yardmax YE0610, a 1,432-pound mini-excavator with a host of available attachments, from a claw to a grading bucket and rake.  Better yet, you might just be able to scoot on down to your local Tractor Supply and pick one up.

That said, Yardmax's diminutive earth mover isn't the smallest thing in the mini-excavator market. In fact, if you want to get crazy with your tiny tools, Honda and Komatsu once teamed up to make what may very well be the cutest little digging machine ever.

You've seen the big stuff. You know, the massive, earth-moving heavy construction equipment required to build a modern highway. Monstrous bulldozers, cold milling machines, and long-necked dinosaur-ish excavators are all part of the process. Some of these dirt-munching excavators can get properly huge, with the biggest examples tipping the scales at over 2 million pounds. Yes, you read that right.

Other excavators, however, go for more of a park-it-in-your-garage footprint. That's what the Yardmax YE0610 mini-excavator is all about. At just 27.5 inches wide, the wee digging machine's width takes up considerably less floor space than a common go-kart. Even with the canopy installed on the YE0610-575003 model, the mini-excavator is 62.4 inches tall and 1,674 pounds. Opt for the topless base YE0610, and that weight drops to 1,432 pounds. Not exactly a 2-million-pound earth mover.

Okay, so it's cute. Can it dig? Yardmax asserts the YE0610 can dig up to 47.2 inches deep, or just under four feet. Then there are the attachments. Depending on the model, the Yardmax can use a claw, grading bucket, or rake for your digging, filling, clearing, and leveling tasks. As far as power goes, the mini-excavator features a 306-cc engine capable of hustling the little machine at a cautious 1-mph pace. As expected, the excavator can rotate 360 degrees, regardless of track position.

This wee workhorse might be one of the wildest things Tractor Supply sells. Then again, it might not be. After all, this is a retailer that also offers a teeny tiny minibike to awaken your inner child.

As of this writing, the Yardmax YE0610, complete with its removable canopy, grading bucket, and rake, is for sale at Tractor Supply for $6,999.99. Want to save a couple hundred bucks on your backyard digging machine? The same setup is available at Walmart for $6,799. Of course, if you'd rather go in another direction entirely, Tractor Supply also sells a Power Wheels-esque mini truck. Your mini truck won't likely do any excavating for you, though.

Here's the thing, though: the Yardmax YE0610 isn't the smallest thing on the mini-excavator market. Croba, a sub-brand of MechMaxx, produces a MEC06 mini-excavator that weighs 87 pounds less than the Yardmax and undercuts the Tractor Supply price for the YE0610 by nearly $2,000, though its max digging depth falls shy of the YE0610 by 3.9 inches.

Like a shrinking set of nesting dolls, things can get even smaller. Komatsu, a Japanese heavy machinery manufacturer, joined forces with Honda in 2022 to produce an unbearably cute rideable excavator for the Japanese market. The result was the Komatsu PC01E-1, a 750-pound electric micro excavator with unmistakable toy aisle looks.

Better yet, the PC01E-1 falls just shy of the 771.6-pound max load capacity of Japanese mini trucks. So, go ahead, load your pint-sized electric excavator into your tiny truck.

Former Stoke City manager Thordarson dies aged 71

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Gudjon Thordarson had spells in charge of four English clubs - Stoke City, Barnsley, Notts County and Crewe Alexandra

Gudjon Thordarson, who led Stoke City to promotion from the third tier in 2002 and managed three other English clubs, has died aged 71.

He had been living with Parkinson's in recent years.

Thordarson had managed his native Iceland before becoming Stoke boss in 1999 and was in charge for their EFL Trophy success in 2000.

After consecutive play-off semi-final defeats in Thordarson's first two seasons, the Potters went up through the play-offs in 2002 - although Thordarson departed the club a few days later.

He also had spells in charge of Barnsley (2003-04), Notts County (2005-06) and Crewe Alexandra (2008-09).

Why Airlines Schedule Flights To Take Longer Than They Used To—Even Though It Costs Them More

Former Ohio Attorney General Dave Yost says his flight to Washington’s National Airport still takes about 50 minutes, but the published schedule has grown to an hour and 42 minutes. He thinks he’s figured out why: airlines get to look more on-time by giving themselves longer to arrive. He’s right that the schedule determines whether a flight counts as on time. But airlines pay for those extra minutes, and they generally have good reasons to avoid adding more than they need. My flight to DCA takes about 50 minutes. It used to be listed as an hour & change. More demand concentrated into busy periods, limited runway and gate capacity, controller shortages and equipment problems all affect how long the trip takes. The problem also isn’t just the average. It’s how much travel time varies. An airline needs a schedule that works when there’s a normal amount of congestion, not just when the plane gets straight to the runway and encounters no delay at the other end. If that variability grows, the schedule can need more time even when the flight takes 50 minutes in the air. I made this point when covering claims about increased schedule padding in 2023. Airport and air traffic control bottlenecks make trip time less predictable. Airlines build that uncertainty into their schedules. Airlines Pay For Those Extra Minutes I’ve been pushing back on the simple ‘they’re juking the stats’ explanation since at least 2015. Making flights look longer is an expensive way to improve a scorecard. Crew costs. Scheduled time can determine pay even when a flight arrives early. American Airlines flight attendants, for instance, receive flight time pay based on the greater of scheduled or actual block time for each flight. Less productive airplanes. Add time across the day’s flying and there’s less room for another revenue flight. The airline can wind up needing more airplanes and crews to operate the same service. Less attractive itineraries. Longer trips can lose bookings to faster connections, competing airlines, or driving. Airlines want to sell you a convenient schedule. Back in 2015, I cited Southwest Airlines’ estimate that adding just a couple of minutes of block time across its schedule would cost the equivalent of 8 to 10 airplanes’ daily flying. You can’t just push flights later in the day, because airlines need to schedule flights when people want to fly. And airlines help create some of their own congestion through banked schedules at hubs. Packing flights into connecting waves makes more itineraries possible, but it also puts more demand on gates, runways and employees at the same time. Longer Schedules Can Improve More Than The Statistics The government counts an arrival as on time when the plane reaches the gate less than 15 minutes after its scheduled arrival. But if an airline keeps missing its arrival times by small margins, increasing block time can be a sensible first step toward fixing reliability. It’s acknowledging how long operating the flight actually takes, including the delays that occur often enough to plan for. American announced longer block times alongside a redesign of their Dallas operation, moving from 9 large connecting waves to 13 smaller ones in April. I explained why that investment could pay for itself through fewer missed connections, delayed bags and disrupted aircraft and crews. In April, American’s Dallas hub chief reported that missed connections were down 50%. At United Airlines’ 2019 media day, an executive explained that 25% of their flights were expected to arrive 10 minutes early. That gave them room to hold some departures for connecting passengers and still arrive on time through their ConnectionSaver program. United had started improving its reliability with longer block times. Arriving Early Can Create A Gate Problem Of course, extra time in the schedule doesn’t create extra gates. Get in early and the gate assigned to your flight may still have another airplane on it. An empty gate may belong to another airline, be unsuitable for your aircraft, or lack the employees needed to bring you in and operate the jetbridge. I covered Doug Parker explaining this problem in 2018: American used essentially all its hub gate capacity at peak times, and a few airplanes failing to leave could leave arriving flights with nowhere to go. So you can land early, sit waiting, and reach the terminal around the originally scheduled time. The government’s arrival clock keeps running until gate arrival. Regardless, it’s not all a conspiracy to juke the stats. Often it’s just a realistic expectation for how long the airline thinks on average it’ll take to travel, though sometimes they beat those expectations. The real solution isn’t just tightening schedules, it’s improving throughput – more capacity in air travel control and at airports. Is the Nikon D850 Really as Good as You Remember, or Is It Just Nostalgia?

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Is the Nikon D850 Really as Good as You Remember, or Is It Just Nostalgia?

I never owned a Nikon D850, although I put some miles on my D700 over the years. I did get a chance to review the D850, though, on one of my first major press trips to Bend, Oregon. This was back in the TCS TV days, and it was there that I met many of the industry professionals who I now consider good friends.

That's a good place to start, because my time with the D850 is steeped deeply in good memories and nostalgia. It got us wondering... Was this the case for everyone else too, or is this camera actually as good as we remember?

It’s quite rare to have reviewed a digital product almost ten years ago and find that it is still available today brand new. When KEH said they had a D850 to lend us, I happily agreed to revisit this legendary camera and focus more on the shooting experience than on a proper review.

The D850 wholeheartedly deserves its legendary status. It was the pinnacle of Nikon DSLR technology, combining a relatively compact chassis with a high-res 45.7-megapixel sensor. I loved composing images through the brilliantly clear viewfinder with 100% coverage and the 2.3-million-dot LCD panel with full tilt articulation.

This is an SLR that could focus quickly, track faces and subjects with 3D-tracking technology, and fire bursts up to 9 frames per second with the right grip and battery combination.

The chassis is fully weather-sealed and made out of solid magnesium, with all the controls required to customize the camera and handle difficult shooting scenarios. Professional shooters will also appreciate the dual card slots for a CFexpress Type B card and a UHS-II SD card.

This is also a bit of a hefty camera, weighing almost 35 ounces (1 kilogram) when you throw a card and battery in. Most full-frame mirrorless cameras will weigh well under this amount, although Nikon seems to like heavy cameras, with the Z8 being the most similar, in both function and weight.

Regardless, the Nikon D850 is a pleasure to shoot, and it feels like the sophisticated tool that it is. It also happens to take some incredibly high-quality images, thanks to a sensor that may have been way ahead of its time.

It's amazing to think that a sensor placed into the D850 can still outperform modern mirrorless sensors in terms of raw dynamic range. Now, to be fair, the improvement is minor, and modern sensors read out way faster than they ever did. However, the fact is that the D850 sensor easily stands shoulder to shoulder with any full-frame camera today.

When we say that sensor technology has plateaued in the last decade, this is the perfect example. The D850 has a base ISO of 64, gathering extra amounts of light when possible, and will deliver incredible dynamic range and low-light performance. If you are looking for a rugged camera that can push shadows and pull highlights to the extreme, the D850 is a perfect choice.

Considering that the D850 can be purchased brand new for much less than a Nikon Z8, and that there is also a very healthy used market where the pricing gets even better, the D850 could make real sense to a photographer who doesn't necessarily need the mirrorless advantage.

That mirrorless advantage will largely manifest in terms of video performance, autofocusing sophistication, and more compact bodies and lenses. However, this isn't to say that the D850 is particularly weak in these regards either. I don't get the ability to autofocus anywhere within the frame, but the D850's coverage is still quite plentiful.

The 3D tracking works great even for inanimate objects, and there are plenty of situations where a single focus-and-recompose technique is all you need. Video is also fairly underdeveloped in the D850, with 4K resolution being available but somewhat soft-looking. The autofocus when using Live View is also abysmal, which was the standard for almost any non-Canon DSLR at the time.

However, there is a large market of photographers who don't care about video, don't need advanced subject-tracking autofocus, and don't mind a larger camera system. You won't get IBIS, but there are plenty of optically stabilized lenses available, and I found the shutter and weight of the camera to be solid and easy to hold steady even at slower shutter speeds.

Perhaps one of the strongest reasons to consider the D850 is just the joy of using an SLR camera as a different shooting experience. I had this same enjoyable realization when shooting the Canon Digital Rebel, and that camera is nowhere close to the D850's league. Composing with a premium viewfinder and having a powerful focusing system make the DSLR experience even better, and you don't have to break the bank to do it.

For the same price as an entry-level Nikon Z5II body, the Nikon D850 would give you a lot of performance for your money, along with an arguably more enjoyable shooting experience. I highly recommend giving it a try.

How Much Does An Airbus A380 Engine Cost?

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ask what an Airbus A380 engine costs, and the most honest answer is that the sticker price barely tells the story. Neither Rolls-Royce nor Engine Alliance publishes a catalog figure for the Trent 900 or the GP7200, and the biggest numbers attached to them in public are billion-dollar contracts that cover much more than the engines themselves. According to analyst estimates reported by AviationPros, a single GP7200 once carried a list price of roughly $7 million to $10.5 million. Yet one engine's share of Emirates' 2015 Rolls-Royce deal works out several times higher, and that difference points to where the real cost lies.

This article traces that difference back to the published estimates for the contracts airlines actually signed. Two engine families sit at the center of the story. Rolls-Royce builds the three-shaft Trent 900, while Engine Alliance, a 50-50 venture between GE Aerospace and Pratt & Whitney, builds the GP7200. Both powered the A380-800, the only member of the A380 family to reach service, but neither was ever simply sold as a piece of hardware.

There is no published list price, so every answer is necessarily an estimate. The analyst range quoted above came from an older report written while the A380 was still being delayed, and Pratt & Whitney declined to confirm it, noting that airlines usually receive discounts on large orders. After two decades of inflation and engine upgrades, it likely understates today's replacement cost. More importantly, it describes a bare engine, which is rarely what an airline actually signs for, and says nothing about spare engines, tooling, or the support network required to keep an engine flying.

The contracts signed by major A380 operators show why the simple number can be misleading. According to Emirates' own announcement, its April 2015 deal with Rolls-Royce for Trent 900 engines and a long-term TotalCare package covering 50 A380s was worth $9.2 billion, although a footnote put the order-book value at $6.1 billion. That figure included the engines and long-term support rather than representing a simple purchase price. Etihad's GP7200 deal was structured similarly: GE Aerospace said in its original announcement that the airline selected GP7200s for ten A380s and signed a multiyear Fleet Management Agreement covering maintenance, repair and overhaul, with a total value of more than $1.3 billion. A subsequent GE Aerospace announcement put the value at up to $1.5 billion. Rolls-Royce described the Emirates order as the largest in its history in its press release.

These figures therefore cannot be treated as straightforward engine prices. Emirates' $9.2 billion package covered 200 Trent 900 engines plus TotalCare, while Etihad's $1.3 billion-plus GP7200 agreement covered engines for ten A380s together with long-term fleet management and maintenance. Dividing the headline contract values by the number of installed engines would produce roughly $46 million per engine for the Emirates package and more than $32.5 million per engine for Etihad's original $1.3 billion figure, but those calculations include services and should not be mistaken for the price of the hardware itself.

The reason these numbers diverge is straightforward: an A380 engine contract is rarely only an engine purchase. The Emirates and Etihad packages bundled hardware with years of servicing. TotalCare covers overhauls, spare parts, and health monitoring, while Etihad's agreement included long-term fleet management. Engine makers can heavily discount the hardware and recover part of their investment through the aftermarket, a model established in the commercial engine business for decades.

The size of those discounts can make a published list price almost theoretical. As AirInsight reported from Rolls-Royce's patent lawsuit against Pratt & Whitney, Rolls-Royce said it had to discount the Trent 900 by more than 87%, compared with a normal discount of 77%. That makes it difficult to treat the nominal price of an engine as a reliable measure of what an airline actually pays.

The service contract is a crucial part of the economics. Aircraft Commerce explains that most Rolls-Royce engines are maintained under TotalCare packages, with services selected from a menu and billed per engine flight hour, giving airlines a predictable cost. AirInsight also wrote that Engine Alliance said it offered its engine at cost in the Emirates competition, only to be undercut by Rolls-Royce. For an A380 operator, the real purchase is consequently a package of hardware, spares and years of support rather than four isolated engines.

Simple Flying makes the same broader point about A380 ownership in its comparison of A380 and 747 costs: the purchase price is only the first part of the aircraft's financial story. For the engines, that distinction becomes even more important once they begin accumulating flight hours.

A closer look at the combination of GEnx strengths and Trent 1000 weaknesses that have led many airlines to opt for the GEnx.

The maintenance figures show just how quickly the original purchase price becomes only one part of the equation. According to Aircraft Commerce's owner's and operator's guide to the Trent family, published in 2012, a Trent 900 first shop visit typically cost $5.0 million to $5.3 million, while a second visit cost $6.2 million to $6.5 million. The first removals were expected after 23,000 to 28,000 engine flight hours.

The same guide put the Trent 900's life-limited parts at $4.9 million for disks and shafts plus another $1.9 million for fan blades and related parts, or about $6.8 million altogether. That is roughly the same order of magnitude as the old analyst estimate for an entire GP7200. These components also have defined service lives. The Trent 900's 18 disk and shaft items were certified for anywhere from 1,000 to 12,500 flight cycles against a 15,000-cycle target, meaning early-build engines could face heavier and earlier work.

Aircraft Commerce translated those maintenance costs into reserves of $245 to $291 per engine flight hour through the first shop visit, rising to $320 to $407 through the second. Across all four engines, that means an A380 could carry roughly $980 to $1,160 in engine reserves per flight hour at the first interval alone, in 2012 dollars. Because those figures are now more than a decade old, they should be treated as a historical floor rather than a current forecast. Newer estimates run higher: aircraft broker Jettly puts full Trent 900 overhauls at $10 million to $15 million each.

The maintenance cycle also explains why the support business remains important even though A380 production has ended. As Aviation Week's data tool forecasts, the Trent 900 fleet will see 548 overhaul shop visits over the next decade. Those visits involve much more than simply removing and reinstalling an engine, and the engineering behind the bill helps explain why the original hardware is so expensive.

The scale of the A380's powerplants is the first clue to their complexity. Each engine swings a 116-inch (296-cm) fan and produces enormous thrust. Aircraft Commerce gives the Trent 900 a maximum thrust rating of up to 84,000 lbf (374 kN), while Engine Alliance lists the GP7200 at 70,000 to 81,500 lbf (311 to 363 kN). The GP7200 also has an 8.8 bypass ratio and a pressure ratio of more than 36, while its 116-inch fan uses 24 swept, hollow titanium blades.

The GP7200 is also a genuinely multinational engine rather than a simple GE-Pratt & Whitney combination. Engine Alliance's current component breakdown assigns the fan and accessory gearbox to Pratt & Whitney, while Safran supplies the low-pressure compressor and booster and the high-pressure compressor. GE Aviation is responsible for the combustor, high-pressure turbine, and FADEC, while MTU Aero Engines supplies the low-pressure turbine. The arrangement combines technologies and manufacturing expertise from several major engine companies in a single powerplant.

The Trent 900 follows a different route with its three-shaft architecture. Aircraft Commerce notes that hollow, wide-chord fan blades reduce weight and drag across the Trent family. It describes the Trent 900 as a scaled-down three-shaft design with 24 swept fan blades, a tiled combustor that cools its inner walls with a film of air so it can burn hotter and cleaner, and a contra-rotating turbine that allows smaller guide vanes to perform the same work. Each feature contributes to fuel burn, noise, or performance, but each also requires precise manufacturing and inspection.

Rolls-Royce said in its 2015 announcement of the Emirates order that the Trent 900 had powered the first commercial A380 in 2007 and was already in service with eight operators across more than 70 aircraft, having accumulated over four million flight hours. The same Rolls-Royce announcement, reproduced by AviationPros. This was therefore a mature engine program by the time Emirates selected it for another 50 A380s.

All that engineering had to be scaled for an exceptionally large aircraft. According to Simple Flying's look back at 20 years of the A380, the A380-800 weighs up to 1,234,600 lb (560,000 kg) at takeoff and spans 261 feet, 8 inches (79.75 meters). That scale makes the engines technically impressive, but technical efficiency alone could not solve the A380's larger economic problem.

Its connection to the Airbus A380 reveals how planemaker reshaped its flight decks.

The Trent 900 and GP7200 were designed to make a four-engine aircraft as efficient as possible, and they achieved meaningful gains. Aircraft Commerce's 2012 comparison of Trent-powered aircraft found that an A380 with Trent 970 engines burned 35,319 US gallons (133,700 liters) of block fuel on a Frankfurt-New York flight. That equated to 0.0196 US gallons per seat-mile (0.046 liters per seat-km), the best result among the four-engine aircraft examined, although it remained slightly worse than the twin-engine A330s and 777-200ERs on unrestricted routes. The analysis assumed 486 seats on the A380.

Engine Alliance presents the GP7200's economics in more favorable terms. The manufacturer says the engine can deliver up to $500,000 in annual fuel savings per aircraft and a payload-range advantage worth up to $5.5 million a year. In a 2010 press release, the company also said Airbus credited the engine with a 0.5% fuel-consumption improvement and that dispatch reliability stood at 99.9% after nearly two years in service. These are manufacturer claims rather than independent cost calculations, but they show what Engine Alliance considered the engine's economic selling points when airlines were choosing between the two A380 powerplants.

The numbers illustrate the problem the A380 ultimately faced. The engines could be remarkably efficient for their size, but four engines still meant four sets of maintenance requirements, four nacelles, and four powerplants consuming fuel. Airbus built its last A380 in 2021, shifting the economics of the engines once again. With production over, their value increasingly depends on the aircraft that are already flying.

The end of A380 production has changed what makes a Trent 900 or GP7200 valuable. No new engine will be built for a new A380, so future revenue increasingly comes from overhauls, replacement parts, upgrades, and the engines already installed on aircraft that remain in service. The engine has effectively moved from a new-aircraft product to a long-term support business.

The size of that installed fleet still matters. According to Simple Flying's report on why Airbus offered two engines, Airbus took 251 A380 orders, 130 for GP7200-powered aircraft and 121 for the Trent 900. That left both engine families with a substantial installed base, and both manufacturers therefore have a long-running aftermarket opportunity even though the production line is closed.

Utilization will determine how long that opportunity remains attractive. The same report found Dubai hosted 29,626 A380 flights in 2026, followed by London Heathrow with 6,974 and Singapore Changi with 5,684. Concentrated flying keeps engines accumulating flight hours and reaching shop visits on a predictable schedule, exactly the type of activity that long-term service agreements such as TotalCare are designed to support. At the same time, the concentration of the remaining fleet around a relatively small number of airlines and hubs means that one operator's fleet decision can affect the economics of an entire engine family.

That makes the future value of the A380 engine more complicated than its original price tag. Airbus has not completely closed the door on the aircraft itself. As Aeroflap reported, Airbus commercial chief Christian Scherer said in July 2024 that the door was closed, "but it is not locked." Whether or not that door ever opens again, the Trent 900s and GP7200s already flying will continue generating maintenance and support revenue for as long as airlines keep their A380s in the air. The real question is no longer what one of these engines costs to buy, but how long operators will consider four of them worth paying to keep running.

Magic: The Gathering’s 5 Best and Weirdest Star Trek References

The set has nods to some of the franchise’s silliest moments

Magic: The Gathering’s Star Trek is a love letter to the franchise. The set uses sagas to pay tribute to standout episodes like “The Inner Light” and “In the Pale Moonlight,” and it’s stuffed with cards representing legendary characters and spacecraft along with famous quotes like “Live long and prosper.” But the set also features plenty of deeper cuts, embracing the times that Star Trek was silly, controversial, and downright terrible. These are the five best references to the franchise’s worst and weirdest moments.

If you’re wondering why one version of the art for Lieutenant Commander Data shows him dressed like Sherlock Holmes, it’s because of the Star Trek: The Next Generation episode “Elementary, Dear Data.” Chief Engineer Geordi La Forge creates a holodeck program where the android Data can play detective, but finds all of the usual mystery programs are too easy. Geordi asks the computer to create a program with an adversary capable of defeating Data, which turns out to be very dangerous.

The version of Holmes’ nemesis Professor Moriarty portrayed in Adversary by Algorithm becomes aware that he exists inside a simulation and gains access to the ship’s computer. While Data and Captain Picard are able to stop him, Moriarty returns to cause trouble in the episode “Ship in a Bottle” and even makes an appearance in the sequel series Star Trek: Picard.

Humpback Whales might seem like a better fit for Magic’s upcoming aquatic-focused set Nauctis than Star Trek, but this creature is a reference to Star Trek’s silliest movie, Leonard Nimoy’s Star Trek IV: The Voyage Home. When an enormous probe begins causing catastrophic storms on Earth, Spock realizes that the spacecraft is playing humpback whale songs. The Enterprise’s former officers travel back to 1986 to save the species from extinction. This involves a lot of antics like Pavel Chekov being mistaken for a Russian spy and Dr. Leonard McCoy using future tech to cure hospital patients. The spacecraft H.M.S. Bounty, Unlikely Timeship represents the stolen Klingon Bird of Prey the crew uses in the film, which is why it lets you search for a whale and Time Walk.

The blue removal spell Pongify, which turns a creature into an ape, gets a perfect reprint paying tribute to the TNG episode “Genesis,” where a flu treatment goes awry, causing everyone on the Enterprise to de-evolve. Star Trek: Voyager did a much worse riff on the same idea in "Threshold," which is considered one of the worst episodes in the entire franchise. De-evolve is a reference to “Threshold,” where helmsman Tom Paris pushes a shuttlecraft beyond the standard threshold of Warp 10 and his body begins mutating when he returns to the ship. Dilemmas are a major theme of this set, representing the many ethical quandaries Starfleet officers face. But none is more notorious than the one represented in Impossible Choice. Tuvix embodies the best traits of both characters, with Neelix’s humor making Tuvok an even better advisor and tactical officer. He begs the rest of the crew to let him live, and the Doctor refuses to p the procedure himself because of his oath to do no harm. Janeway decides she wants her old crew members back and handles the separation herself. Star Trek fans have considered her a monster ever since.
