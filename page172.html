West Ham manager Scott Parker’s comments following last night’s incredible comeback against Cardiff City were an absolute joy to listen to. Having watched the Hammers recover from three goals down to draw 3-3, I was already feeling considerably more optimistic about our prospects. But listening to Parker afterwards was almost as encouraging as the second-half performance itself.

In the modern game, much is made of tactics, and rightly so. But one thing I believe gets underplayed is not only man-management, but a head coach’s personality. And quite frankly, Parker has bundles of it.

I have to say, it’s a refreshing change from what we’ve seen in recent seasons. Nuno Espírito Santo was a softly spoken man, not prone to using a few words when considerably more would do, and the less said about Graham Potter as a leader and orator, the better at this point. But my word, it’s thrilling to hear Parker speak.

Speaking in his post-match interview with West Ham’s official website, Parker described the transformation in his team after the break:

“It was a forceful team, an aggressive team, a front-footed team, which was obviously a big improvement.”

That’s exactly what I want to hear from a West Ham manager, and more importantly, it’s exactly what I want to see from a West Ham team.

As Martins morning round-up demonstrated, the difference between the two halves was extraordinary.

We went from a team seemingly incapable of stringing together a meaningful attack to one that looked determined to take the game to the opposition.

And Parker clearly recognised the difference.

He was particularly critical of the backwards passing and the lack of purpose in possession during the opening 45 minutes.

Then came another observation that I found particularly encouraging:

“There’s no point in just building, building, building.”

Again, music to my ears and we saw that with the first two goals from Mukasa and Engels.

New findings suggest that "food challenges" can help prevent children from being saddled with unnecessary allergy diagnoses.

A peanut allergy diagnosis in infancy can mean years of avoiding the food or undergoing treatments to build tolerance. But a recent study suggests that more than one-third of babies thought to have the allergy may stop reacting badly to peanuts within months of that diagnosis.

In an analysis published in The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice, researchers conducted supervised "food challenges" with infants diagnosed with a peanut allergy. These challenges involved giving the babies increasing amounts of peanut protein, with the intention of confirming whether they indeed had an allergy.

The team found that more than 1 in 3 infants who underwent the food challenge tolerated the peanut protein, meaning they didn't experience symptoms of an allergic reaction.

"This rate is much higher than we thought," study co-author Dr. Timothy Brettig, a pediatric allergist at the National Allergy Centre of Excellence in Australia, told Live Science.

Between April 2024 and October 2025, Brettig and colleagues retroactively reviewed the medical records of 1,275 infants who had been referred to Australian public hospitals after having a reaction to peanuts before their first birthday. These babies had first been assessed by allergy specialists at a median of 10 months of age.

For their initial diagnosis, the babies had to have a history of an allergic reaction to peanuts and show evidence of peanut sensitization on an allergy test. These tests included a skin-prick test, in which peanut extract is placed on the skin before it's lightly pricked, to check for a local reaction; and blood tests that detect antibodies against peanut proteins.

"Children under 1 year of age are an interesting group, as their immune system is still in rapid development and existing allergy tests may not reflect their allergic status well," said Dr. Alexandra Santos, a professor of pediatric allergy at King's College London who was not involved in the study. While these tests can hint that a child is sensitized to certain foods, they don't necessarily confirm that a baby will have an allergic reaction upon eating said food; the tests can also give false positives.

Of the 1,275 infants, 778 later underwent a food challenge roughly six months after their first recorded allergic reaction; at that point, their median age was 13 months old. They were given up to seven gradually increasing doses of peanut protein, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams. These doses were given at 15-minute intervals while medical staff monitored the children for signs of an allergic reaction.

Nearly 35% of the participants — 271 kids — completed the challenge without reacting.

"This is an excellent article," said Dr. Drew Bird, a pediatric allergist at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center who was not involved in the study. "The take-home message is to strongly consider a supervised oral food challenge when an infant's peanut allergy diagnosis is uncertain."

Bird emphasized that these challenges should ideally be done before a child starts oral immunotherapy, a treatment in which patients regularly consume carefully measured amounts of the food they are allergic to. The dose is gradually increased under medical supervision, with the goal of helping the patient tolerate greater amounts of the allergen and reducing the risk of reactions from accidental exposure.

An incorrect diagnosis can prompt families to unnecessarily avoid exposing their children to peanuts.

The starting dose is often determined through a similar oral food challenge to the one used in this study, though. So in theory, that challenge would help reveal whether the child needs treatment in the first place.

Bird cautioned that food challenges should be performed by a board-certified allergist-immunologist and an experienced allergy team in a setting equipped to treat anaphylaxis — a severe, potentially life-threatening allergic reaction.

In this study, 507 infants reacted during the food challenge, and of those, 61 developed anaphylaxis and were treated with epinephrine (the hormone in EpiPens).

"In an ideal world, with infinite resources," Santos said, "all patients except for those with a history of severe reactions would have a food challenge for diagnosis."

Because that is not the case, the families of infants whose allergy diagnosis is uncertain may be advised to avoid peanut products while they await a formal food challenge, she noted. But in the interim, they may miss an opportunity to help the child develop tolerance and, in turn, increase the risk that their allergy will persist.

Food challenges should be prioritized for infants whose allergy diagnoses are uncertain, she said. This would include babies who have low levels of peanut-specific antibodies in their blood or who show only small welts on skin-prick tests.

Luke Cuddy, a philosophy professor at Southwestern College who has edited books about The Legend of Zelda, offers the “Ship of Theseus” thought experiment as a way to think through the conundrum. International studio Linehouse has completed the interiors of the Jen hotel in Hangzhou, China, which features details that nod to local craft and the area’s industrial heritage.

The hotel, which is owned by the Shangri-La Hotel Group, is located along Hangzhou’s Dongxin River in an urban setting and flanked by two former industrial factory compounds.

The 18-floor hotel building was designed by US architecture firm Kohn Pedersen Fox, using steel, grey brick and glass to reference the site’s industrial past.

The same design approach was extended into the interior by Linehouse, which focuses on showcasing local craft and nods to the industrial elements of the former factories.

“Jen Hangzhou is an urban hotel that celebrates its neighbourhood context – one that layers history, craft, culture and rich materiality into a new perspective on Hangzhou,” Linehouse said.

The studio used earth-toned marble, oak panelling and relaxed furniture in the ground-floor lobby to create a calm and warm atmosphere.

At the entrance, guests are greeted by a textured artwork by artist Elsa Jeandedieu that depicts an abstract version of the bridges and canal close to the hotel.

Patchwork patterns on the marble floor and on a bespoke rug in the lobby were informed by the street layout and tile patterns of the surrounding neighbourhood.

All the hospitality spaces, including a restaurant, a bar and function rooms are located on the sixth floor.

The long, linear OpenHouse restaurant is wrapped by full-height glazing that invites natural light inside.

A timber design feature spans the ceiling and was informed by the craft of sunmao, a traditional timber joinery technique. It is highlighted by a thin black metal frame that supports suspended lighting.

At the centre of the restaurant, a coffee bar splits the space into a dining area on one side and an all-day lounge on the other.

In the private dining room, the timber canopy has an angled design and is decorated with burnt red and yellow hues. In the rest of the OpenHouse restaurant, rooms feature teal tiles and handmade ceramic wall lighting.

The adjacent Koan Bar is a triple-height venue characterised by an undulating black metal staircase that takes guests to a terrace on the upper floor, with incorporated platforms where guests can socialise.

The sinuous staircase is carved between walls lined with concrete panels, another nod to the site’s industrial heritage.

Beneath it, curved booths in walnut timber and tan leather provide intimate seating spaces.

Three interconnected spaces on the sixth floor, enveloped by undulating timber walls, will be used for events and intimate social gatherings. Like in the lobby, bespoke carpets feature patterns abstracted from urban details in the neighbourhood.

The seventh floor is dedicated to wellness and comprises a gym, a yoga room, a spa, a pool and changing rooms, made from tactile materials with muted tones to create a calm environment.

At the gym, a lava stone wall has engravings that illustrate movement, while the yoga room features hand-chiseled timber panels.

A sloped skylit annex accommodates the swimming pool.

The 176 guest rooms were conceived as “urban retreats”. Here, Linehouse used a restrained palette of gun metal, oak datum, textured render and tan leather to underline the industrial aesthetic.

“The public areas explore traditional craft techniques such as sunmao timber joinery, clay tiling and bricks, natural patterns, textures and renders, whilst the guestrooms explore a refined industrial aesthetic,” Linehouse said.

“Red brick, concrete, metal and bold forms reference the surrounding industrial environment whilst handcrafted tiles, vintage timber, colours and metals with different patinas introduce warmth, richness and texture.”

The guest rooms have curved ceilings that create indirect lighting and add softness to the space, while the bathrooms are illuminated by glass-block walls.

Founded by Alex Mok and Briar Hickling in 2013, Linehouse is an architecture and interior practice with studios in Shanghai, Hong Kong and Auckland.

It has recently designed Ink Ink Market in Shanghai with a translucent sloping facade, as well as the interiors for Bangkok’s Central Park shopping centre with three dramatic atriums, which was shortlisted of Retail Interior (large) by this year’s Dezeen Awards.
