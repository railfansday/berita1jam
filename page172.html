Cigna offered investors additional color on its new healthcare transformation strategy on Thursday morning.

First unveiled at the company's investor day on Sept. 30, the Lead to One initiative aims to bring together the array of assets under the Cigna Group's umbrella—from medical and pharmacy benefits to behavioral health to specialty care—to address the individual needs of members and patients.

CEO Brian Evanko said during Cigna's earnings call that the program aims to lean on data analytics and technology to identify risks more quickly and deliver personalized supports. The goal, he said, is to drive better outcomes while lowering healthcare costs in tandem.

"Our Lead to One vision will benefit all customers, and we see a particular opportunity with the most clinically complex patient needs, where healthcare costs are increasingly concentrated and represent over 50% of total healthcare spending," he said.

Evanko said that the approach helps to establish "clear strategic growth framework for creating long-term value."

"It is an exciting time here at The Cigna Group as our company unifies around a single vision, Lead to One, and begins to compound the distinctive capabilities we have developed to capture the opportunity set in front of us," he said.

In addition, the company is undertaking an initiative called Accelerate to One, which aims to support operational efficiency for its workforce while supporting greater accountability and better decision-making. Evanko said that program is set to drive $3 billion in run rate savings for the company by 2030.

Evanko said the company expects to see those savings opportunities materialize across the company.

Cigna is the latest health insurance company to boost its guidance for the year on the back of its Q3 results.

The company released its earnings report Thursday morning, posting $1.4 billion in profit. That's down year over year from the $1.9 billion haul the company reported in the third quarter of 2025.

In addition, revenues for the quarter were $73.7 billion, up from $69.7 billion in the prior-year quarter. Both figures surpassed Wall Street analysts' predictions, per Zacks Investment Research.

Through the first three quarters of the year, the company has earned $4.8 billion in profit, on par with the $4.7 billion posted for the first nine months of 2025. Total revenue at the end of Q3 was $213.8 billion, growing from $202.4 billion through three quarters in 2025.

CEO Brian Evanko said in the release that the company's current financial strategy is rooted in three key principles, including "driving core growth, leveraging our distinct capabilities and executing with discipline."

"As we put our strategy in action, our strong third quarter results demonstrate how we are building on this momentum by making health care more affordable and personalized, while advancing Accelerate to One to drive productivity, reduce costs, and support sustained long-term earnings growth," he said.

The company reported a medical loss ratio in the quarter of 85%, up slightly from 84.8% in Q3 2025. Cigna attributed the uptick to higher costs in its employer plans, while lower costs in the individual market segment did partially offset this trend.

Cigna Healthcare, the company's core insurance business, reported 18.45 million members as of Q3. By comparison, the insurer had 18.1 million members at the end of 2025.

Pharmacy benefit membership, meanwhile, was 117.7 million, down 123.6 million as of Dec. 31, 2025. Cigna said the decrease reflects "expected client transitions" as well as lower membership at its health plan clients.

Its Evernorth unit reported $63.8 billion in adjusted revenue for the quarter, up from $61.5 billion a year ago. Highlights include ongoing organic growth in its specialty pharmacy business, the company said.

Cigna Healthcare reported $11.7 billion in adjusted revenue for Q3, up from $10.7 billion in the prior-year quarter. The company said this largely reflects higher premium revenue as it adapts to higher medical costs.

On the back of the results, Cigna elevated its outlook and now expects at least $30.60 in earnings per share for 2026. It also projects a full-year MLR of 84.2%.

Browser Location Access: What It Actually Shares and How to Control It

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When a website asks "Allow location access?" — most users either click Allow reflexively or Block without fully understanding what they're granting. Here's what browser location access actually means, what extensions can do with it, and how to make deliberate choices.

The browser's Geolocation API lets websites request your location. When a site calls navigator.geolocation.getCurrentPosition(), the browser shows a permission prompt. If you grant it, the browser returns a latitude/longitude coordinate to the page.

On desktop: Not GPS. Desktop browsers use a combination of:

The accuracy on desktop varies from neighborhood-level (Wi-Fi triangulation) to city-level (IP-only). It's not your exact address.

On mobile: GPS is used if available, which is accurate to meters. Mobile location requests are much more precise than desktop.

When you grant location access to a site, the site receives:

That's it — no street address, no "home address," no place names. The website has to reverse geocode the coordinates to get a named location, which is an additional step they may or may not do.

Your IP address is always visible to sites, regardless of location permission. Many sites geolocate from IP before even requesting the Geolocation API.

Browser extensions are different from websites for location access. Extensions don't use the Geolocation API the same way — they have access to browser APIs that websites don't.

What extensions typically do with location: Most legitimate location-related extensions (weather, map, local search) request location to provide their core function — a weather extension needs your location to show weather. This is expected and appropriate.

Red flags: An extension that requests location access but whose stated purpose has nothing to do with location is worth scrutinizing.

Weather & Clock Dashboard for Firefox uses your location to show the weather for your area. The permissions requested are specifically for location and the ability to fetch weather data from an API (Open-Meteo).

The extension doesn't send your location to its own servers. It passes your coordinates to Open-Meteo (the weather data provider) to get forecast data. Open-Meteo is a public API with an open-data license; it doesn't have accounts or persistent tracking.

For users who don't want to share location even with the extension: you can enter a manual location by city name instead, and the extension will use that. No location permission required.

Firefox gives you granular control over location access:

Per-site management: Settings → Privacy & Security → Permissions → Location → Settings. This shows every site that has asked for location access and what you answered. You can change individual site permissions here without affecting others.

Default behavior: You can set Firefox to block all location requests by default, prompt case-by-case (the default), or allow all. Settings → Privacy & Security → Permissions → Location → check "Block new requests asking to access your location" to default to block.

Incognito/private windows: Location permissions granted in a private window don't persist. The site has to ask again next time.

Allow location for: mapping/navigation sites (Google Maps, OpenStreetMap), weather services, local search, delivery sites where location saves you typing your address.

Consider blocking for: news sites, social media platforms, general-purpose shopping sites, sites where your precise location has no clear functional use.

Always review when: a site you don't recognize or haven't used before asks for location, or a site asks for location but you can't figure out why they'd need it.

The permission prompt is there for a reason. Using it deliberately — not reflexively clicking Allow or Block — is the main privacy practice worth building.

Firefox's default geolocation provider is different from Chrome's. Firefox defaults to routing geolocation queries through Mozilla's geolocation service (which uses Google Location Services under the hood but via Mozilla), which provides some additional privacy layer compared to Chrome's direct Google use.

For higher privacy: about:config → geo.provider.network.url can be changed to alternative providers, or geolocation can be disabled entirely by setting geo.enabled to false. This disables the API completely — all location requests will appear to fail to sites.

The practical privacy tradeoff: disabling geolocation entirely means weather extensions, map sites, and local search can't use your location, which is often the reason you're visiting them. The per-site permission model is a better balance for most users than a global block.

Starting from Abbottabad: What Your Hometown Decides — and What It Doesn't

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I am from Abbottabad, Pakistan. It is a small city in the hills, and it is home. Everything I have built — Buzz Capitol, Digi Skill Pro Academy, findservicenest.com, more than fifteen YouTube channels, six books — started there. People sometimes ask me whether starting from a small town held me back. The honest answer is: it decided some things, and it did not decide others. Knowing the difference matters more than the town itself.

Your hometown decides your starting point. It decides what is around you when you are young: the schools, the people you meet, the examples of what a working life can look like. In a small city, you see fewer big companies and fewer obvious paths into media or technology. That is real, and I will not pretend it is not. When I started, there was no local roadmap for what I wanted to do. I had to find the road myself, mostly online.

But your hometown does not decide your reach. The internet changed that equation completely, and it changed it for everyone at once. A student in Abbottabad can take the same free class as a student anywhere else. A reader in another country does not know or care where an article was written. A client hiring a service does not ask for your address before asking whether you can do the job. The work travels; the writer stays put.

That is the first thing my hometown taught me: place is a starting point, not a limit. It taught me to be resourceful, because when the thing you need is not nearby, you learn to find it online, to teach yourself, to ask strangers for help and to help strangers in return. Some of the most useful skills I have came from that habit — looking for answers instead of waiting for them to arrive.

The second thing it taught me is patience. In a small city, things move at their own pace. People know each other. Reputation travels by word of mouth, and it travels slowly — but it also lasts. That shaped how I think about building anything. I did not chase a quick splash. I built steadily, in my own name, one article and one video and one class at a time, because that is how trust works where I come from: you show up, you do the work, and over time people believe you.

There is a part of this story I want to be careful about. I am not saying that where you are from does not matter at all. It matters. It shapes your accent, your manners, your sense of what is possible, and the problems you understand first-hand. I understand what it means to start with limited resources, because I lived it. That understanding is part of why I made Digi Skill Pro Academy's classes completely free. In 2025, more than ten thousand students joined, and more than five thousand of them reported that they started earning. I do not claim those numbers as a triumph of mine; I mention them because they are the reason the academy exists — to give people starting from places like mine a way in.

Starting from Abbottabad also taught me something about independence. When you are far from the centres where decisions get made, you learn to make your own decisions. Buzz Capitol is an independent news and media platform because I wanted it to be free to say what it thinks. That independence was not a strategy I read in a book. It was a habit I learned from building things on my own terms, in a place where nobody was going to build them for me.

I still live and work from here, and I do not see that as a compromise. The world comes to my work through a screen, and my work goes out to the world the same way. My channels reach more than ten million followers across platforms. My books reach readers I will never meet. None of that required me to leave. It required me to stay, and to build something worth finding.

If you are starting from a small town, here is what I would say. Do not waste energy resenting your starting point. Use it. The constraints of a small place teach you to be self-reliant, and self-reliance is the most portable skill there is. Start with what you can do today, with the connection you have. Publish your work under your own name. Let the internet do what it is good at: carrying good work further than its author's address.

Your hometown decides where you begin. It does not decide how far your work can travel. That part is yours.

Sami Ullah is the Founder, CEO and Editor-in-Chief of Buzz Capitol, Chairman of Digi Skill Pro Academy, and author of six books. He lives and works in Abbottabad, Pakistan.

Illegal gambling ring busted after using Pokemon cards as payouts

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Police in Texas have busted an alleged illegal gambling operation that was using Pokemon and sports trading cards to pay out winnings instead of cash.

Pokemon cards have become increasingly valuable in recent years, with rare collectibles selling for thousands or even millions of dollars. However, authorities in San Antonio believe one group of businesses was taking advantage of their value for an entirely different reason.

According to police, customers who won money on gambling machines were instead handed valuable trading cards, which they could then exchange for cash at a nearby business.

The unusual arrangement came to light after officers raided seven locations on November 4, detaining 23 people and seizing hundreds of gambling machines.

Bexar County Sheriff Javier Salazar explained that the businesses, which operated under the name “777,” were allegedly attempting to get around Texas gambling laws.

Under state rules, certain gaming establishments are restricted in the value of prizes they can award. Rather than paying customers large sums directly, the businesses allegedly used Pokemon and sports cards as an alternative.

Winners could then take their cards to a neighboring store, which would purchase them for cash.

Speaking to the San Antonio Express-News, Salazar explained how customers could receive a card and sell it to collect their winnings.

“So here’s your sports cards that you won for gambling with us, you can go next door and sell this to them, and they’ll give you a couple of hundred dollars for it,” Salazar said. “We know what they’re doing, they’re trying to skirt the law, it doesn’t make it any less illegal.”

making room for nature: the urban landscapes of kongjian yu

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

what happens when urban landscapes are designed around ecological processes rather than human convenience alone? from restored waterways to constructed wetlands, these projects raise questions about biodiversity, interdependence, and whether cities can become shared habitats for multiple forms of life.

In Landscapes for Adaptation: Evidence from China, architect and Harvard professor Charles Waldheim examines 60 built experiments realized by Chinese landscape architect Kongjian Yu and his practice Turenscape over the past two decades. Analyzing their environmental performance, the book documents how landscape architecture can respond to flooding, climate volatility, and ecological change through implemented projects. These built experiments offer a starting point for exploring a broader question: can cities be designed as shared habitats for humans and other forms of life?

Yu, founder of Turenscape and a leading advocate of the sponge city concept, has spent decades arguing that urban development should work with natural processes. His approach uses landscape as infrastructure, allowing water, vegetation, and existing ecological systems to play an active role in addressing flooding, pollution, and habitat loss.

In Shanghai, Houtan Park demonstrates how landscape architecture can make ecological processes part of the urban fabric. Designed by Turenscape and completed in 2010 for the Shanghai World Expo, the 14-hectare park occupies a former industrial site along the Huangpu River. Once used by a steel factory and shipyard, the land was heavily degraded, while the adjacent river was severely polluted.

The design builds on the industrial past of the site, transforming its existing landscape into a new ecological and public space. A constructed wetland runs through the park, using a sequence of terraces, cascades, and wetland plants to treat polluted river water. The system was designed to treat approximately 2,400 cubic meters of water per day, improving its quality from Lower Grade V to Grade III for non-potable uses. The wetland also forms part of the flood-management strategy of the park, while its planted river edge creates conditions for aquatic and bird habitats. Reclaimed industrial materials, agricultural terraces, and a network of paths connect these ecological functions with the public experience of the park.

Water treatment, flood protection, habitat creation, and public access operate within the same landscape. Houtan Park makes infrastructure visible, allowing visitors to encounter environmental processes that are usually hidden behind concrete walls and underground networks.

A related approach appears in Yichang Yunhe Park in Hubei province. Designed by Turenscape and completed in 2013, the 12-hectare project transforms twelve polluted, abandoned fish ponds into what the practice describes as a ‘green sponge’. The design uses the existing water bodies as the foundation for a system that cleanses polluted canal water and remediates contaminated stormwater. The project relies on regenerative design and minimal intervention. The former ponds become part of a wider landscape of vegetation and water, while paths and elevated walkways allow people to move through the site. Its significance lies partly in the decision to work with an existing, damaged landscape.

In Ningbo, the Yinzhou Central River Transformation explores the potential of restoring an urban waterway. Completed in 2010, the project introduces wetland planting along the riverbanks, alongside walkways and areas for public use. The intervention softens the engineered edges of the river, retaining its role as part of the city’s water infrastructure.

These are some examples of Turenscape’s projects that suggest that ecological urbanism does not necessarily depend on introducing untouched nature into the city, but it can also involve reconsidering infrastructure that already exists: drainage channels, abandoned ponds, industrial waterfronts, and flood-control systems. Such sites become opportunities to restore ecological functions, changing how people encounter water and landscape. However, creating landscapes that manage water and support vegetation is not necessarily the same as designing cities around the needs of other forms of life. In Yu’s projects, wetlands and planted riverbanks help reduce flooding, improve water quality, and provide habitats. Their value also depends on whether other species can thrive there, how ecological systems develop over time, and how well these landscapes are maintained.

In her 2016 book Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene, feminist theorist Donna Haraway challenges the idea that humans exist separately from the rest of the living world, describing life as a network of interdependencies in which organisms and environments shape one another. Her concept of sympoiesis, or making-with, offers a way to understand the world as something produced collectively rather than controlled by a single species.

Seen through Haraway’s ideas, Yu’s projects can be understood as more than infrastructure for managing water and flooding. The wetlands and river corridors also create environments where plants, insects, birds, microorganisms, and aquatic species interact. This shifts the focus from what these landscapes provide for people to the wider ecological relationships they support and how these relationships shape urban life.

This does not mean that ecological infrastructure automatically produces a more-than-human city. A landscape can support biodiversity while remaining primarily designed around human priorities. Its ecological value depends on factors such as long-term maintenance, water quality, habitat connectivity, and whether different species can establish and survive. The presence of vegetation or the appearance of a natural landscape is not, on its own, evidence of a functioning ecosystem. There are also social questions to consider. Green infrastructure can improve urban environments, but it does not necessarily address unequal access to public space, the displacement of existing communities, or the uneven distribution of environmental benefits.

Ecological restoration can become part of wider redevelopment processes, sometimes increasing land values and changing who can afford to live nearby. Assessing these projects therefore requires looking beyond their environmental performance to consider how they affect both local communities and other forms of life.

The projects documented in Waldheim’s Landscapes for Adaptation: Evidence from China demonstrate how landscape architecture can contribute to climate adaptation through built, functioning systems. Their significance extends beyond the technical integration of water management and public space. They suggest that cities can be understood as environments shaped by relationships between human activity and ecological processes.

Yu’s work offers a practical foundation for this shift, while Haraway’s thinking invites us to take its implications further. If cities are understood as shared environments, their success cannot be judged solely by how efficiently they protect people from environmental change. It must also be considered in terms of the relationships they allow to develop between different forms of life. The more-than-human city is not necessarily a finished ecological utopia, but an ongoing process of learning how to live alongside the many species and systems that already make urban life possible.

SERP data providers say Google throttles goto links per results page, so some rank data may store Google redirect links in place of page URLs.

Google is restricting the speed at which anyone can follow (google.com/goto) redirects for search results on each page, as per DataForSEO and Serper. Both sell Google search result API access. Due to this restriction rank data provided by these services may contain Google redirect URLs instead of actual URLs.

According to DataForSEO, the restrictions mean that they have seen some of their request modes only resolving part of the destination URLs. Serper’s status page first noted the issue at 1:30 a.m. UTC on October 30. SerpApi, another seller of search data, also logged goto links for organic results on the same date. Neither DataForSEO nor Serper links to a Google announcement regarding the restriction and the statements below are from their websites.

DataForSEO stated in its November 2nd article that when you open multiple links from the same results page in rapid succession, Google will begin to throttle the redirect(s). Each link carries an identifier based on the results page it originated from.

When Google throttles your redirects, instead of sending your requests for resolution directly to the destination, Google will send back a page with a “Continue” button. That button will only become available after a waiting period. The amount of time the continue button is disabled grows the faster the previous links were resolved.

For example, take a results page that contains 18 goto links to other websites. If all 18 links are requested in quick succession, Google may allow the first 5-6 to be resolved immediately. However, the 7th link may result in a 1 second wait before being able to access the website. The 8th link may then take 3 seconds. And this process continues until the 15th link, where there could be a 40 second wait before accessing that website. DataForSEO reported that they had instances of waiting up to 120 seconds for 1 URL.

The limit is based on the results page and not based on the IP address or the search query used. Also, DataForSEO reports that simply switching IP addresses or search queries will not help bypass this limitation. Additionally, it says that even if you wait an hour or longer before resolving all of the links, Google still uses the same results page limits.

DataForSEO’s Standard Priority mode now resolves all goto URLs, although the added delays may mean execution takes longer. In High Priority and Live modes, the company says about half of goto URLs are resolved.

Serper’s status page says Google “limits how many /goto result links can be followed per results page.” Its first fix resolved the top three organic links and returned results four through 10 as goto links, which Serper said work about 30 seconds after the search. It added a /resolve endpoint to fetch the rest.

On Oct. 31, Serper said it had found another way to get the real URLs and that all result links had them again. It called that “likely temporary.”

SerpApi’s Status Page displays an October 30th event indicating that its Google Search API returned goto links instead of regular links for organic search results; it states that they had the issue fixed by November 2nd. However, there is a second incident reported on November 3rd reporting intermittent issues with the same problem. Neither incident gives a cause.

DataForSEO dates Google’s move to www.google.com/goto links in Search Results to late August. It says the links cannot be decoded, and therefore a tool will have to follow each link to find out where it goes. The link-following part is what the new limit slows down.

Some rank tracking services as well as in house reports using landing pages for URL-level reporting may display Google goto redirects (google.com/goto) in place of true destination URLs from approximately October 30th onward based upon the service provider and how the requests were made. If a specific URL is missing from a URL-level report it could still be showing up in a ranking position and have an unresolved redirect at the URL location. Prior to reporting a URL-level loss check whether the stored URL is a Google redirect, also contact your service provider and determine what method was used to collect the data.

As of my check on Nov. 4, SerpApi’s Incident Report on goto links from Nov. 3 is still listed as Identified. Serper states that in the event that Google also puts a cap on the method it uses for retrieving actual URLs, it would revert to automatically resolving the top three results and use /resolve to fetch the remaining after approximately thirty seconds. DataForSEO stated it will continue to adjust its processing based on what it observes regarding how Google deals with goto resolutions.

Reports covering the last few days of October through early November should be reviewed again at some point when providers have determined how they will handle goto resolution.
