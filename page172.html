The AI Detours That You Shouldn’t Be Paying For

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s not that frontier AI companies wouldn’t necessarily put their thumb on the scales when selling us tokens, if they could get away with it – after all, Anthropic ramped up an effective 30% price increase in June; DeepSeek raised API pricing in August; and OpenAI reluctantly walked back a downgrade in its mid-tier token allowance at the end of September. So a trend towards austerity is now pretty clear.

Indeed, that thought tends to recur when your AI agent burns through your daily token allowance in a 45-minute blaze of not-necessarily-glorious code, and tells you to come back in 3-4 hours. In such a frustrating moment, one can’t help but wonder if some of the machinations that go into that activity are weighted in the providers’ favor. After all, it’s a good way to get us to cough up for a special top-up of tokens – or even to get us to turn on ‘automatic reload’.

Well, if that ever is truly the case, in this period, the vanguard companies can very credibly hide behind the assumption that we don’t completely understand how tokens get used in agentic workloads, across models and harnesses. And neither do they. Except for their payment induction pipelines (which are very smooth indeed), it’s all pre-alpha stuff, and remains fodder for bleeding-edge research, despite the widespread commoditization of the product.

The fact that AI agents do waste tokens (and therefore waste your money) when reasoning in agentic workflows is already well-established: exactly a year ago I wrote of new research indicating the extent to which reasoning AI models proved disposed to burn tokens through ‘rambling’; earlier this year, of further research indicating chain-of-thought reasoning as ‘decorative’; and last week, Tsuga’s Head of Product Nils Bunge, in a thought-leadership piece here, declared:

‘A team that consumes twice as many tokens is not necessarily creating twice as much value. It may be handling more difficult work, using an unnecessarily powerful model, or relying on an agent that repeatedly retries failed tasks.’

What is less known is the way in which tokens get thrown away on unnecessary tasks or processes; and this is the focus of a new paper that drew my attention back to this topic today.

Titled How Do Ai Agents Waste Your Money?, this new US academic collaboration* offers a unified framework capable of quantifying how wasteful of tokens agentic processes actually are. In cases where the models are closed-source, a ‘shadow’ methodology combines with providers’ official stats and metrics to give equivalent blow-by-blows on what happened to the tokens while the AI was reasoning and acting:

The new method for measuring wasted AI tokens. Each agent’s reasoning, actions and costs are recorded, then examined for repetition, unnecessary requests and avoidable detours. Identified waste is removed, and the agent’s ability to complete the original task is tested again. Source

As shown above, the agent is allowed to work normally, intercepting and recording communications with the AI provider, including instances of reasoning, use of tools, token consumption and costs. The records are then examined by Large Language Model (LLM) judges to separate useful work from repetition, unnecessary searches and detours. After this, suspected waste is removed, the token savings calculated, and the agent’s work resumed to see whether the task can still be completed successfully.

In tests, the researchers found that around 18% of agents’ output tokens were wasted. In more than 45% of tasks, one agent wasted more tokens than another needed to complete the same job successfully. They further found that removing 41–46% of unnecessary reasoning had very little effect on performance, suggesting that substantial savings are possible without significantly compromising results.

‘Across the collected trajectories, roughly 18% of output tokens are identified as waste.

‘Strikingly, for more than 45% of tasks, the wasted output of one agent alone exceeds the entire output of another agent that successfully solves the same task.’

Additionally, the study found that agents wasted the most resources when navigating unfamiliar tools and pursuing approaches that ultimately led nowhere; and that increasing reasoning effort fails to deliver consistent improvements: at maximum effort, Kimi-K3 was found to generate 6.6 times more reasoning tokens than at low effort, without a corresponding guarantee of better results.

Test results showing how Kimi-K3 performs at four reasoning-effort levels across five benchmarks. Higher reasoning effort did not consistently improve success rates, and maximum effort sometimes produced worse results than lower settings.

By analogy, rather than driving a powerful car that one never really pushes to its capabilities, these agentic sessions are more like driving a car that will accelerate to its maximum capable speed on every trip, even when there is no benefit to it, and in spite of the possible negative implications.

The paper also examines the role that different harnesses share in creating token waste, finding that the same AI model, Kimi-K3, could generate more than four times as much reasoning on identical tasks, depending on the harness used, without necessarily achieving better results.

Some waste was traced to basic software faults, with OpenCode, Kimi Code CLI, and OpenClaw found to repeatedly launch tasks in the wrong Python environment, causing errors and token-burning retries. Kimi Code CLI also allowed the AI to request timeouts that its own software would reject, forcing yet more costly attempts.

Counterintuitively, harnesses offering more tools were not necessarily more wasteful, and some recurring inefficiencies appeared to originate in the models themselves of which harness controlled them.

‘[Several] seemingly obvious harness properties do not consistently explain the observed differences in waste. For instance, as shown [below], harnesses that expose more tools are not necessarily more wasteful. OpenClaw exposes many more tools than the other harnesses, yet exhibits only moderate waste. Moreover, not all waste changes with the harness.

‘[Below right] demonstrates that on SWE-bench Verified, there is a recurring detour in which Kimi-K3 writes out what it believes to be the project’s original fix and later abandons it. ‘

Test results comparing wasted tokens across AI agent harnesses. Left: offering more tools does not necessarily increase waste. Right: Kimi-K3 repeatedly attempts and abandons a supposed original software fix, regardless of harness, but rarely does so on a different type of task.

By way of remediation, the authors suggest that agent harnesses should recognize when environmental restrictions make further progress impossible, communicate those limitations clearly to the AI model, and stop execution rather than allowing repeated failed attempts to consume more tokens. They also propose using their measurements of wasted tokens to improve model training, reasoning strategies and harness design.

To reconstruct an AI agent’s activity, the researchers tracked each exchange between the model and the software controlling it, recording its reasoning, responses, tool requests and token costs – with the level of detail varying by provider: Kimi-K3 reveals its entire reasoning process; Gemini 3.1 Pro only summaries; and Claude exposes none.

Each tool’s results were fed back into the agent, allowing the researchers to follow the process through to completion, or until its operating limit was reached.

Four examples of how AI agents spend tokens. Repeating known information, making identical searches, and adding pointless apologies are counted as waste, while an unsuccessful search is not necessarily wasteful, since the agent may have had good reason to try it.

The agents’ requests were routed through a proxy to track their activity, with only minor code changes required, and each request tagged with its benchmark, run, task and trial (except OpenClaw, which doesn’t support such tags – in that case, a separate proxy – built on LiteLLM – was used for each test).

The study defines token waste through four recurrent patterns: Restate (repeating known information); Duplicate-Fetch (repeating unnecessary tool requests); Padding (adding irrelevant text); and Detour (pursuing plausible but ultimately unproductive approaches).

Each recorded session was examined by an LLM-as-a-Judge (LLMaJ**), which was given the original task, the complete record and criteria for identifying all four waste patterns. Suspected waste was marked and removed, and the remaining tokens recounted to calculate the wasted proportion and cost. The counts were then checked against each provider’s own tokenizers or counting tools and reported output usage (with only minor formatting differences reported).

Models tested for the study comprised Kimi-K3; Gemini 3.1 Pro; GPT-5.6 Terra; GPT-6 Astra; Claude Sonnet 5; and Claude Fable 5.1, tested over the benchmarks τ²-bench (airline and retail customer service); SWE-bench Verified (software engineering); Claw-Eval; and MCP-Atlas (general-purpose tool use).

Additionally, Kimi-K3 was tested at different reasoning levels, and five agent harnesses compared: OpenCode; OpenClaw; OpenHands; Hermes; and Kimi Code CLI – with these latter comparisons used to assess how models, tasks, reasoning effort and harness design affect token waste (and with suspected waste removed in further tests to check its effect on performance).

To establish how much money was wasted by each model, the cost of unnecessary text and tool calls was compared against total output costs, averaging the results across the four benchmarks. Internal reasoning was assessed separately for Kimi-K3 – the only model that exposed its complete reasoning process:

Test results comparing wasted output costs across six AI models, averaged over four benchmarks. Left: overall waste rates, with Kimi-K3’s internal reasoning shown separately. Right: waste rates for successful and failed tasks, with failures generally producing more waste.

As shown above, between 11% and 24.9% of the money spent generating answers and using tools was judged to have been wasted, with GPT-6 Astra performing best and Claude Sonnet 5 worst. For Kimi-K3, around one in six tokens was wasted on answers and tool use, rising to more than one in four during its internal reasoning.

More waste was generally found when tasks failed, though the reverse was true for Kimi-K3’s reasoning; and there was no clear evidence that spending more tokens on unnecessary work improved the chances of success.

In another test, an attempt was made to cut wasted tokens while the AI was still working: at each step, an AI judge** was used to remove repeated information and pointless text from the model’s reasoning, before the task was allowed to continue:

Test results showing how removing unnecessary AI reasoning affected success rates on two benchmarks. Removing 41-46% of reasoning had little effect on performance, while removing all reasoning caused substantially more failures.

On SWE-bench Verified, 41% of reasoning was cut away, with no loss of performance, with the success rate actually rising slightly, from 89% to 89.5%. On MCP-Atlas, 46% was removed, with success falling only modestly, from 65% to 62.3%.

In other words, almost half the reasoning could be discarded while the AI was working, without seriously harming its ability to finish the job.

Finally†, Kimi-K3 was tested on identical tasks using different agent harnesses. Here, reasoning output varied more than fourfold, without any consistent improvement in performance, as shown below:

Test results comparing Kimi-K3’s token consumption across agent harnesses on two benchmarks. Internal reasoning is shown in blue, and responses and tool calls in orange. On coding tasks, more than four times as much reasoning was generated with Mini-SWE as with Hermes.

Some waste was traced to basic software faults. OpenCode, Kimi Code CLI and OpenClaw launched coding tests in the wrong Python environment, causing errors and retries.

This is only a glance at a paper whose length and assiduous method may reward a deeper read.

The subject matter did bring to mind something that I am often conscious of: the sheer gulf between what we and the frontier suppliers actually understand about these processes, and the companies’ willing disposition to package the product as if it had just spent the last three months in a linter, and passed with flying colors.

As it stands, for the moment, for bad outcomes, we can currently blame no-one but ourselves, because we are buying what nearly all of us know to be a deeply experimental and unpredictable product.

Rule to Allow Crypto in 401(k)s Arrives at White House for Final Review

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A final DOL rule that would ease alternative assets such as private equity, real estate and cryptocurrency into 401(k) retirement plans arrived at the White House on Thursday for review, Bloomberg Law reported, a procedural step that usually precedes public release.

The Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs logged the package on Nov. 5 under the title “Fiduciary Duties In Selecting Designated Investment Alternatives,” RIN 1210-AC38. The filing lists the DOL’s Employee Benefits Security Administration as the authoring office, marks the stage as a final rule, flags it as economically significant and notes that there is no legal deadline for completion.

The DOL unveiled the proposal on March 30. In that draft, plan fiduciaries who follow a documented process when adding private assets to a 401(k) menu would get a legal safe harbor under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974.

The six factors named in the proposal are performance, fees, liquidity, valuation, performance benchmarks and complexity. Satisfying them does not force plans to offer crypto. It lowers the litigation risk for employers that decide they want to.

JUST IN: 🇺🇸 The White House is now reviewing a new rule which will allow Americans to invest in crypto through their 401(k) retirement plans 👀 pic.twitter.com/uXGGIXEzdC

President Donald Trump’s Aug. 7, 2025, executive order, “Democratizing Access to Alternative Assets for 401(k) Investors,” directed the Labor secretary to reexamine ERISA guidance and, where appropriate, propose rules with “appropriately calibrated safe harbors.” The order defined alternative assets to include private-market equity and debt, real estate, holdings in actively managed vehicles that invest in digital assets, commodities, infrastructure and lifetime-income strategies.

The March proposal implements that order. A DOL fact sheet said prudence under ERISA is process-based and that fiduciaries retain discretion to select any designated investment alternative if they follow the outlined review. Deputy Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling cast the approach as neutral: “The department’s days of picking winners and losers are over.”

That stance reverses a prior chill. In May 2025 the DOL rescinded its March 2022 compliance release that told fiduciaries to use “extreme care” before putting cryptocurrency on a 401(k) menu. The department said that phrase was not found in ERISA and restored a context-specific standard.

Later, it was reported that Sens. Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren pressed the DOL to abandon the crypto-friendly path. House Republicans have separately pushed to codify Trump’s crypto order in statute.

OIRA review under Executive Order 12866 is the last interagency checkpoint before a final rule can be published in the Federal Register. Bloomberg Law said the arrival signals the package is nearing completion. The same office cleared the proposed version in late March before the DOL published it; that earlier pass took roughly two months after the January submission, per prior Bloomberg coverage of the roughly $12 trillion 401(k) market.

What is not yet public is the final text itself. Whether the six-factor safe harbor survived comment intact, how digital-asset vehicles are described, and what effective date the DOL chose will only be clear when OIRA returns the rule and the department posts it. Comment volume on the proposal was heavy; industry and labor groups have argued opposite sides of the prudence standard for months.

For bitcoin, the stakes sit in plan menus rather than spot ETF flows. Trump’s August order helped frame digital assets as a retirement-access issue for more than 90 million defined-contribution savers. A finalized DOL safe harbor would not put bitcoin and crypto on every lineup overnight. It would give plan committees a clearer map if they want exposure through an asset-allocation sleeve that holds digital assets.

The test now is how fast OIRA clears the economically significant package — and whether the published final rule still gives fiduciaries the process cover the March draft promised, or whether the comment fight rewrote the safe harbor before the White House stamps it.

Brookfield Reports AI Factory Pipeline and $25B Quarterly Fundraising

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Brookfield Asset Management announced third-quarter results on November 6, 2026, reporting $25 billion of fundraising for the quarter ended September 30, 2026 alongside higher fee-related and distributable earnings. The release also detailed a global AI factory project pipeline, placing the AI buildout within a much broader capital-raising quarter.

Credit contributed $12 billion of the quarterly total and infrastructure $7 billion, so the fundraising spans Brookfield’s businesses rather than representing an AI-only raise. Year-to-date fundraising reached $123 billion. Fee-bearing capital, the committed, pledged or invested capital that entitles Brookfield to fee revenue, grew 13% year over year to $682 billion, driven largely by $157 billion raised in the twelve months ended September 30, 2026.

Over the year leading into the results, Brookfield’s AI infrastructure fund and infrastructure debt strategy received a combined $10 billion in commitments, a figure covering both strategies rather than the AI fund alone. The third-quarter supplemental lists the Brookfield Artificial Intelligence Infrastructure Fund as a 2025-vintage vehicle with $3.75 billion of fund capital and $553 million of unrealized investment value at quarter end. The closed-end fund is focused on developing AI infrastructure and designed around demand from hyperscalers, enterprises and governments for scalable, integrated solutions.

Brookfield reports AI factory projects in execution in the United States, Canada, Korea, France and the United Kingdom. That is an execution pipeline, not a statement that all the facilities are operating. During the third quarter, the company signed agreements to jointly develop, deliver and operate an AI factory in the United Kingdom and to invest up to $9 billion in next-generation AI computing infrastructure in Korea through partnerships with NAVER and NVIDIA.

Infrastructure deployment totaled $1.2 billion in the quarter, including $500 million of incremental funding for construction of a U.S. semiconductor fabrication facility. That deployed capital is distinct from commitments and signed development agreements.

Fee revenues increased 11% to $1.624 billion. Fee-related earnings, which Brookfield defines as fee revenues less the direct costs of earning them, rose 15% to $864 million from $754 million a year earlier. The fee-related earnings margin at Brookfield’s share was 54%, compared with 55% a year earlier.

Distributable earnings, a company-defined measure of earnings available for distribution to common shareholders or reinvestment, rose 15% to $763 million from $661 million. The distributable-earnings calculation included $48 million of realized carried interest net of costs from technology and private-equity investment monetizations.

Net income was approximately $3.0 billion and included a $2.0 billion non-cash gain from the acquisition and consolidation of Oaktree. That gain helps explain why bottom-line income substantially exceeded the fee-driven measures; it is an accounting effect of the transaction rather than additional quarterly fee revenue.

At quarter end, Brookfield had $148 billion of uncalled fund commitments across the company. Of that total, $68 billion was not yet earning fees; Brookfield estimates that deploying it would generate approximately $680 million of annual fees. Neither the uncalled balance nor the fee potential is AI-specific. The figures distinguish capital pledged to funds from capital already producing fee revenue.

That distinction is the central qualification to the fundraising momentum. Brookfield has a larger fee-bearing base, signed AI infrastructure agreements and projects in execution, but the additional fees tied to uncalled commitments depend on deployment. The next measurable stage is converting that committed capital into fee-earning investments while advancing the AI factory pipeline from agreements and execution to completed infrastructure.

Bowhunter Tags Massive 191-Inch Buck That’s Somehow Still in Velvet

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 6, 2026 10:00 AM EST

Jackson Berner was at work when one of his remote trail cameras captured a photo of a giant velvet buck the morning of October 20. The 21-year-old commercial insulator was on the job in southwest Ohio when he got the early morning picture of the deer near his hunting treestand.

“I said ‘Holy Smoke’ when I saw the size of the deer and its velvet,” Berner tells Outdoor Life. “The buck came by my camera at 7:50 a.m., and I’d never seen it before.”

Berner wanted to be in his treestand the following morning in case the buck happened to walk by the spot again.

“I asked my boss if I could have the next day off to hunt,” Berner says. “I showed him photos of the buck, and he said to go and get that deer.”

The following morning on Oct. 21, Berner was 22 feet up a tree sitting in a lock-on stand near where his trail camera had gotten photos of the velvet buck the previous morning.

“My brother-in-law told me he believed I was gonna shoot that buck,” explains Berner, of Springfield, Ohio. “I just had a feeling I’d get that deer, too.”

He climbed into his treestand just as it was nearing legal shooting light, a bit after 7 a.m.

“Legal shooting time was 7:22 a.m., and the velvet buck showed up at 7:30, like the previous day,” he says. “The velvet buck had been with a smaller buck on camera the first day I had photos of him. I first saw the little 4-point buck on the trail that morning, and not far behind him was the big buck.”

When the big deer got closer it stopped and looked up directly at the hunter.

“I froze, and when he dropped his head and walked a few steps, I drew my bow and took the shot at 15 yards.”

His 62-pound Darton bow sent an arrow tipped with a G5 Megameat 3-blade expandable broadhead. The arrow hit home and passed through.

“My adrenaline was on fire, as I watched him run out of sight about 60 yards away,” Berner says. “I decided to leave the woods and come back in an hour with some help to track my deer.”

Berner returned with his father Jason, cousin and brother-in-law. It was an easy-to-follow blood trail, and the party found the deer dead only 50 yards beyond from where Berner had lost sight of it that morning.

They loaded the deer into a Polaris side-by-side and then started field dressing the deer.

“We got to looking at him for gutting and thought perhaps it had no testicles, which is common for late-season velvet bucks,” Berner says. “But he had his man parts. The only thing we could figure is his testes had been injured somehow, and that resulted in him keeping his velvet so late in the year.”

Berner took his velvet buck to Toby Hughes, a popular Buckmasters scorer who measured the 16-pointer rack at 191 inches. Hughes says it’s one of the biggest velvet bucks ever taken in Ohio.

Berner delivered his deer to a meat processor, and the butcher told him he found a bullet in the deer’s hindquarters. Berner says the deer showed no signs of having been shot previously, and its hide was completely healed over.

“One other thing about that buck, is when I spotted it first on my trail camera, it was near where my fiance and I are planning to get married,” Berner says. “That’s pretty amazing.”

Inside Disney’s Animal Nutrition Center, which doles out 10,000 pounds of food every day

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 6, 2026 10:00 AM EST

Cases of peanut butter, bright red boxes of Shredded Wheat cereal, and so many varieties of tea that even Mary Poppins would be excited. Around the corner, large wooden crates teeming with wide leaves and sturdy grasses, and a walk-in refrigerator full of fresh produce sit ready to turn into dishes that are a feast for the eyes and the belly.

For the team working at Disney’s Animal Nutrition Center (ANC) at Walt Disney World Resort in Orlando, Florida, it’s like Thanksgiving every day. The roughly 40 cast members on the animal nutrition team is responsible for feeding the over 5,000 animals at Disney’s Animal Kingdom Theme Park and The Seas at EPCOT. It takes time, a devotion to the science of nutrition, and really knowing your audience to deliver about 10,000 pounds of food per day. And like putting together any big meal, it requires a team effort, which extends well beyond the animals at Disney.

“What we’re trying to do is provide the optimal nutrition for animals to live long and healthy lives, and we do that by taking information from a lot of different fields,” Animal Nutrition Operations Manager Dr. Heidi Bissell tells Popular Science. “From the field of agriculture, we learn a lot about animal physiology and how animals work, and can then design diets with the ingredients we have for specific purposes.”

In addition to bringing in knowledge from across different scientific disciplines, the team collaborates with other accredited zoos, aquariums, and universities to share research and data on animal nutrition. They’ve published studies on animals from bigger animals like zebras and rhinos, to smaller fish and amphibians, which is crucial to filling in nutritional gaps. If the team is not sure what a particular species needs to eat in order to thrive, they will turn to comparative nutrition for answers.

“We go with a close relative [and] take what we know about the animals we do know a lot about and apply that to some of our other species,” Bissell explains. “Then we learn from our own animals by assessing how they’re doing on the diets that we’re offering, so we can gauge their nutritional status and make adjustments as needed.”

Staying nimble and making these adjustments is also important. If a supply chain issue disrupts what the nutrition team can get, having a back-up plan that includes nutritious food is critical. If an animal is familiar and comfortable with at least a few types of food, they will generally fare better, whether there are shortages or not.

At the same time, the nutrition team works to ensure that the food and its presentation mimics life in the wild as much as possible.

“Animals in the wild are spending their days solving puzzles, trying to figure out how to eat, while staying away from predators or toxins,” Bissell says. “They’re making a lot of decisions all day, so if we just offered them food in a bowl that would not provide them with mental stimulation.”

One of the most common behaviors is foraging, or digging through obstacles like grass, mud, or leaves to find food. The dishes the team creates are often presented in a way that encourages foraging, so that the animals keep up those natural behaviors. Leaves will be hung up in a tree for the giraffes to eat as they would in the wild. Carnivores like lions will get whole prey and bones to eat, which also helps keep their own jaws and teeth healthy. The team also pays attention to seasonal shifts to keep animals in their natural rhythm.

“Probably the biggest group of animals that we do this with would be birds,” says Bissell. “If they’re laying eggs, they might need more calcium, so we might add sources of calcium into their diet.”

Surprisingly, feeding bigger animals like lions and elephants is not as challenging as making sure that the insectivores have enough to eat. True to their name, anteaters almost exclusively eat bugs, and can consume up to 3,000 insects per day. To keep up with all of those the bugs, Bissell and the team work side-by-side with manufacturers to ensure their insect pellets meet the physical and nutritional standards needed to align with insects the anteaters encounter in the wild. To supplement, the anteaters will also get a bit of produce with the insects, since they do also eat fruit.

“We do a lot with like ice cakes, where we can freeze food in blocks of ice and can color the water with like vegetable dyes, so it’s fun and engaging. And the ice, especially in Florida in the summer, is really nice for hydration,” Bissell explains. “We are often pulling from what’s already in the animal diet or similar things, because just like humans, it’s not a great idea to just add a bunch of stuff on top of your existing diet.”

They’ll also follow themes, from a little fall pies to a driver’s license out of fruit and leaves for a gorilla’s 16th birthday and a food sculpture shaped like a margarita for a 21st birthday. That devotion to keeping meals fresh and creative helps all animals, humans included.

“I like making animals’ lives better, and by extension people’s lives better, because we care about these animals so much,” says Bissell.

Binance Hackers Swap About $50 Million of Stolen XRP Mostly Into Bitcoin, Elliptic Says

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hackers tied to the record Binance theft have begun converting a large slice of the stolen XRP into bitcoin, blockchain analytics firm Elliptic said in an update dated through Friday, with about 42 million XRP — roughly $50 million — already swapped, mostly through THORChain.

Converted bitcoin from other stolen assets nearly doubled overnight to about 1,650 BTC, worth some $124 million, Elliptic wrote. Most of that jump came from the XRP route. About 192 million XRP, roughly $226 million, remained unswapped in addresses linked to the Binance theft.

The XRP was part of the Oct. 29 drain that Binance later put at $6.34 billion. Between 20:05 and 20:09 UTC that day, five hot wallets sent 233.46 million XRP to an attacker address. The FBI has attributed the campaign to North Korea’s TraderTraitor group. Binance has reopened withdrawals in stages and said it has frozen or intercepted about $2.85 billion while covering customer losses from its own funds.

The stolen XRP sat quiet at first. Early on Nov. 2, between 02:12 and 04:00 UTC, all about 233 million XRP moved into six new addresses, where it stayed until Nov. 5, Elliptic said. From the afternoon of Nov. 5, two of those six addresses began sending XRP on to dozens of new accounts that swapped it, mostly through THORChain into bitcoin.

A small amount of ether also went through THORChain into bitcoin that evening. The main bitcoin and ether holdings from the theft — the largest pools — have still not moved, Elliptic said. That pattern matches what the firm saw after the Bybit hack: turn freezable and mid-liquidity tokens into bitcoin first, then attempt cash-out later through OTC brokers.

The broader laundering trail is already vast. Since the Binance theft, Elliptic has counted more than 20,000 wallets on 16 blockchains, at least 36 services and at least 65,000 transactions — roughly one new wallet every 30 seconds and one transaction every 10 seconds. About $3.11 billion remains in addresses linked to the Binance theft, mostly as ether and bitcoin. A negligible amount has likely been cashed out so far.

Issuers can freeze many stablecoins. They cannot freeze XRP the same way, and they cannot freeze bitcoin. After Tether and Circle locked down large stablecoin tranches on Oct. 29, the attacker prioritized assets that sit outside that kill switch. Swapping XRP into bitcoin continues that logic: bitcoin is harder to claw back once it is fragmented across fresh addresses.

All of the stolen SOL, about $87 million at Elliptic’s prices, has already been swapped, bridged or mixed, with much of it ending as bitcoin. Most early Tron laundering — about $220 million in TRX — has also run, a flow Bitcoin Magazine noted when covering the quiet shutdown of the T3 Financial Crime Unit before the hack. Only small parts of the main ether and NEAR piles have moved.

Some converted bitcoin has gone into Wasabi-style coinjoins — about 33 BTC, or $2.5 million, since Nov. 1 — and about $1.1 million in ether and SOL entered the Privacy Cash pool before that service paused deposits. Elliptic said the speed points to conventional automation, not an AI agent making live decisions: fixed timing, set amounts, blind retries and new tactics introduced hours apart.

U.S. bank supervisors have already checked in with lenders after the Binance breach. For bitcoin markets, the nearer question is how much more of the remaining ~192 million XRP — and eventually the parked ether — gets turned into BTC before anyone tries to cash out.

The test now is whether THORChain operators and other bridges keep blocking the published attacker addresses fast enough to slow the XRP-to-bitcoin pipe, or whether the rest of that pile follows the same overnight path the first $50 million already took.

AWX OAuth Scopes and RBAC Both Decide What Automation Can Do

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AWX centralizes inventory, credentials, projects, and job execution. Its API can launch automation and change the objects that store credentials. OAuth tokens carry read and write scopes, while AWX role-based access control limits the resources those scopes can reach. Reviewing only the token scope, or only the role, leaves half of the authorization decision unexamined.

AWX's token documentation describes OAuth 2 access tokens and explains that read and write scopes provide a mask over the RBAC permission system. RBAC documentation describes delegation across organizations, inventories, projects, templates, and credentials.

A token with write scope is not automatically allowed to modify every object. A highly privileged role is still constrained by the token scope. The effective permission is the intersection. Test both dimensions with a non-production object before granting a production token.

Create one OAuth application or token per integration and record the owning organization, user, scopes, and last use. A deployment pipeline that launches a known template does not need permission to edit credentials or create new projects.

Review credentials attached to job templates. API access to a template may reveal its configuration or allow execution under a stored credential. The ability to launch automation can be as sensitive as direct access to the target system.

AWX administration documentation describes commands for revoking OAuth2 tokens for a user or clearing revoked tokens. Build revocation into offboarding and incident response. Confirm that revoked tokens fail and that refresh tokens cannot recreate access.

Rotate tokens after role changes. A user or service account that loses an organization role should not retain an unrelated long-lived token that can still reach another resource.

The count-only queries used sub_type=all, page 1, and page size 1. app="AWX" returned 9, title="AWX" returned 9,788, and http.body="AWX" returned 966,935.

Treat all three values as inventory leads, not an exposed-control-plane census. Compare them with owned domains, certificates, and AWX deployment records.

Map each token to the jobs it can launch, the inventories it can target, and the credentials those jobs can use. Review webhook and notification integrations that can trigger jobs without a human. Confirm that project updates cannot introduce an unreviewed playbook into a privileged template.

T3 Code: Control All Your AI Coding Agents From Your Phone

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at devpik.com

T3 Code lets you control the AI coding agents on your machine from your phone. It works with Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode and more, uses the subscriptions you already pay for, and it had 45,475 stars when we checked.

T3 Code calls itself an "agent harness control surface", which is a grand way of saying one thing: it is the remote control for the AI coding agents running on your machine. You open an app on your iPhone, your Android phone, the web or a desktop app, and you drive the agents that are already set up on your computer, from wherever you are.

It works with the subscriptions you already have. Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Grok Build, OpenCode, Google Antigravity and fx are all supported, and if a provider is installed and authenticated on your machine, T3 Code can control it. There is no new subscription to buy and no new model to pay for. The README asks the question itself, "Wait, what are you selling me?", and answers it in one word: "Nothing."

The project is written in TypeScript, licensed under MIT, and it had 45,475 stars when we checked on November 4, 2026, after gaining roughly 12,500 stars in the past week. The team says they built it because they wanted the best possible development experience with agents, inspired by the Codex desktop app, Conductor, Claude Desktop and Cursor Glass, but none of those met their bar for something performant, remote-ready and truly open. If the project ever goes the wrong direction, they want you to have everything you need to fork it and build the editor you want.

The practical appeal is consolidation. If you use Claude Code for one project, Codex for another and Cursor somewhere else, each normally has its own interface and its own way of being driven. T3 Code puts them behind one control surface, so you check on runs, steer agents and keep working from your phone instead of being chained to the machine the agents run on.

Setup follows the provider, not T3 Code. Install and authenticate at least one provider before use: Codex with the Codex CLI and codex login, Claude with Claude Code and claude auth login, Cursor with the Cursor CLI and agent login, Grok Build with grok login, OpenCode with opencode auth login, and fx with fx login on the machine running the T3 Code server. Antigravity is the exception: enable it in Settings, then use Install Antigravity and Sign in with Google, with no CLI required.

There is also a docs folder in the repository covering the details that matter once you are running: permission modes, keyboard shortcuts, project settings, appearance preferences, remote access from a phone or another machine, connecting agents over MCP, source control integrations, multiple accounts per provider, and running T3 Code as a background service. There is no docs site yet, so the repository docs are the documentation.

The fastest route is the install script, then the t3 command to start the server and open the local web app:

On Windows, the PowerShell equivalent is irm https://t3.codes/install.ps1 | iex. Once installed, t3 service install keeps the server running in the background, t3 update moves to a newer release, and t3 --help has the full reference. To try it once without installing anything, run npx t3@latest instead.

Prefer a desktop app? The latest release is on GitHub Releases, and the package managers are covered: winget install T3Tools.T3Code on Windows, scoop install extras/t3code, brew install --cask t3-code on macOS, a .deb for Debian and Ubuntu, t3code-bin or t3code-nightly-bin from the AUR on Arch, and a nixpkgs package that includes both the desktop app and the t3 command. The mobile apps are on the App Store and Google Play, and the web app lives at app.t3.codes.

The README is blunt about the project's age, and you should take it seriously: "We are very very early in this project. Expect bugs." A control surface for your coding agents sits in a privileged position, so running an early project here deserves more caution than installing a text tool. Keep it on a machine you can afford to babysit, and read the permission modes doc before you hand an agent a long leash. Supervising from a phone also makes it easy to approve work you have only skimmed, so read the actual changes at a desk before you merge: a text diff shows every line an agent rewrote, and the AI Code Detector flags the placeholder scaffolding and debug leftovers agents tend to leave behind.

Contributions are mostly closed for now. The team says small fixes may be considered, but big features will not be, so if you adopt T3 Code hoping to shape its roadmap, expect to wait. Building from source is also a heavier lift than usual: it uses Vite+ and needs the global vp command-line tool, with full instructions in the repository's contributing guide.

Your Churn Benchmark Is Lying to You in a Specific, Fixable Way

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Somewhere in your last board deck there is a churn benchmark: an industry average, sourced from a respectable report, against which your number is pronounced fine or alarming. This note is not an argument against benchmarks — it is an argument for reading the one you have with the suspicion it deserves, because churn benchmarks fail in a specific and predictable way: they average together businesses whose churn is made of entirely different stuff, and then invite you to compare your stuff to the average.

Churn decomposes into voluntary exits (decisions) and involuntary ones (payment accidents), and the ratio between them varies wildly by model. A B2B tool with annual contracts and corporate cards might lose almost nobody involuntarily; a consumer subscription on monthly billing with debit cards can lose more people to failed payments than to every competitor combined. When a benchmark blends those worlds into "media subscriptions average 6%," it has produced a number that describes no actual business, and comparing yourself to it can mislead in both directions — complacency when your voluntary churn is terrible but your involuntary is low, panic when the reverse holds.

Benchmarks also blend definitions: customer churn against revenue churn, monthly against annualized, trials counted or not. Before any comparison, force the two questions — which churn, computed how — and refuse to proceed until the benchmark answers them or admits it cannot. A surprising share cannot, at which point the number is decoration and should be treated as such in the deck.

Two things. First, existence proofs: a benchmark table broken out by industry and model can reveal a loss category you have not been counting at all — if businesses like yours routinely lose 2% a month to payment failure and your reports show no such line, the gap is in your accounting, not your performance. Second, orders of magnitude: knowing whether a problem is a 0.2% problem or a 3% problem in your model class decides whether it deserves a meeting. A benchmark set organized by industry, with the sources named and the definitions stated is built for exactly those two uses; treat its finer distinctions as hypotheses about your business, never as grades.

Your own history, decomposed. Last year's involuntary share against this year's, voluntary exits by stated reason over time, recovery rates by class — these comparisons survive scrutiny because the definitions are constant and the composition is yours. External benchmarks can start that conversation once a year. The internal series should be running it every month.

The most expensive benchmark error is not analytical at all; it is managerial. A leader reads that top-quartile businesses in the category hold churn under some line, sets that line as the team's target, and thereby commits the roadmap to a number whose composition nobody examined. If the line was achieved in businesses with a fraction of your involuntary share — different cards, different geographies, annual billing — the team will spend the year chasing voluntary-churn programs for what is substantially a payments problem, or vice versa, and will be judged against a target that was never reachable by the route they were funded to take. Targets should be set from your decomposed ledger outward: this much voluntary reduction from these programs, this much involuntary reduction from that recovery work. The benchmark can witness the sum. It should never dictate it.

Compare to learn what exists. Measure yourself to learn what is true. The first is a library; the second is an instrument, and businesses are steered by instruments.

Zcash Zcash (ZEC) announced that its major network upgrade, NU7, activated on the mainnet at block height 3,543,405 on Nov. 5.

The upgrade reduces the target block interval from 75 seconds to 25 seconds. Block subsidies are scaled down by the same factor so that the rate of new ZEC issuance remains essentially unchanged. The number of blocks between halvings is tripled, which preserves the existing halving schedule and the 21 million ZEC supply cap.

NU7 also activates the Network Sustainability Mechanism. The mechanism removes 60% of each block's transaction fees from circulation and stores it in a public reserve, with miners receiving the remaining 40%. The reserved fees are scheduled for gradual reissuance through block rewards beginning at block 8,939,664, expected to start in 2031.

"Like Bitcoin, Zcash faces a long-term challenge: how to sustain network security as block rewards decline. The NSM helps address that while preserving the 21 million coin cap," said Shielded Labs, an independent organization supporting the development of Zcash. The organization said NSM is its first major mainnet deployment on Zcash.

The upgrade also disallows version 4 transactions. As the original Sprout shielded pool relies exclusively on that format, any ZEC remaining in Sprout becomes unspendable. Approximately 12,224 ZEC sat in the pool at the activation height.

Node operators were required to upgrade software before the activation height to remain on the consensus chain. The changes do not require significant updates for most wallets.

Michael B. Jordan's Fantasy Series On Prime Video Reinvents The Genre, And Some Fans Won't Like It

While Michael B. Jordan has a massive Prime Video fantasy show on the way soon, fans of the genre should be aware that the show will divide audiences as it relies heavily on a majorly controversial trope. Ever since HBO spent an unprecedented amount of money on the budget of Game of Thrones season 1 back in 2011, every streaming service and cable network has been working overtime to find the next truly gigantic fantasy series.

From Prime Video’s Lord of the Rings prequel The Rings of Power to Netflix’s The Witcher, creators spread no expense replicating and even outdoing the ambition of HBO’s A Song of Ice & Fire adaptation. However, while Prime Video’s Critical Role shows The Legend of Vox Machina and The Mighty Nein share the mature content and fantasy adventure appeal of early Game of Thrones, no single show has supplanted the HBO hit as the biggest fantasy series ever.

Even HBO’s own Harry Potter reboot may struggle to replicate the popularity of Game of Thrones, since the series is a retread of a highly familiar intellectual property rather than an exciting new fantasy project. In contrast, Prime Video’s upcoming Michael B. Jordan vehicle Fourth Wing could potentially become the show to outdo Game of Thrones, but that hotly anticipated series has another issue of its own. The first major romantasy adaptation, Fourth Wing will decide whether this massive publishing trend can translate to small screen success.

A portmanteau of romance and fantasy, romantasy is a subgenre that mixes romance of varying spice levels with settings, tropes and characters usually found in the world of fantasy novels. Where Terry Pratchett’s books mix fantasy and comedy, romantasy novels mix fantasy and romance, and this blend has become big business in the book world. A Court of Thrones and Roses alone has managed to sell over 13 million copies since the novel series began.

However, as the long wait for George R.R. Martin’s A Song of Ice & Fire to reach the screen proves, fantasy trends in publishing aren't always immediately seized on by movies and TV. Fantasy is expensive and, while Game of Thrones and its spinoffs have proven that there is a mainstream market for mature, R-rated fantasy shows, their tone could not further from the romantic atmosphere of books like Fourth Wing.

Written by Rebecca Yarros, 2023’s Fourth Wing is the first in the Empyrean Pentalogy, followed by Iron Flame and Onyx Storm. Prime Video's Fourth Wing show will adapt the book’s immersive fantasy story, which follows the 20-year-old Violet Sorrengail as she is forced by her strict army general mother to become a dragon rider at the Basgiath War College, despite a challenging physical condition that makes her prone to injury.

While this set up might remind readers of How To Train Your Dragon, the reality of the books is much more adults-only in nature. The Empyrean Pentalogy is unapologetically adult in its content, with a lot of explicit sex scenes surprisingly early on in its enemies to lovers story. Describing the plot any further would run the risk of giving away who these scenes are between, but it is no spoiler to say the story’s romance elements owe as much tonally similar to Bridgerton than Game of Thrones.

Although Game of Thrones was extremely explicit, the fantasy show’s sex scenes were as apt to be disturbing as they were to be romantic. In contrast, the Fourth Wing books are mainstream romance novels in terms of their sexual content, and this means that the show’s big-budget live-action adaptation will have a markedly different tone from earlier major fantasy shows.

From the PG-rated Percy Jackson & The Olympians to Prime Video’s relatively family-friendly The Rings of Power, all the way to hard-R-rated, adult fantasy shows like House of the Dragon, A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms, and The Witcher, a heavy focus on romance and frequent sex scenes simply aren’t commonplace in the genre on the small screen. As such, some fantasy fans will no doubt be put off by Fourth Wing’s style.
