Crypto PACs eye 2028 “new map” as Democrats take Congress

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Crypto super PACs spent more than $176 million on the midterms, but the Democratic sweep raises the prospect of tougher industry scrutiny.

Two years after crypto money helped drive Sherrod Brown out of the Senate, roughly $64 million couldn’t keep him from returning.

Brown’s victory over Republican Jon Husted in Ohio delivered the industry its biggest electoral loss of the 2026 midterms as Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. The sweep also puts Sen. Elizabeth Warren, one of crypto’s fiercest critics, in line to chair the Senate Banking Committee.

Crypto super PACs spent about $176 million across 100 seats during the election cycle, including primaries, according to Reuters, up from more than $130 million in 2024. Fairshake and its affiliated PACs accounted for $154.5 million across 89 races.

“The next Congress will bring a fundamentally different environment for crypto in Washington, and the industry should expect significantly more scrutiny,” Kyle Bligen, executive director at the Decentralization Research Center, told Cointelegraph.

Fairshake, meanwhile, is left with more than $67 million heading to the 2028 elections. “Newly elected and current members will have a choice: protect consumers and American jobs or fight progress,” spokesperson Geoff Vetter told Cointelegraph in an email. “We will be watching and ‘28 is a new map.”

Ohio was the industry’s biggest bet as crypto PACs put more than $64 million into keeping Senator Jon Husted in office. His opponent, former Sen. Sherrod Brown, won the special election to complete former Sen. JD Vance’s term, which ends in January 2029. He could seek a senior role on the Senate Agriculture Committee, which oversees the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC).

Brown’s comeback came two years after crypto’s biggest win in 2024, when Fairshake spent $40 million to help Bernie Moreno unseat him in Ohio’s other Senate seat. Some industry leaders treated that result as a warning to Congress. Gemini exchange co-founder Cameron Winklevoss declared on X: “This is what happens when you mess with the crypto army”.

This year, crypto spending was part of a much larger flood of outside money. Independent expenditures in the race topped $350 million, while Brown turned the crypto spending into a campaign theme, writing “I’ll be damned if crypto billionaires decide this race,” in a campaign message.

The loss in Ohio may weaken the perception that defying crypto PACs carries an electoral cost in Washington. Before the vote, a Republican crypto lobbyist told Politico that a Brown win would “neuter Fairshake’s perceived Sword of Damocles.”

Nebraska, meanwhile, offered crypto PACs a significant win, with Sen. Pete Ricketts retaining his seat after Fairshake affiliate Defend American Jobs spent $13.5 million opposing independent challenger Dan Osborn.

“As the biggest spender in the Nebraska Senate race, we’re proud to have helped Sen. Ricketts weather the waves of a hard-fought cycle,” wrote Vetter. “Fairshake played a meaningful role in winning competitive races across the country in 2026 and we will do it again in 2028.” The PAC’s other Senate wins came in Republican strongholds, including Barry Moore in Alabama and Andy Barr in Kentucky.

Other spending failed to deliver the intended results, both in primaries and in November. The Fairshake network’s roughly $10 million spent opposing Juliana Stratton in the Illinois Democratic primary failed to block her nomination. She later won the Senate seat. In Massachusetts, John Deaton lost despite roughly $700,000 in spending by crypto-backed Commonwealth Unity Fund to help elect him. Ken Paxton, who received crypto PAC backing in his successful Texas Republican runoff against John Cornyn, went on to lose to James Talarico.

The House brought another setback when digital assets subcommittee chair Bryan Steil lost to Democrat Mitchell Berman despite $1.9 million in crypto PAC funding.

The shift in congressional control could slow crypto legislation, but BitGo CEO and co-founder Mike Belshe said the election results would not alter the industry’s trajectory. “Crypto has continued to move forward under different administrations and different political majorities. The GENIUS Act is now law, stablecoin rules are moving ahead, and regulators are continuing to work on tokenization and digital asset markets.”Democratic victories could also bring crypto supporters into the Senate. Advocacy group Stand With Crypto counted 19 winning Senate candidates it classified as pro-crypto and eight as anti-crypto, with 33 of the 35 Senate races called as of 9:45 p.m. UTC on Nov. 4.

Despite the crypto PAC spending against him in Texas, Talarico voted for the Texas Bitcoin reserve as a state lawmaker and is rated by the group as strongly supportive of crypto.

For industry advocates, the next challenge is building relationships with incoming lawmakers. Jessica Martinez, US policy director at Fireblocks, told Cointelegraph that many newly elected members had limited records on crypto, making “educating members early” key to shaping the Democratic policy agenda.

DNC Vice Chair: “We Had to Hit the Bottom”

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A leader of the Democratic blue-nami on viral videos, the billionaire’s tax and how everything had to go wrong before it could go right.

The Democrats and their many supporters in Hollywood had a historic election Tuesday night, and it’s leaving some of the people who orchestrated it both celebratory and trying to learn from the results.Jane Kleeb is one such person. A vice chair of the Democratic National Committee under Ken Martin who also heads up the Association of State Democratic Committees (which basically represents the Democratic party in each state and territory), she helped lead the movement that resulted in Tuesday’s many wins.

A native Nebraskan and longtime activist, Kleeb was in a giddy mood Wednesday, and also a pragmatic one. We got on the phone with her to break everything down.First I have to ask, how much of a surprise were some of these races? A few felt on the bubble coming in but others were just shockers. Does it feel that way to you?I definitely thought we were going to win the House and the Senate back because I knew the quality of candidates and the infrastructure we were building, and I thought we would win some governors’ races and break some supermajorities at the state legislative level as well. But I didn’t know it would be to this extent — that the margins would be so wide. In some of these races it wasn’t one or two percentage points; it was five or six or more. So it was a resounding victory, not just voting against Trump.So you don’t see a lot of these as “anti” votes.When I was watching some pundits [on cable news] last night people were pontificating saying this was just a rebuke to Trump. And no question it was. But it was also because Democratic organizers everywhere and candidates who looked and sounded like their communities and were also fighters. So it was all of those factors.Of course, you’ve tried to do that before. What changed?I think it’s because our candidates this cycle thought they had nothing to lose. That Donald Trump was such a threat to democracy that they were putting everything on the field and because of that it happened all across the country not just in targeted battleground states.You say nothing to lose. In your mind did you need to hit rock bottom — did 2024’s calamity enable this?There’s no question we had a major reality-check as a party in 2024. And even in 2016. We felt like we recovered some footing in 2020 but not enough to carry us. We saw that with the divisions over keeping Biden as the nominee or switching candidates 100 days out in [2024]. And that’s a picture of where we were as a party, that we couldn’t find our voice, we couldn’t find our footing. We couldn’t figure out how to communicate to the American people all of the good things we were doing. So we hit the bottom. We had to hit the bottom. When Trump won again we saw with so many of the communities that we frankly took for granted — Black voters and young voters and Latino voters — they saw Trump as a change agent and someone who would break up the status quo. And obviously he didn’t deliver on any of that, so we picked back up and said “actually we’re the party fighting for change to level the playing field.” And voters received that message.That certainly happened across the Midwest and the South. But a lot of our readers are in California, where that didn’t happen — it looks like the billionaire’s tax is going to fail pretty badly. Why couldn’t you make that case to Californians?If the tax fails it could be for a lot of reasons. Maybe it’s that people don’t like taxes, it could be that.Honestly the way it was laid out on the ballot was also confusing — you basically had a billionaire tax and a ban on a billionaire tax right next to each other, both just looking like a property tax.I think it could also have been that. I definitely still believe people are deeply frustrated by the billionaire class and want to see accountability from billionaires. So maybe it’s not a tax but maybe it’s stopping giveaways at the federal level. There are other ways to get that accountability.One thing that struck me about the election is that unlike in past party sweeps — say, the Democrats in 2008 with Obama — there was no central charismatic figure around which all these House and Senate and local races coalesced. That you did it bottom-up instead of top-down.

The difference this time was definitely the story of a team instead of a lead actor. And I think that’s where the party shines. When rely on one person to carry us through we’re going to lose that battle. That happened with President Obama during his terms. We lost 1,500 seats at the local level over his eight years because we weren’t investing in state and local infrastructure; we got so wrapped up in “Obama.” So I think we learned the hard lesson that it’s this infrastructure model that works. And we saw that this time.What would you say was the biggest surprise last night?Symone Sanders, who is from [here in] Nebraska had been talking about Mississippi. So I went down and visited. I thought ’this was a state where it could happen, but not this cycle.’ But that was a mistake. We had Brandon Presley, [a moderate Democrat] who almost won [for governor in 2023]. But we didn’t give the resources to get over the finish line. And to see what Scott [Colom] has done without those resources — if this happens [Colom is currently leading his opponent Cindy Hyde-Smith by a few dozen votes with 44,000 ballots outstanding] it’s from the sheer grit of Scott and the Democratic Party there and the candidates down-ballot.And the viral videos were insane — he did that whole pick “another protein/pick another Senator” bit to troll Hyde-Smith’s line from a debate. I don’t think I can remember a political ad as subtly effective and smile-inducing as that.He’s been great. And it does make me feel like, damn, we should have known.You do have limited resources, though, and I wonder how you spread that out now that so many states seem to be in play, places like Kansas and Nebraska and Texas and South Carolina and maybe even Alaska. It seemed a lot easier when certain red states were just automatically out of bounds. Does this indeed make where to spend your money more complicated?What is very clear from the amount of money raised this cycle is that we don’t have limited resources. There’s plenty of money in the political system. What you will hear from me and Chair Martin and many who live and work in our 57 states and territories is we can and should have permanent infrastructure everywhere, no exceptions, year-round.You don’t think there are simply some states too far gone to justify Democrats spending money in — that if, say, Florida couldn’t elect a Democratic Senator or governor in this climate, maybe it’s beyond hope for the party?

I think this is absolutely the wrong way to look at it. Colorado was red not that long ago, and then we began organizing and getting young people to vote. Georgia was red not that long ago and now look at it. [SZ: Both those states saw large influxes of Democrats from other states that Florida has not seen.] I grew up in Florida and it was a swing state. We’re starting to organize there. You have places like Colorado and Mississippi where there are cities that are blue and then you spread out to the suburbs and beyond making inroads there. Florida has that too. I think you’ll begin to see things shift significantly in places like Florida and Nebraska [where the party came close to defeating a Republican this cycle with an independent Senate candidate] as we head into 2028.Speaking of looking ahead, now the Democrats have to govern. And they have to govern with moderates and progressives and far-left activists in the came caucus. We see here in Hollywood the rifts that can happen even among Democrats — we see it, and I’m kidding but not kidding, all too well unfortunately. How do you ensure those rifts don’t happen in the next two years?I think having progressives and moderates gives us an advantage because we’ll have durable long-lasting policies that all kinds of Americans will embrace. Because the American people have all those different views too. People look at these disparities as a risk, but I see them as an advantage.

10 Classic Rock Albums You Must Listen to Before You Die

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Classic rock is maybe definable as the kind of rock you're likely to hear on classic rock radio stations (unsurprisingly), but a better definition could be narrowing it down to rock released during a certain period. That period was when rock was at its most popular and culturally dominant, and arguably, during that time, rock music was also at its best, so it’s classic rock, and then it also happens to be rock that is classic (in terms of quality and historical importance).

There’s an attempt to single out some particularly great classic rock albums below, all of them worth listening to before you die. Or, less morbidly, listen to them all while you're still breathing; before the bucket’s kicked. That’s still pretty morbid. Sorry. But these albums are amazing, and enough so that you’ve probably heard most of them before, but if you haven’t, they're essential to give a spin at least once before… you know…

It feels uncool to call them Creedence Clearwater Revival instead of just “Creedence,” but either way, here's Cosmo’s Factory, which is probably the band’s best album. Creedence (yeah, let’s be cool for a bit; for fun) is entirely classic rock in sound, really being demonstrative of this particular era of rock, and so sure, Cosmo’s Factory is blunt, certainly straightforward, and pretty easy to listen to quite passively.

Sometimes it’s just what you need, though? Something loud, catchy, and very of its time, but all of those things in good ways. Cosmo’s Factory is strong enough that you might not love Creedence, or the particular kind of rock they made, but you're still likely going to, at the very least, tolerate this particular album. And if you are fond of classic rock and/or Creedence, then it’s safe to say you're going to go well beyond tolerating this, since you'll probably love it.

If there’s one flaw with Damn the Torpedoes, it’s that it starts in a way strong enough that you could argue the momentum isn't entirely made, yet that’s a nitpick, since the album after the first three tracks is still strong. It’s more just that the run from “Refugee” to “Here Comes My Girl” to “Even the Losers” is especially great, with three high-quality songs on their own all feeling perfectly sequenced, and thereby made even better.

But Damn the Torpedoes is not a long album, so once you're three tracks down, there are only six to go, with “Don't Do Me Like That” and “Louisiana Rain” among those remaining songs. Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers had a knack for pumping out punchy rock music early on, so the two albums before Damn the Torpedoes and the one following it are also great, yet it’s Damn the Torpedoes that’s the most consistent and memorable of the bunch.

Make no mistake: The Rolling Stones had a great run throughout a good chunk of the 1960s, but there’s an argument to be made that it was their 1970s run that demonstrated the band at their strongest. Sticky Fingers is especially strong, and the one being singled out here, but it wouldn’t be wise to overlook the 1960s, and then ‘80s-wise, the band was still going fairly strong there, even if their music in that decade wasn’t quite as “classic rock” in sound.

Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century later.

Anyway, Sticky Fingers was the album that helped demonstrate The Rolling Stones could well be the new kings of rock, making a great start for the whole decade, right around a time when another legendary British band had broken up, leaving the future of rock temporarily uncertain, to some extent. More on that other band later, by the way. But yes, Sticky Fingers is great, and most of it holds up incredibly well over half a century on from its initial release.

Easily one of the best double albums of all time, Electric Ladyland is probably The Jimi Hendrix Experience’s greatest album, which is a big take, even without too many albums by the band. It comes down to all those albums being phenomenal and influential, and it’s just the case that Electric Ladyland, with its size and also all the wild things it’s going for – and successfully trying to cram into – a single album.

Electric Ladyland is still such a wonderful and strange album to dive into, even today, with it coming up on nearly six decades since it first came out (safe to assume it would've been even more mind-blowing to hear back in 1968). There’s an undeniably interesting atmosphere to the album overall, and it’s surprisingly consistent, all the while saving two of the best songs until the very end: a cover of Bob Dylan’s “All Along the Watchtower” and then “Voodoo Child (Slight Return).”

This spot could’ve easily gone to Who’s Next, which has the singles and perhaps more historical/cultural importance, so consider that an honorable mention for that possibly easier pick (the one that’s slightly more straightforward, not to mention potentially simpler to praise). But Quadrophenia is even better, and maybe ever so slightly underrated, being like Electric Ladyland in the way it sustains itself for twice as long as a normal album, but then it’s also more of a concept album.

Broadly, Quadrophenia is about struggling to fit in while growing up and dealing with intense isolation, and lots of what it tackles lyrically still packs a punch. The story might not be as comprehensible as was the case with The Who’s Tommy, but it doesn’t really matter when there’s so much to feel throughout Quadrophenia, since it’s very emotional, cathartic at times, and then also a bit intense at times thanks to how full-on the introspection gets at other points.

Bruce Springsteen had a remarkable run throughout the 1970s and 1980s, since it’s that period of time that saw the release of the majority of his all-time great albums. There are albums after that era that are still pretty great, but then again, it’s also hard for anything to be close to Born to Run in terms of quality. And even compared to the other Springsteen albums of those two decades, Born to Run is special.

It does a lot to condense everything great about Springsteen into just eight largely phenomenal tracks, and while they'd all be worth mentioning, it’s especially worth mentioning the title track (an all-timer), “Thunder Road,” and “Jungleland,” the former kicking off Born to Run expertly, and the latter being one essentially perfect closing track. Everything here works, and it’s 40-ish perfect minutes of classic rock, in the end.

It says something about how great Led Zeppelin were, as a band, that you could probably put an album of theirs without “Stairway to Heaven” in a ranking like this and it would still feel right. The first half-dozen albums are all very hard to fault, but among greatness, it’s probably Led Zeppelin IV that well and truly is the greatest, and sure, “Stairway to Heaven” helps, but the other songs here are also fantastic.

Technically, this album’s untitled, but it’s easier to call it Led Zeppelin IV after the band put out three self-titled albums in the couple of years before Led Zeppelin IV. There’s also a great variety of tracks here, with some not being particularly rock-heavy, but then other parts do indeed get very energetic and loud. The slower songs make for a nice contrast, and Led Zeppelin IV transitions from song to song incredibly well, with the pacing and overall flow being impeccable.

There are arguments to be made for several other albums by The Beatles that aren’t Abbey Road to be worthy of being called the best, but it’s Abbey Road that feels like a culmination of the band’s entire run. No shade to those other albums, though, since the band was remarkably consistent and boundary-pushing, especially through the last half of the 1960s (see also Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band, the self-titled or "White" Album, Revolver, Rubber Soul, and Magical Mystery Tour, if you want to count that one).

Abbey Road has some all-time great tracks on it, and the whole thing flows incredibly well, too, especially all those short songs in the second half that make up a medley. It’s got almost everything great about The Beatles packed into one album, and is also significant for being the final one the band recorded, even if Let It Be, largely recorded earlier, was the last album of the band’s to be released (and the only one that didn’t come out in the 1960s, since it dropped in 1970).

Pink Floyd is known for making albums that you can really get lost in, and The Dark Side of the Moon is especially dense and immersive in a mostly good way. “Mostly” because this album is pretty heavy-going at times, so maybe it’s not for every mood, yet it’s for a lot of them, and it is overall about life quite broadly, helping it be a little easier to get into than some of the band’s other great (and also ambitious) rock albums.

So, it’s great within Pink Floyd’s discography, and then when judged on its own, The Dark Side of the Moon is still phenomenal and everything. It’s just such a quintessential album – rock or otherwise – that it’s hard to know how to even praise it or convince someone who’s yet to listen to it that they really need to get on it. Also, it’s probably the kind of thing almost everyone’s heard at this point, so maybe it’s an odd recommendation for present purposes. Still, uh, maybe re-listen to it before you die? Yeah, there we go. That works.

If you want to find great David Bowie songs, you can go as far back as the 1960s, since “Space Oddity” came out then and is an obvious classic, but if you want essential albums of his, it’s best to start in the 1970s. He kept going beyond that decade, of course, with his final album (Blackstar) even being a masterpiece, but the most masterful of his masterpieces is probably The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars.

And since no one has really ever done it better than Bowie, why not also call it the best classic rock album of all time? The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars is just too good, with everything being perfect to the extent that it’s hard to know what else to say. Why bother saying anything when you can just listen? Thankfully, The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars never really gets old, either.

ChatGPT Is Getting a GIF Maker Powered by OpenAI's Unannounced Images 2.6 Model

The internet may soon have an unlimited supply of AI-generated dancing cats, courtesy of ChatGPT.

OpenAI is rolling out a feature that lets users generate animated GIFs directly inside ChatGPT, according to The Verge. The feature reportedly comes powered by its new image-generation model called Images 2.6, which OpenAI has yet to formally introduce through its usual product announcements.

Users can describe an animation in plain English or upload an existing photo and ask ChatGPT to bring it to life. Once the GIF is generated, they can adjust its playback speed, inspect individual frames, make edits, and share the finished animation.

OpenAI demonstrated the capability with a simple request: “can you make a gif of a cat dancing?” The resulting interface reportedly includes controls to change animation speed and examine the GIF frame by frame.

We tried generating GIFs using ChatGPT, but the feature was not available on our accounts at the time of testing. In our interaction, the model generates a series of frames, but not the animation.

The rollout appears to be gradual, so users may have to keep asking for their dancing cats a little longer.

The GIF maker is reportedly part of a broader Images 2.6 update that promises faster image generation. The Verge reports that OpenAI is bringing the model to ChatGPT and Codex users across web, desktop, and mobile platforms.

Developers are also getting two new API models, GPT-Image-2.6 Flare and GPT-Image-2.6 Sunburst, designed for faster generation and higher-quality results, respectively. OpenAI has not yet published a standalone announcement detailing the new model's capabilities, and we could not independently test its performance.

The update follows the September release of ChatGPT Images 2.5, which introduced sharper details, more precise editing, and generation speeds up to 50% faster than its predecessor.

In our previous testing, Images 2.5 went head-to-head with Google's Nano Banana 2 across six categories, with each model winning three. OpenAI's model delivered particularly impressive illustration quality and improved on the oversharpening problems that affected earlier versions, although it still made mistakes when rendering text.

The move into GIF generation also comes months after OpenAI shut down Sora, its standalone AI video-generation app, in March. Animated GIFs are considerably simpler than the videos Sora generated, but the new feature brings a form of motion generation directly into ChatGPT without requiring a separate application.

OpenAI has not provided a specific date for when every ChatGPT account will receive GIF generation.

The Lincoln Lawyer's final season is leaving Harry Bosch out of the case.

Netflix's The Lincoln Lawyer and the multiple series that feature Titus Welliver's Harry Bosch on Prime Video all come from the same author. Bestselling writer Michael Connelly has penned several novels starring Bosch, who Welliver has played in Bosch, Bosch: Legacy, and Ballard; and Mickey Haller, who is the star of The Lincoln Laweyer, portrayed across four seasons thus far by Manuel Garcia-Rulfo. The two characters are not only connected by the fact that they were created by the same author, but also due to how Bosch and Haller are half-brothers in Connelly's novels. However, Bosch is being replaced for season 5.

On the Northwest Passages conversation series shared by The Spokesman-Review, Connelly addressed Bosch's replacement in The Lincoln Lawyer season 5.

According to the writer, very early in the final season of the Netflix legal thriller series, Garcia-Rulfo's Mickey Haller will find out that he has a half-sister, Emi Finch, played by Cobie Smulders, continuing the cliffhanger from season 4's finale, where the mysterious character claimed to be Haller's sister. Smulders' character is an original creation for the Netflix series, not existing in Connelly's books. The reason why The Lincoln Lawyer could not use Welliver's Harry Bosch comes down to the rights to adapt the separate characters, with Haller set up at Netflix, while Amazon keeps the ever-expanding Bosch universe apart on Prime Video.

I Tried Hailey Bieber’s Favorite Creatine Gummies for Two Months, and I Have Thoughts

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Why these candylike gumdrops have converted me into a creatine-lover—plus, the other formulas I love

The supplement landscape has never been murkier, more confusing, or more overwhelming. While I have a pretty extensive wellness regimen, it feels like there are a million and one powders, pills, and gummies I “should” be adding to my routine. One supplement that keeps coming into the spotlight is creatine.

I first heard about creatine from my 62-year-old mother, who waxed poetic about the supplement. As someone who isn’t one to experiment with supplements, my ears perked up. Then, the influencer of all influencers, Hailey Bieber, posted on Instagram a few weeks later touting the same supplement—this time, a gummy version from Arrae. I thought: That’s it, I’m taking the creatine plunge.

“Creatine works like an energy recycler,” says Roxanne Pero, a Dallas-based obstetrician and gynecologist and part of O Positiv’s Medical Advisory Board. “Every cell in the body needs energy, and that energy comes from a molecule called ATP, or adenosine triphosphate. Creatine helps the body regenerate ATP more quickly, especially when energy runs low, and especially in tissues that use a lot of it, like muscle and the brain.”

As someone who works out five times a week, often in heated classes that push my body to depletion, I figured I was the perfect candidate for creatine. However, creatine is one of those supplements anyone can benefit from; it kicks in when our cells need more energy, like during strenuous exercise, but also during sleep deprivation and stress—and even perimenopause, menopause, and postpartum for women—which everyone experiences at some point.

North Carolina Passes New Limits on Property Tax Levies

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On Tuesday, North Carolina voters narrowly approved House Bill 1089, which proposed a limit on property tax increases. The amendment needed 60% of voters to support it in order to pass. It received 60.4% support.

The amendment does not specify the terms of those limits. Instead, that will be up to the North Carolina General Assembly to decide.

Proponents, including North Caroline House Speaker Destin Hall, contended that surging property assessments have enabled local governments to impose excessive tax burdens on residents without delivering proportional public services. Last year, Hall formed the House Select Committee on Property Tax Reduction and Reform, a 23-member committee to examine property taxes in the state.

“Families are getting ripped off as some, but by no means all, local governments rake in billions more than inflation and population growth warrant,” Hall said in a press release earlier this year.

Support for the amendment fell across party lines. Conservatives and commercial organizations supported the measure, arguing that the amendment would help curb local government spending and protect property owners.

Political pundits theorized that the ballot measures were meant to entice Republican voters to the polls during an off-election year. But Western Carolina University Professor Chris Cooper told NPR's All Things Considered the theory didn't hold much weight to him.

"The notion that North Carolinians were not going to show up and vote from the Republican persuasion, but now they are because of a constitutional amendment, is on there, frankly that stretches credibility for me a little bit."

But critics of the proposal said that creating a property tax cap would hobble local governments. Further, the North Carolina Housing Coalition argued that the proposal would most benefit larger corporate property owners while providing little benefit to owners of single-family homes.

Tamika Walker Kelly, president of the North Carolina Association of Educators, criticized the bill for favoring “tax breaks over classrooms.”

“When local revenue is constrained, schools feel it immediately with overcrowded classrooms, aging school buildings and educators stretched too thin,” Walker Kelly told NC Newsline. “A constitutional cap does not protect struggling families, it protects the largest landowners in the state, while the families who depend on public schools are left with less.”

In the U.S., property tax rates are determined at the local level and vary widely. The average effective property tax rate on owner-occupied housing in North Carolina is .66%, which is relatively low compared with states like New Jersey and Illinois, where the effective tax rate is 1.88%, according to Tax Foundation, a nonpartisan tax policy nonprofit.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: A proposed liquefied natural gas project in Pakistan underscores close ties with the Trump administration, garment workers in Bangladesh protest for pay increases, and India’s opposition alliance rallies to call for the resignation of the chief election commissioner.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s South Asia Brief.

The highlights this week: A proposed liquefied natural gas project in Pakistan underscores close ties with the Trump administration, garment workers in Bangladesh protest for pay increases, and India’s opposition alliance rallies to call for the resignation of the chief election commissioner.

Since U.S. President Donald Trump returned to the White House last year, Pakistan has banked heavily on doing business with the United States. With the Democrats flipping both houses of Congress in the U.S. midterm elections on Tuesday, though, one has to wonder if Islamabad could come out as another loser.

For one thing, scrutiny of Trump’s business dealings will certainly increase, constraining his administration’s capacity to pursue deals abroad.

A big topic of discussion in Pakistan this week—along with the midterms—is the news that a consortium of U.S. companies has proposed a $4.4 billion liquefied natural gas (LNG) project in Port Qasim, near Karachi. It would be Pakistan’s first LNG storage facility, helping to shield the country from global energy shocks.

The proposal entails a large-scale storage terminal and eventually a 680-mile delivery pipeline. The plan is backed by the U.S. Trade and Development Agency, U.S. International Development Finance Corp., and the Export-Import Bank of the United States.

Washington’s support for the proposed LNG project underscores just how much the U.S.-Pakistan relationship has changed in Trump’s second term. Bilateral ties were downgraded after the U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021, in large part because Washington had long viewed relations with Islamabad through a security lens.

The Trump administration turned this approach on its head. The United States now prioritizes ties with Pakistan, and Trump often praises the country’s leadership. Though counterterrorism still figures in the relationship, Washington has emphasized economic and commercial issues in its engagement with Islamabad.

In particular, the United States has taken a strong interest in Pakistan’s critical minerals and cryptocurrency resources. It has pushed for the country to import more U.S. goods, including energy supplies. In October 2025, Pakistan received its first-ever shipment of U.S. crude oil. Washington has pushed to remove trade obstacles with Islamabad, including ending a two-year ban on U.S. soybean exports.

Pakistan, which had long pitched its economic offerings to the United States unsuccessfully, has capitalized on the Trump administration’s newfound interest in its assets. The Financial Times reported last year that Islamabad went as far as to offer Washington the opportunity to build and manage a port on the Arabian Sea. (Pakistan publicly denies this.)

The proposed LNG project would be one of the Trump administration’s most ambitious commercial ventures in Pakistan, perhaps putting an exclamation point on the shift in the U.S. approach to the country. Yet that shift might not even be sustainable—especially given the midterm results.

The Trump administration’s commercial focus is largely driven by the president’s whims and by the economic interests of his family members and close associates. But this arrangement won’t outlast Trump’s term, which ends in two years. Further, with Congress in the hands of the Democrats starting in January, it could also become more difficult to pull off.

Conversations with Pakistani interlocutors this week revealed a combination of curiosity and concern about ties with the United States in the Trump era. There seems to be a desire to understand how the relationship improved so quickly alongside a sense that it might not last for long, given Trump’s unpredictability. The Democrats’ victory will only sharpen these concerns.

These worries reflect an evergreen challenge for the U.S.-Pakistan relationship: how to craft a strong partnership that can withstand political change in both countries and weather external shocks.

The last time the White House officially announced a policy on Pakistan was during Trump’s first term, in August 2017, when a new South Asia strategy included a hard-line approach toward Islamabad. It’s clear that the United States is overdue in conducting a formal review of its Pakistan policy, even as the relationship is being informally reevaluated and refashioned.

Garment protests in Bangladesh. Garment workers have taken to the streets in industrial areas of Dhaka, Bangladesh’s capital, in recent days to call for pay increases. Clashes with security forces have prompted the closure of dozens of garment factories and raised concerns about shipment delays.

Garment workers are a critical constituency in Bangladesh, numbering around 4 million, and the sector accounts for 80 percent of the country’s export earnings. Bangladesh’s ready-made garment industry is a global success story; it made the country one of South Asia’s best-performing economies until a slump in the last few years.

With the downturn, Bangladesh seems to be paying the price for relying so heavily on a single export—as globally competitive as it was—for so long. The protests are just the latest policy challenge for Dhaka, which is already struggling with economic stress, energy shortages, and the world’s most serious measles crisis.

These issues risk distracting Bangladesh from pursuing economic and governance reforms—something the government, which took office in February, was given a mandate to prioritize.

India’s opposition alliance rallies. On Sunday, the main opposition alliance in India staged protests in New Delhi to call for the resignation of India’s chief election commissioner, Gyanesh Kumar. The rally drew tens of thousands of people and marked the latest mobilization against a voter roll revision that critics say favored the ruling Bharatiya Janata Party.

The youth-led Cockroach Janta Party has spearheaded the protests calling for Kumar to step down. This summer, the Cockroach movement galvanized protests over leaked exam papers that ultimately resulted in the resignation of the country’s education minister.

Now, India’s opposition is riding on the coattails of this activism. The success of the Cockroach movement is a gift to the opposition, which has long been hobbled by the absence of a popular leader and disagreements within its alliance.

Heat stress becomes serious vulnerability. The International Labor Organization (ILO) released studies last week warning that by 2040, workplace heat stress spawned by rising temperatures could lead to reduced working time equivalent to around 145,000 full-time jobs in Sri Lanka and 2,400 in the Maldives.

The reports estimate that these losses will be valued at around $26 billion in Sri Lanka and $576 million in the Maldives. The hardest-hit sector in Sri Lanka will be agriculture, along with services, manufacturing, and construction. In the Maldives, services and construction will be the most vulnerable industries.

The ILO findings are a reminder of the high economic cost of climate change in a region where economies are fragile—and where some of the most labor-dependent sectors are especially vulnerable to climate effects.

Last Sunday, around 200,000 people converged on the Bhutanese town of Gelephu to watch volunteers construct 108 stupas. (The number 108 is sacred in Buddhism.) The initiative is part of a campaign led by Bhutan’s king to transform Gelephu, near Bhutan’s border with India, into a “mindfulness city.”

Bhutan hopes to use the initiative, which emphasizes ecological sustainability and happiness, as an antidote to the country’s deepening economic stress. The stupa project is certainly an unorthodox way to tackle these problems. Critics will likely say it does little to reverse rising unemployment and a rapid outflow of highly skilled workers.

However, the project aligns with a long-standing Bhutanese principle known as “gross national happiness,” which measures national well-being not by income or other economic indicators but instead by the level of contentment among citizens.

PSV Eindhoven endured a frustrating night in the Champions League as their defensive frailties were exposed in a 2-1 defeat by Club Brugge.

PSV started full of attacking intent and should have taken an early lead when Ivan Perisic put Sergiño Dest clean through on goal, but the defender’s shot was too close to goalkeeper Yann Sommer.

Instead it was the visitors who took the lead with their first real attack when Nicolo Tresoldi headed in a teasing cross from Joaquin Seys.

PSV equalised early in the second half as an outstanding pass by Sven Mijnans into the path Lutsharel Geertruida allowed the defender to charge to the byline. His pullback found its way to Kodai Sano, whose shot took a deflection off Seys into the net.

But five minutes later PSV were caught out as another razor-sharp pass by Seys released Carlos Forbs in space to net the winner.

The home side refused to give up, but were unable to find a way past Sommer in the closing stages as the Belgian side closed out the win.

Team captain Mauro Junior admitted that PSV had been undone by shortcomings at the back. “We had a lot of opportunities in the first half but we didn’t score,” he told Ziggo Sport.

“We want to attack and score goals, but we weren’ well organised today in defence. We need to keep our heads up and look forward to the next game.”

The result leaves PSV in 24th place, just above the elimination line, ahead of their next match away to Real Madrid on November 24, with four points from their opening four matches.
