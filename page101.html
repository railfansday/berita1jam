If I Could Give My Younger Self 1 Piece of Investing Advice, This Is What It Would Be

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

There's no substitute for experience, but it still helps to know some important rules of thumb.

If I could give my younger self one piece of investing advice, I'd find most of the material I'd need by reviewing my first-ever investment. My first stock was Lucent Technologies, which in 1996 was spun off from AT&T, and then bought by me at age 10 with all $500 of the birthday money I had saved up from prior years. Shortly after my purchase in late 1999, it began a long decline that saw its price fall 99.2% by September 2002.

The sting of that collapse kept me away from investing for about 15 years. So here's the investing advice that I would give to my younger self if I had to do things over again -- it's one central theme, and it has three parts.

This advice seems obvious now, but new investors still need to learn it.

Funneling all your capital into one stock means one failure can wipe out most of your portfolio. Per research conducted at Arizona State University, about 4.3% of the nearly 26,000 U.S. stocks included in the analysis accounted for nearly all of the market's wealth creation above Treasury bills from 1926 to 2016. That's an argument for owning a large and diversified basket of stocks via an index fund like the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO +0.30%).

The lesson extends to the types of assets you invest in, too. The traditional 60/40 stocks/bonds portfolio split is timeless because holding bonds can make up for underperformance that stocks experience under certain conditions, and vice versa. Today, adding some cryptocurrency into your portfolio is another way to diversify even beyond that.

If you invest in a lot of different companies, some of those investments will not play out.

According to a 2014 J.P. Morgan study, 40% of Russell 3000 index stocks from 1980 to 2014 ended up experiencing a catastrophic loss, which in this case is defined as a 70% or larger drop from the peak with little recovery afterward. In the case of my first investment, in the telecom industry, the catastrophic loss rate was 51%.

The bigger point here is psychological in nature. Expecting every one of your stock picks to work out is not reasonable, but investing requires us to be rationally hopeful each time. The truth is that failure in investing is inevitable, and vastly more common than success. Quitting after the first big failure, as I did, cost me years of compounding, and not having the proper context to process the defeat in a constructive way was especially deleterious.

So when an investment does poorly, diagnose whether the cause was bad luck, incomplete or poor analysis, or something else, and then fix that and move on to the next opportunity. And then if a different investment fails, repeat the process, again and again, until your results improve. Reading about past stock market crashes can also show you how much pain is normal.

I originally picked Lucent for my first investment because it was among the first companies I knew of in general -- at age 10, I wasn't a sophisticated investor, to say the least! Its original identity before the spinoff from AT&T was the legendary Bell Labs, one of the greatest research and development (R&D) organizations of all time, which even as a child I knew enough about to admire.

Still, I did not really have a firm understanding of why Lucent's stock was going to be worth more (or, in this case, less) in the future. I didn't really have an investment thesis. And while it's certainly a tall order to develop that understanding, especially as a new investor, it's the core of the art of investing in stocks, so my advice to my younger self would be to start grappling with these issues to the extent that it would be possible to do so at that point.

Before buying a stock, I'd tell my younger self to write two sentences: one on what the company sells, to whom, and why they buy it, and one on why its profits should be bigger in five years.

Even at age 10, I think I could've worked out those questions. And while that probably still wouldn't have actually saved me from investing in Lucent, I'm sure that I'd approach the questions with a bit more sophistication in the next investment. And improving your process over the long run is the heart of the battle.

How much did an iPhone cost in 2007 vs. 2026? The gap is smaller than you think

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Here's how the first iPhone's price stacks up against Apple's latest once inflation is factored in.

Understandably, it can feel like iPhone prices are out of control these days. The iPhone Duo starts at $1,999 and costs as much as $3,199 if you want to max out its storage. An iPhone Air is $1,100, despite having one rear camera and sporting inferior battery life next to the iPhone 17, never mind the iPhone 18 Pro. You can buy a decent laptop for the same price or less.

If you've been alive long enough, you might be pining for the days when iPhones were hundreds of dollars cheaper. For a brief time, mostly during the 3G era, price was part of their appeal. While they were hardly dirt cheap, it was possible to get a revolutionary do-it-all device without bearing the whole cost upfront or absorbing an absurd amount of debt.

In reality, the difference between old and new iPhones isn't as big as you might think. The leading factor is, of course, inflation, but you're also getting far more capable hardware, and some early carrier deals might be messing with your perception.

Let's start with the inflation issue. The original iPhone started at $499 for a 4GB model. There's no exact 2026 equivalent (the vanilla iPhone 18 has yet to be released), but we can get close enough with 2025's iPhone 17, which is currently $899. Superficially, that's a $400 gap. But if you adjust for inflation of the US dollar, the 2007 iPhone jumps to over $800.

Remember also that the 2007 model required a two-year AT&T contract, so your monthly payments subsidized the phone. Today, you're buying an unlocked device unless you specifically seek out a carrier deal. Those AT&T plans were also expensive. You were paying at least $60 per month in unadjusted dollars for unlimited 2G data. Today, you might pay a similar amount or less for unlimited 5G.

Many subsequent models like the iPhone 3G and 3GS sold for only $199, but again, the trade-off was a two-year carrier contract. Adjusted for inflation, the unlocked iPhone 4 ($599 in 2010) costs around $920 today, slightly more than the $899 iPhone 17. Carriers expected to profit from iPhone owners over the long term, so they were willing to absorb some of the cost to get them in the door.

Newer iPhones include not only better parts but also more of them. The 2007 iPhone didn't have a front-facing camera for selfies or video calls, nor did it have an NFC radio for Apple Pay or features like AirDrop, Find My or GPS. Modern iPhones can fetch location data from satellites and even send emergency messages when no cellular or Wi-Fi is available.

iPhones and other smartphones have become so capable that for some owners, they may be the only portable devices they need. A smartphone can replace cameras, flashlights, radios, navigation systems, gaming handhelds and portable TVs. You can even use one for serious productivity tasks like writing and spreadsheets. Back in 2007, Apple lacked a framework for third-party apps — that had to wait until 2008.

There is an argument to be made that Apple should care more about affordability. In 2026, $899 is still too much for a lot of people, to the extent that putting out a $1,999 model might feel like Apple is playing a cruel joke. Still, Apple has been surprisingly consistent, even holding base iPhones at $799 until memory prices forced its hand.

A New Fiber Supplement Could Be a Game Changer for Millions With IBS

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

For people with IBS, a specially designed fiber blend could support a healthier gut without added pain or bloating, a trial alongside a low-FODMAP diet suggests.

Cutting out foods that leave you bloated or in pain can bring welcome relief. For millions of people with irritable bowel syndrome, or IBS, however, a diet that eases discomfort can come with a trade-off. Restricting certain foods can also reduce fiber intake and the beneficial bacteria that help support gut health.

Researchers at Edith Cowan University (ECU) in Australia have tested a specially formulated fiber blend that could help address that dilemma. Called ur gut, the supplement combines high-amylose maize resistant starch with psyllium husk powder. In a small clinical trial, the blend changed the composition of participants’ gut bacteria without worsening their abdominal pain, bloating, diarrhea, or constipation.

IBS is common worldwide and affects nearly 11 percent of Australians. Its cause remains unknown, although stress, infection, and medication are potential triggers. Certain foods, poor sleep, psychological stress, hormonal fluctuations, and lifestyle factors can aggravate symptoms.

One common recommendation is a low-FODMAP diet, which limits certain carbohydrates that gut bacteria readily ferment.

“For years we’ve known that a diet low in fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols (FODMAPs) can relieve symptoms, but they can also reduce beneficial gut bacteria and negatively impact fiber intake over time,” said lead author Dr. Tina Yan.

“We wanted to find a way to build better gut health while following a low-FODMAP diet.”

Scientists at ECU’s Nutrition and Health Innovation Research Institute (NHIRI) developed the blend and trialed it in people with IBS who were following a low-FODMAP diet. Adding fiber in this setting requires care because bacteria can produce gas as they break it down.

“Traditionally, fermentable fibers can be difficult for people with IBS because they can increase gas production and discomfort,” Dr. Yan said.

“What’s exciting about this study is that participants maintained stable symptoms while showing meaningful improvements in their gut microbiota as well as in their gut-related anxiety.”

The changes in participants’ gut bacterial composition were significant, and their extent stood out to ur gut® co-creator Professor Amanda Devine.

“Changes of this magnitude are rarely seen with such a short intervention,” she said.

“The study demonstrated that our targeted dietary fiber blend positively reshaped the gut microbiome in people with IBS following a low-FODMAP diet, supporting beneficial bacteria linked to gut and mental health. This was great to see.”

The researchers suspect the explanation lies in how the blend’s two ingredients, resistant starch and psyllium husk, work together. Resistant starch escapes digestion in the small intestine, leaving it available for bacteria to ferment in the colon. Psyllium appears to slow that fermentation, allowing the starch to travel farther into the colon before bacteria break it down. The researchers believe this enhances the production of beneficial metabolites, substances the bacteria produce as they process the starch.

“For many people with IBS, success has traditionally meant simply avoiding certain foods,” said ur gut® co-creator Associate Professor Claus Christophersen.

“Our findings suggest we may also be able to actively rebuild gut health and the microbiome while not making IBS symptoms worse. That’s an important step forward.”

Reference: “Dietary fiber blend improves gut health in patients with irritable bowel syndrome following a low FODMAP diet: a randomized, double-blind control trial” by Ran Yan, Amanda Devine, Johnny Lo, Evania Marlow, Ian C. Dunican, Kanita Kunaratnam, Lesley Andrew and Claus T. Christophersen, 24 September 2026, European Journal of Nutrition. DOI: 10.1007/s00394-026-04113-5

The index is near a record, the AI trade is wobbling, and every headline wants you to decide whether this is the top. Nobody knows, and the portfolios that can thrive during the long stretches of uncertainty share one trait: They include a healthy helping of reliable dividend payers.

The three consumer companies below have paid and raised their dividends through recessions, pandemics, and the bursting of more than one market bubble. Two of them are in the middle of the biggest deals in their histories. All three sell things people keep restocking for their homes in good months and bad.

In June, discount retail giant Target (TGT +0.29%) increased its quarterly dividend by 1.8% to $1.16 per share, making 2026 the 55th straight year it has raised its payout. At the current share price of near $163, that works out to a yield just over 3%, but the dividend still consumes less than half of its earnings, which leaves the company plenty of room to conduct share buybacks and invest in the stores themselves.

The reason Target belongs in retail investors' portfolios is not the yield alone. It is that the company keeps acting like a retailer that expects to be around for another 55 years. This fall, it relaunched Threshold, its house brand for home goods, finished a full remodel of its SoHo store in Manhattan, and promoted a shopping list showing that folks could pull together a Thanksgiving dinner for six in its stores for just $30.

The chain's dividend-hiking streak started in 1971, which means Target raised its payouts through every market the S&P 500 has produced since Richard Nixon was president.

Spice and sauce powerhouse McCormick (MKC -3.32%) is paying a quarterly dividend of $0.48 per share, up from $0.45 per share a year ago, and has raised its dividend annually for 40 consecutive years. From its 2020 peak above $100, the stock has fallen to about $48 today, which pushed its yield up to nearly 4%, the highest it has been in a generation.

What has investors nervous this year -- and what contributed to its year-to-date decline of around 28% in the stock -- is McCormick's pending combination with Unilever's foods business, which the two companies announced in March. That deal would create a company with about $20 billion in annual revenue, with roughly $600 million in expected cost savings, according to Reuters. It's slated to close by mid-2027.

Meanwhile, McCormick's existing business keeps doing what it does. Third-quarter sales rose 17%, gross margin expanded by 190 basis points, and McCormick de Mexico, which it acquired a majority interest in back in January, is already integrated. Spices and sauces are among the least cyclical products in the grocery store -- even in a recession, people are still going to season their food. So McCormick is the kind of business you want dishing out quarterly payments to you while the market argues about chip stocks.

Sysco (SYY +2.15%) has raised its dividend for 57 straight years and now pays $0.55 per share each quarter. Because its streak of hikes has hit the 50-year mark, it has earned the rare title of Dividend King. It's also the most ambitious of these three companies right now. In March, it agreed to buy Restaurant Depot, the cash-and-carry chain where independent restaurant owners go to load up on a wide array of the items they require, from paper towels to chicken. That deal moves Sysco into a higher-margin channel that it previously didn't have.

Sysco already delivers to more than 700,000 customer locations, and every one of those food service locations needs its trucks to show up every week, regardless of whether the S&P 500 is up or down. That is my whole case. The company reported its fiscal 2027 first-quarter results on Nov. 3 and held its full-year guidance steady. Its payout ratio of around 60% is high for a distributor, but Sysco has sustained that for years. Add the Restaurant Depot deal, and you have a reliable dividend grower that is also, for the first time in a while, a growth stock story.

Target gets you the consumer, McCormick gets you the pantry, and Sysco gets you every restaurant in between. None of them are exciting, which is actually the point. To me, the right way to own them is to make them the ballast in a portfolio that also holds the growth names everyone is nervous about this month.

If November goes badly for Wall Street, these three will pay you anyway. If it goes well, you will own three companies that have been raising their dividends since before many of today's investors were born.

Building Reliable iOS Apps With AI Agents

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Senior iOS engineer at Uber. Indie app developer. 8+ years of professional experience.

One year ago I wrote Breaking Free from Xcode: Unlock AI's Full Potential in iOS. At that time, getting an agent to build, run and check an iOS app took a pile of MCP servers, VS Code extensions and some patience.

A lot has changed since. Models are much smarter. Xcode ships with agents built in. Claude Code can control the simulator on its own: it builds, launches, taps through screens and checks the result with screenshots.

For prototypes and MVPs, mostly yes. You can describe an idea and get the app “one-shotted”. But if your software has to be as reliable as running water (a phrase I borrow from Uber's mission: "transportation as reliable as running water, everywhere, for everyone"), two principles still matter: design and harness.

This is the biggest shift I've seen. While building a feature, I don't keep in my head which method calls which method, which file it lives in, or how state moves through each type. The agent handles that.

That doesn't mean you can stop reviewing code. Where quality matters, you still read PRs. But your attention moves up a level. You own the design of the whole system, not just one module, so architecture is now the most valuable skill you have.

At the design level you are pretty high in the hierarchy of components, that’s why visualization helps. A diagram with the right amount of detail shows how the pieces fit together, which is hard to see in plain text. The C4 model gives you that visualization at a few zoom levels.

Last year I suggested putting the project architecture in AGENTS.md. I've changed my mind: a dedicated docs/architecture/ folder is a better place. You don't need the entire architecture loaded into the context every time you do something. Instead, AGENTS.md keeps a reference to docs/architecture/context.md, so the agent can retrieve it when on demand. Luis Herrera's deep dive walks through C4 levels on a sample system.

System Context. Your app, its users and the outside systems it talks to: the backend, push notifications, analytics. This is context.md, and it links to the containers.

Containers. Conventional C4 container usually stands for a piece of software that can de deployed, like a micro-service or app. I had to adapt the original idea: a container is a feature, a flow or an SDK module, like Feed, Checkout or Networking. containers.md shows how they connect, explains what each one owns and what it may depend on, and links to components where they exist.

Components. The parts inside a container. Only draw these for complex areas, like a post composer with drafts, uploads and retries, or a sync engine. A stateless FeedService with a single getFeed() call doesn't need a component diagram. C4 itself says you only need the levels "that add value".

Code. No diagrams. Component docs just link to the specific files. The code is the source of truth, and an agent can read it faster than you can draw it.

Think about C4 as a pattern that generalizes an approach to structure progressive complexity of the architecture.

Models are far more capable now, but they still need guidance. I regularly see agents get confused by strict concurrency and SwiftUI performance. The code compiles and the tests pass but the produced code can crash, that why your hard skills still matter.
