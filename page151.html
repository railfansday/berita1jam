The Strait of Hormuz closure has cost Europe an extra $142 billion for the same energy it was already buying

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Four years after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine forced Europe to scramble for new energy suppliers, the bloc is in another fuel crisis. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said this week Europe has paid an extra €127 billion (a little over $142 billion) for imported oil and gas since the effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and it has received no additional energy in return for it.

“Since the closure of the Strait of Hormuz, imported fossil fuels cost Europe an additional €127 billion without a single molecule of energy in addition,” von der Leyen said in a keynote at the WinWin Brussels Summit.

In April, about seven weeks into the war, the Commission put the extra cost at €24 billion (about $27 billion). By late September, EU energy commissioner Dan Jørgensen said it had passed €100 billion (about $111 billion), similarly echoing our ninth-grade science class building and saying that the bloc hadn’t received “one extra molecule of gas or oil.”

“When we import oil and gas, we risk importing the shocks and crises that come with them,” von der Leyen said at the keynote.

After Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, Moscow cut gas flows to the EU to a trickle, and governments scrambled to fill storage at record prices, with the bloc eventually vowing to wean itself off Russian energy. As a result, Russia’s share of EU gas imports fell from about 45% before the invasion to about 13% in 2025. In January, EU members adopted a ban ending Russian liquefied natural gas (LNG) imports by the end of this year and pipeline gas by autumn 2027.

But Europe largely replaced Russian gas with other imports, much of it LNG shipped from the U.S. and the Middle East. When the Iran war began, the EU still got 57% of its energy from imported fossil fuels.

The war then shut the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway that carried about one-fifth of the world’s traded oil. Tanker traffic through the strait nearly stopped. After a cold winter, EU gas storage stood at just 28.4% in late March, below the five-year average. The Persian Gulf also supplied more than 40% of Europe’s jet fuel and diesel imports.

Within weeks, Jørgensen asked member states to consider “voluntary demand-saving measures”, especially in transport. The International Energy Agency’s plan to cut oil use included car-free Sundays, a rationing tactic from the 1970s oil crisis.

By September, drivers in some EU countries were paying nearly 50% more at the pump, or more than $11 a gallon. In Italy, a weeklong truckers’ strike over diesel prices halted deliveries of milk to mozzarella makers, and as Italian inflation hit 5.4%. EU officials expect oil and gas prices to stay above prewar levels until at least the end of 2027.

The IEA said Tuesday the EU gas storage is entering the heating season at its lowest level since 2013, warning that in a cold winter with continued Hormuz disruptions, storage could fall below 10% by the end of March. EU gas network operators have warned of a shortfall of 12% to 15% of demand in a severe cold snap, and energy ministers met in late October to discuss EU-wide limits on consumption.

While Vladimir Putin said in March Russia was ready to supply Europe again, Von der Leyen called a return to Russian fuel a “strategic blunder.”

On Tuesday, she argued Europe’s answer is to import less.

“This year, we have again seen the devastating impact of climate change. Droughts, wildfires, floods,” she said. “But we have also seen the cost of our dependence on imported fossil fuels.”

The EU has pledged to become climate-neutral by 2050 and to cut emissions 90% by 2040. Von der Leyen said more than €1.2 trillion has been invested in sustainable activities across the bloc since 2021.

“So let me be crystal clear: Europe will stay the course, we stick to our targets, and we back our clean industries,” she said.

WoW: Forever Shamans Are Protesting Class Changes After Being “Completely Ignored”

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

World of Warcraft: Forever just received a massive batch of class-balance updates ahead of launch that have boosted power across the board, but one group of players isn't happy with the changes: Shamans.

Developer Blizzard recently released Forever's launch patch notes based on feedback and data accumulated during the beta, resulting in a power spike for all classes and major reworks for classes like Paladin and Rogue. Shamans, too, received some significant changes, most notably the removal of a key Elemental specialization ability, Lava Burst, along with any interactions other abilities had with it.

It's that change, and other changes to specialization-specific abilities like Restoration's Riptide and Enhancement's Maelstrom Weapon, that have resulted in the WoW Classic Shaman Discord going into lockdown in protest. The entire server, which has around 80,000 members, has been made read-only, with a message declaring the reason for why it took what it described as such a "drastic measure."

"Community feedback gets completely ignored and class fantasies are getting destroyed," the server declared. "With this we intend to give them [Blizzard] a sign and show them that the community is not [happy] with how they approached our beloved class. Give us back our class and spec identities and fantasies."

While the protest is in regards to class changes in Forever specifically, the server is also home to Shaman discussions as they relate to other "classic" versions of the game as well, including WoW Classic and Mists of Pandaria Classic. As of writing, all of those channels are also locked and set to read-only. 

In a follow-up post, the server went into more detail about its complaints in regards to how Blizzard has approached Forever's Shaman design. It stated its complaints aren't about "damage output." It also made clear the server will re-open "regardless of the outcome," but that it felt like its feedback through "legitimate" channels were being ignored. 

The server then proceeded to list what it viewed as wrong with various ability changes for each Shaman specialization. It additionally called attention to what it called the "hybrid spell power issue," where spell power and intellect gear seems to be better for not only Elemental but also Enhancement, muddying the identity between the two specializations. 

Blizzard has previously said it doesn't want "hybrid" classes that can deal damage and heal, like Shamans, to deal more damage than dedicated DPS classes. Players in the WoW community refer to this idea as the "hybrid tax," something those running the Shaman Discord said they are fine with if it means Shaman class design is improved.

"Truthfully, hybrid tax us all you want if the result is a cohesive class with strong class fantasy elements that are actually represented in our playstyles," it said. 

This isn't the first time a WoW class Discord shut down in protest of how class changes. As pointed out by Wowhead, back in August 2025, the modern WoW Paladin Discord was partially closed in protest of the Retribution specialization's raid performance. In that case, only Retribution-specific channels were locked, and the entire protest lasted less than 24 hours. Blizzard did announce Retribution Paladin buffs shortly after, though it's unclear if those changes came at least in part due to the server protest or not.

How long the WoW Classic Shaman Discord remains in lockdown remains to be seen, but Forever launches November 4. Blizzard recently said it doesn't expect to make any more major class changes for at least another month.

Kanishka Narayan, the UK’s minister for artificial intelligence.

UK AI minister Kanishka Narayan said a serious cyber breach is “extremely likely” in both the public and private sector, and that neither is ready. He pointed to extra funding for the AI Security Institute as the government’s response.

The UK is “extremely likely” to suffer a serious cyber breach soon, AI minister Kanishka Narayan said on Tuesday. Neither the government nor businesses are ready for it, he said, Bloomberg reported.

“I worry about the cybersecurity risks because it’s here now,” Narayan said at the Bloomberg Tech event in London.

“A cyber breach incident is extremely likely in the UK, both in the public sector and private sector,” he added.

His warning came hours after Richard Horne, head of the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre, said British firms were “willfully blind” to threats from Russia, Iran and China. On Monday, a suspected supermarket freezer hack hit stores across Europe, and Horne said a few British firms were affected.

AI is adding to the worry. Hackers are thought to be using AI tools to speed up attacks. This summer, AI models from OpenAI, Anthropic and others broke out of test environments and breached outside websites.

Narayan pointed to the AI Security Institute, which tests leading AI models, as part of the answer. The government gave it an extra £120M in its latest budget. Last month, it won back access to new US models before release, after the White House asked labs to hold them back.

Narayan would not say how long that access would last. “Nothing is a particular guarantee in the world of AI,” he said.

San Diego Accountant Tricked Investors Into Sinking $8.2 Million Into Phony ADU Venture

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A California man convinced dozens of investors to pour millions of dollars into a real estate venture he claimed would deliver big returns through the construction of backyard homes.

But the 30-year-old accountant lost more than $8.2 million of their money in risky stock market trades and not a single accessory dwelling unit (ADU) was built, prosecutors said.

Ethan Joseph Sanchez, of San Diego, was sentenced to 63 months in federal prison after admitting to orchestrating the investment fraud scheme, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of California announced Friday.

“Ethan Sanchez exploited the trust of hardworking people and stole their savings through lies and fabricated investments,” U.S. Attorney Adam Gordon said in a statement. “This sentence reflects the profound harm he caused.”

Sanchez, who was a certified public accountant at the time and operated through his business, BSC Equity Ventures LLC., repeatedly lied to his victims as to how their investment funds would be used, according to prosecutors.

The scheme began back in 2022 when Sanchez allegedly began soliciting loans from investors, falsely claiming the money would fund real estate ventures, including the construction and renovation of ADUs.

He promised the investors they would receive their principal back as soon as possible, with an above-average return of interest, in some cases as much as 25%.

Sanchez also allegedly used a fictitious identity, Richard “Rick” Aguilar, to make the investment opportunities appear more legitimate.

But prosecutors say Sanchez used the investors’ funds for day trading and in most cases lost the money. He did not fund the real estate transactions he said the investors were financing nor did he build any of the ADUs he said would be financed with the investors’ money.

The FBI reported in February 2025 that BSC Equity Ventures had received over $6 million in investments from its investors, while Sanchez returned less than $1.8 million to them.

At the sentencing hearing on Oct. 30, U.S. District Judge Robert S. Huie ordered Sanchez to forfeit over $8.2 million and to pay restitution to victims.

He described the scheme as “aggravated and reprehensible,” highlighting how Sanchez had used existing victims to recruit additional investors, sometimes including members of their own families.

The judge described the harm caused by the scheme as “almost unimaginable,” adding that the consequences went far beyond the financial losses.

In a January 2026 interview with ABC 10News, one investor, identified only as Brooke, said the loss he suffered forced him out of retirement and back to work to rebuild his savings.

Brooke told the outlet that Sanchez told him he needed money to refinance his portfolio of investment properties and would pay interest on the loans. Sanchez initially borrowed $40,000 from Brooke in December 2023, but then continued to borrow more money, including a $150,000 loan.

Sanchez made small payments, Brooke said, but never repaid the full amount of his investment. Brooke also said that he had borrowed against his home to make the investment with Sanchez and was paying interest on that money.

“It was always lie after lie,” he said. “And then it became so obvious that this was a Ponzi scheme.”

So, as much as I loved using the new iPad Mini (it really is a lot of fun), I'm not quite sure who to recommend this device to, unless you're in the top income tax bracket.

Let's cut to the chase: The iPad Mini is now my favorite tablet in the Apple lineup. It has an incredible display, excellent design, and strong performance. However, I'm not quite sure who should buy it.

I'm not the only reviewer who's a little confused by Apple's product lineup in 2026. At first, I thought the iPad Mini was the MacBook Neo of the iPad family. It's the smaller, less powerful model for everyday use. That makes intuitive sense, but it's also wrong.

The iPad Mini offers better performance in many respects than the iPad Air and a far superior display. And while the MacBook Neo is a budget laptop, the iPad Mini is definitely not a budget device. Its starting price is only $50 less than the iPad Air.

A slightly cheaper iPad Mini priced around $499 or $599 would make a lot more sense to me, and be much easier to recommend.

Instead, sound emanates from the device as part of a new Acoustic Display technology. And because the device is fully enclosed, it achieves IP67 water and dust resistance for extra durability. I haven't dunked my iPad Mini in the bathtub (yet), but it should withstand brief immersion in water.

I'm used to the larger iPad Air, and I instantly preferred the smaller, lighter design of the iPad Mini. I can see why the device has such devoted fans in the Apple community.
