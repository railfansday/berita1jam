📅 2026-11-16  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
HLTH 2026: CVS Health, Cigna and Abridge collaborating with OpenAI to test computer-use agents" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

LAS VEGAS—OpenAI is expanding computer-use agents for healthcare, enabling AI to navigate electronic health record systems, payer portals and other software to tackle repetitive and manual tasks.

Computer-use agents, also called computer-using agents, are AI systems designed to interact with software interfaces the same way a human does—by viewing a screen, moving a cursor, clicking buttons and typing text.

Research teams at OpenAI have been exploring how computer-use agents can assist healthcare teams with fragmented clinical and administrative workflows while keeping people in control, Karan Singhal, head of Health AI and Artificial General Intelligence at OpenAI, told Fierce Healthcare.

"Around six months ago we set a research bet that we thought that the abilities for agents to use computers would end up being helpful and reliable enough in healthcare to help people with clinical and administrative workflows," Singhal said in an exclusive interview sharing evaluation results on OpenAI's early work with computer-use agents in healthcare.

Singhal also shed light on how OpenAI is working with CVS Health, Cigna Healthcare, Abridge and Ambience to apply computer-use agents to specific workflows, including referrals, prior authorizations, discharge planning, specialist consults and care manager preparation.

Healthcare systems are often disconnected, forcing administrative staff and care teams to manually move information from one system to another before a patient can get care. If a clinician orders an MRI, for example, the insurer may need information from the patient’s medical record, but a staff member still has to pull that information and enter it into a separate portal to request authorization.

OpenAI executives and researchers framed computer use as the next step in healthcare AI, moving beyond copilots that draft or summarize information to agents that can actually navigate EHRs, payer portals and other applications on a user's behalf.

Computer-use agents can bridge healthcare's fragmented software environment, especially where application programming interfaces (APIs) and other integrations don't exist or don't connect an entire workflow, Singhal noted.

"We have been working with health systems on things like ChatGPT for Healthcare and our integration with Epic, but there are still limitations in how useful AI agents can be today. Computer use is really a way of resolving a lot of these barriers that exist between AI capabilities and healthcare software so that care teams can deliver better care, more effectively and more efficiently," he said.

OpenAI continues to invest in APIs, plugins and MCP connectors to integrate AI with healthcare software, but the industry’s fragmented and legacy technology can make those integrations slow and incomplete, Singhal said. Computer use can help bridge those gaps by allowing AI to work across existing software interfaces and take on manual, repetitive workflows.

"What we hope to do with this release is move us closer to a world where, because AI is connected to more services, AI can help more with a lot of these workflows," he noted.

Care teams, developers and individual clinicians can access computer use for healthcare through ChatGPT for Healthcare, ChatGPT for Clinicians and the OpenAI API.

In a research demonstration using synthetic patient data, OpenAI showed how a computer-use agent could support a clinician and an authorization specialist working on the same MRI request. In the demonstration video, a patient messages their clinician about lower back pain. The clinician then instructs the computer-use agent to review the patient’s visit notes, physical therapy records and prior imaging in the EHR. The computer-use agent drafts an MRI order, an encounter note and a reply to the patient for the clinician to review.

In the same demo video, the agent then uses the same records to prepare a prior authorization request in the insurer’s portal for an authorization specialist to review.

"The clinician and authorization specialist were both completely in control of their workflow, but the computer-use agent was able to help across different parts of the workflow and translate that into action," Singhal noted about the demo video.

OpenAI evaluated GPT‑6 Astra on tasks clinicians handle in their EHR inboxes. These tasks, shaped by physician input, require Astra to carry out multi-step workflows designed to test both clinical reasoning over longitudinal patient context and navigation of complex interfaces. Reviewers examined the model’s actions alongside the notes, orders, and patient messages it produced, assessing whether the work would be helpful and clinically safe in the context of the task, according to an OpenAI blog post.

OpenAI says its GPT-6 Astra model can perform multi-step EHR tasks such as reviewing patient records, drafting messages and notes, preparing medication refills and proposing orders, while leaving key actions for clinician review. In OpenAI's evaluation, 99% of 325 reviewed tasks were rated clinically safe, while physicians rated the model "very helpful" or "extremely helpful" in 93% of 646 assessments.

Clinical safety was assessed by evaluating whether inaccuracies, omissions or actions in the model’s work could plausibly lead to patient harm, OpenAI said.

Regarding the 1% of tasks rated not clinically safe, Singal said researchers did not identify any cases where the model created a specific, plausible risk of patient harm. "In some cases, the model might overreach and might take an action without getting the proper sign-off or authorization," he said.

"The models are not perfect today, and what we're sharing is an update on research results where we're seeing this critical point where the models seem to be useful in practice," he added.

OpenAI researchers also evaluated computer-use agents for administrative tasks, such as prior authorizations, appeals and medical equipment orders.

OpenAI designed its computer-use agents with robust safeguards and data security in place, according to the company. The agents can be configured with limits on system access and actions, and audit logs provide a clear record of agent actions and approvals, giving healthcare teams visibility into how each workflow was completed.

Organizations are developing, piloting and deploying computer-use applications across a range of workflows and administrative tasks.

CVS Health is using OpenAI’s computer-use capabilities to automate repetitive work across clinical systems, including sending documents, processing referrals and supporting specialist consults. In one production workflow, an agent cleared a months-long document-sending backlog over a weekend, OpenAI detailed in the blog post. Another agent handles the system steps for tens of thousands of consults each day.

Generative AI company Abridge is building computer-use agents to help clinicians carry out care plans across use cases and applications they use daily, including those without APIs. The company also plans to develop an inpatient use case, adding EHR information and nurse and clinician context to identify what needs attention and to coordinate next steps. As an example, agents can work concurrently on medication access and home health coordination, using computer interaction alongside calls and messages.

Cigna Healthcare is using OpenAI’s models to pull together member information from more than 10 clinical and administrative systems, including benefits, providers, appointments and previous interactions. The goal is to reduce the time case managers spend searching across systems and give them more time to support members, OpenAI wrote in the blog post.

Companies including Assort, Bunkerhill, Forus, Gravity and Tennr are testing agents that interact directly with EHRs and payer portals for referral management, authorization checks, medication approvals and other operational tasks.

“We're continuing to invest in the models, making the models better at computer use. An important way that we think that they could continue to improve is around improved latency and costs,” Singhal noted.

OpenAI sees its role in healthcare as building foundational AI capabilities and letting healthcare companies handle the “last mile” of implementation, Singhal noted. Computer use is one example: OpenAI provides the technology and safeguards, while partners apply it to specialized workflows and deploy it within healthcare organizations.

The company also announced today improvements to trusted clinical search in ChatGPT for Healthcare and ChatGPT for Clinicians, including how ChatGPT finds, evaluates and cites medical evidence. OpenAI also expanded its healthcare plugin ecosystem with more than 20 new healthcare plugins from companies across clinical care, care coordination and business operations.

LAS VEGAS—Ambient clinical intelligence platform Suki announced Monday the launch of an agentic operating system designed to connect fragmented healthcare technology.

The operating system—dubbed Suki OS—is built on three core pillars, the company says: a partner ecosystem, unified artificial intelligence agents and a performance guarantee. It plans to expand the capabilities beyond its Suki for Clinicians platform over time.

Suki is introducing two initial AI agent initiatives as part of its goal to reduce administrative work.

Suki Prior Authorization will streamline the process by identifying requirements and gathering supporting clinical documentation. Its Clinical Documentation Improvement (CDI) Agent will focus on identifying documentation gaps, improving accuracy and supporting downstream coding and revenue cycle workflows.

Additional agents are planned across clinical, operational and financial workflows as part of the company’s broader strategy to develop agents that can execute complex workflows with human oversight.

Suki’s Partner Ecosystem is comprised of its Insight Partners, which integrate intelligence with Suki patient context, and Platform Partners, which let health tech companies embed Suki’s AI directly into their products.

The Suki Guarantee offers eligible customers up to a 100% refund if agreed-upon performance targets aren't met, the company says.

Suki CEO and Founder Punit Soni told Fierce Healthcare that healthcare has an "execution problem" rather than an AI shortage.

"We've spent years building technology that can understand clinical conversations, but understanding is only the beginning," Soni said. "The real opportunity is to turn that intelligence into action, connecting the systems, people, and workflows that make healthcare run."

That’s the motivation behind Suki OS, according to Soni.

"Nearly a decade ago, we pioneered ambient AI, and today our technology is trusted by more than 450 healthcare organizations, with independently validated clinical and financial outcomes," Soni said. "We've built deep clinical intelligence, integrations across EHRs, and an expansive partner ecosystem. That foundation uniquely positions Suki to move beyond documentation and help automate the complex work that surrounds patient care."

Soni said the next generation of AI tools have to "do more than save time" as health systems face mounting financial pressures and workforce constraints. "It has to deliver meaningful clinical and financial outcomes," he said.

Soni continued, "Ultimately, the measure of AI's success won't be how many agents we build. It will be how much complexity we remove from healthcare, how much time we give back to clinicians and their patients, and how much measurable financial value we create for the organizations delivering care."

In October, the company secured $90 million in series E funding to accelerate its growth and continue expanding its AI capabilities.

The company has raised more than $250 million to date, executives say. Patient encounters supported by its tools grew more than 190% year over year in 2026, with revenue doubling in 2025 and on track for similar growth this year.

LAS VEGAS—Artificial intelligence-driven disease detection and intelligent care platform Viz.ai announced Monday a strategic partnership with Wolters Kluwer Health to integrate the Viz.ai One platform into UpToDate Expert AI.

Through the partnership, providers caring for patients with complex conditions will be able to ask detailed questions and receive evidence-based answers relevant to clinical context from UpToDate directly within existing workflows.

“The greatest leap we can achieve in healthcare is to make the best medical evidence always available, applied automatically to every patient, at the moment it matters,” said Chris Mansi, Viz.ai CEO and co-founder, in a statement to Fierce Healthcare.

Mansi continued, “Bringing UpToDate’s trusted evidence into Viz.ai’s workflow takes us closer to that vision: an AI safety net that detects disease, helps clinicians identify who needs care and empower them to act on the evidence. Our goal is to close the gap between what medicine knows and the care each patient receives, so no patient is left behind.”

The companies say it is the first integration to bring UpToDate into AI-driven care coordination workflows.

Wolters Kluwer CEO Greg Samios said in a statement that one of healthcare AI’s biggest opportunities is "to help clinicians see more of the patient's full picture when making healthcare decisions."

“Viz.ai's technology can proactively flag signs of disease and alert care teams, even before a condition is suspected,” Samios said. “Paired with UpToDate's trusted clinical intelligence, clinicians have more of the information they need, directly in their workflow, to help inform care decisions.”

While 72% of health tech startups lead with “AI-powered” in their descriptors, zero surveyed venture capitalists in a new report from HLTH and SolComms said the phrase increased their confidence.

Independently surveyed health tech reporters also ranked “AI-powered” as one of the most overused phrases in pitching–in addition to “revolutionizing/transforming” and “disrupting.”

Alongside these survey responses, researchers compared–and scored–the messaging of 72 U.S.-based, venture-backed health tech startups at series A to series C for the “Healthtech Attention Economy Report.” Startups’ messaging across their website and social media was scored on four main dimensions: clarity, differentiation, proof and policy awareness.

SolComms Vice President of HealthTech Isabella Morreale said in a statement the companies that will get attention within the next year “are leading with proof, not adjectives.”

“That means speaking to clinical data, measurable outcomes, and validation from third parties,” Morreale said. “Founder-led storytelling will also continue to stand out. Among the data, one common through-thread for investor and reporter interest was founders with a real point of view on the industry.”

Morreale continued, “Reporters don't need another company description; they need a source who can help them explain what's happening in healthcare.”

Less than half (42.2%) of patients say communications from healthcare providers “often” or “always” feel relevant to their needs, a new report from Lirio found.

Analysts at the behavioral science and AI-powered behavioral intelligence platform polled 1,500 adults across the U.S. for the survey. While 58% of respondents report communications not consistently feeling relevant to their needs, 55.6% rate overall communication experiences with healthcare organizations as “good” or “excellent.”

When asked why healthcare communications are ignored altogether, respondents report generic or irrelevant content (24.4%), too many similar messages (21.8%) and confusing, jargon-filled messages (18.8%) as top reasons.

Nearly half of respondents (47.9%) report the potential value of more personalized, better-timed messages as an “8, 9 or 10” for how much it could help them make better care decisions and stay engaged.

Lirio Chief Behavioral Officer Amy Bucher, Ph.D., said in a statement patients “are not rejecting healthcare communications outright.”

“They are telling us, in specific and measurable terms, that the system is talking at them instead of to them,” Bucher said. "The instinct in healthcare has long been to solve engagement with more outreach - more emails, more reminders, more portals. This data shows that volume is not the problem. What is missing is relevance — content and timing designed for where a specific patient is in their own health journey, rather than a single message broadcast to an entire population.”

Bucher added the healthcare organizations that close the communication gap will be “the ones that use behavioral data to determine the right message, the right channel, and the right moment for each individual patient, which is the model Lirio was built to deliver."

Community-based specialty palliative care provider Vynca is expanding its reach into four more states.

Patients will now be able to access Vynca’s services in Georgia, Hawaii, New Jersey and Texas. Vynca is one of the first practices approved to provide services under New Jersey’s Community-Based Palliative Care Medicaid benefit, launched in April, according to the Nov. 16 announcement.

Founded in 2013, the company’s hybrid care model combines in-home visits with virtual clinical support and predictive technology, and executives say it has shown 43% fewer emergency department visits and 52% less hospital stays within six months of enrollment.

“Health plan and government program leaders understand the value of community-based palliative care for people with serious illness,” said Darren Schulte, M.D., Vynca CEO, in a statement. “Our expansion in four key states reflects growing momentum to expand specialty outpatient palliative care outside of hospices. The key is to broaden access and convenience for the entire eligible population by offering home-based options. Too often, patients are limited to seeking palliative care when available in the community, at hospital-based clinics and facilities.”

Personal Branding From the Buyer's Side of the Desk: What Clients Actually Check Before Hiring

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 16 • Originally published at tanit365.com

Most advice about personal branding is written from the freelancer's side of the desk: pick a niche, post consistently, grow an audience. This piece flips the camera around and looks at the same problem from the buyer's side — what a potential client actually scans for in the ten minutes between opening your profile and deciding whether to reply, hire, or move on. The original guide on tanit365.com lays out the full twelve-week system; here we focus on the handful of details that decide most real hiring decisions.

A client evaluating two freelancers is not comparing follower counts. They are running one silent question: has this person solved a problem like mine, and can they show it? A profile with twelve thoughtful posts and no documented work loses to a thin profile with two detailed case studies.

So before touching any platform, build an evidence inventory: every project you have finished, written down as situation, problem, action, measurable outcome, and duration. Even two modest projects documented this way give a buyer more to evaluate than a month of posting. If you have no client work yet, document one self-initiated or discounted project in public — one real teardown with numbers beats weeks of opinion posts, because evidence is the only content a buyer can verify.

Strip away the branding language and a hiring decision is a short checklist, usually run unconsciously:

Notice that four of the five are answered by artifacts, not activity. Publishing helps, but it is the last mile, not the road.

The three-page home base from the original guide works because it mirrors how buyers scan. Order the sections the way a client reads: positioning statement first, proof second, terms third, contact last. Two details most freelancers omit and buyers quietly look for:

Technical notes that matter more than they seem to: a custom domain rather than a platform subdomain, a page that loads fast on a phone because most first visits are mobile, and a visible email address, since some corporate buyers cannot submit external forms from their networks. A plain static page on affordable hosting does the whole job; spending a week choosing a framework is a week of positioning and proof not being built.

The cadence advice in the original guide — one substantial piece every two weeks plus short updates, sustainable inside three to five hours a week — survives contact with reality for one reason: reuse. Freelancers who publish consistently are usually not writing more than anyone else; they are repurposing better. One case study becomes a short update, a section of the work page, an answer in a client conversation, and a paragraph in a proposal.

A twelve-week cycle makes this measurable. Review at weeks four, eight, and twelve — but from the buyer's side the only review question that matters is: which pieces produced enquiries? Shift toward those. If eight weeks of consistent publishing produces no enquiries at all, the problem is almost never effort; it is that the positioning answers a question nobody is asking. Narrow the audience until the sentence makes a specific person think "that is me."

The brand moment most freelancers neglect is the first reply. Answer enquiries within one business day, and make the reply itself evidence: one line acknowledging the project, two questions about scope and deadline, one proposed call slot. Asking about the outcome, deadline, and budget range before sending a price turns the conversation from "is it too expensive" into "which option fits" — and lets you reduce scope instead of price when budget pushes back. Cutting price on the same scope teaches the client your first number was decorative.

At the moment of delivery, while goodwill peaks, ask for two things: a short testimonial and one introduction to someone with a similar problem. Those two requests replace most cold outreach a freelancer would otherwise do next quarter.

Working signals: qualified enquiries that mention your positioning language back to you, enquiries arriving from the proof pages rather than the profile, and clients referencing a specific case study. Noise: impressions, follower growth, and engagement on posts that never mentions your service. A brand that produces applause but no enquiries is entertainment, not marketing; tighten the positioning sentence and publish the price anchor before posting anything new.

Social platforms rent you attention and can change the rent overnight. The durable part of a personal brand is the home base — the domain, the case studies, the record of finished work. Keep it small, keep it fast, keep it yours, and update it on the twelve-week rhythm rather than rebuilding it every quarter. That is what makes the right client find you, verify you, and pay you without a bidding war.

The full twelve-week system — positioning statement, channel choice, page structure, cadence, conversion path, and review checkpoints — is on tanit365.com. This article is an independent companion piece written for the dev.to community; no syndicated text, all angles re-examined from the buyer's side.

How long before a personal brand actually produces clients? Plan on months, not weeks: the first stretch builds the habit and the home base, the middle builds search and referral surface, and enquiries usually start once the proof inventory is deep enough for a buyer to evaluate you in one sitting.

One platform or many? Two channels: one discovery, one credibility, typically your own site. Five half-active profiles read as an incomplete story; two consistent ones read as a professional.

What if I have no client results to show? Publish process and trade-off content, and get one documented project — even free or discounted — in exchange for the right to write it up. That single project generates more enquiries than a quarter of opinion posts.

Auditable AI billing without a blockchain: signed batches and their limits

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 16 • Originally published at teerouter.hashnode.dev

The difficult part of AI API billing is often the request that almost finished. The router sent the prompt, the provider began responding, and the connection disappeared before final usage arrived. A database can store a precise number for that request without making the number well justified.

I am building TeeRouter, and this led to two separate design questions: what evidence supports a charge, and how can someone later detect changes to the published accounting records?

The implementation borrows a useful idea associated with blockchains: publish cryptographically verifiable records that readers can inspect independently. It does not run a blockchain or claim decentralized consensus. This article examines the public source at commit 5c89b551; it is an implementation case study, not an independent certification of the deployed service.

A receipt should preserve the pricing decision used for that request. Looking up today's price when displaying last week's usage makes historical charges difficult to reproduce.

TeeRouter freezes the pricing revision, applicable rules, and pool multiplier at admission. Provider-specific calculators interpret native usage fields. The basis is public API list pricing multiplied by the pool's multiplier, not a claim about the merchant's actual subscription expenditure or negotiated invoice.

The billing implementation uses integer arithmetic, retains pre-rounding amounts, and rounds upward once per request to micro-USD. This makes the chosen calculation reproducible without promising to match a provider's monthly invoice rounding, taxes, or discounts.

For a simple illustrative quote, 10,000 ordinary input tokens at $2 per million plus 1,000 output tokens at $8 per million produces a $0.028 base cost. A 1.2 multiplier gives $0.0336. These are made-up rates, not a current model quotation. Real records must also distinguish cached input, applicable pricing dimensions, and how each provider reports usage.

The interrupted-request policy separates several outcomes:

A generated refusal inside a successful model response is different from an HTTP rejection: generation may have consumed billable tokens.

The controversial row is the timeout after a full flush. A successful local flush does not prove that the remote application received or executed the request. The policy chooses a financial treatment for that ambiguity; it does not eliminate the ambiguity. Customers need to know that an estimated input charge can occur at this boundary.

Estimation is restricted to failed or interrupted model requests and is labelled estimated or mixed, with partial coverage. Unknown hidden output is not invented. A downstream cancellation cancels the upstream operation rather than draining it in the background, although closing a connection cannot guarantee the provider immediately stops computing.

Publishing these distinctions matters more than making every row appear confidently “settled.” A signed estimate remains an estimate.

The current public receipt format signs batches of records. A manifest includes the boot and key identity, batch number, previous hash, sequence range, record digest, count, and calculated total.

Conceptually, verification follows this chain:

There is one signature per batch. The current public format does not have per-record Merkle inclusion proofs. A reader needs the original complete records_json to verify the records; reserializing JSON may change the bytes being checked.

The verifier distinguishes a valid signature from an independently trusted TEE signing identity. The latter also requires hardware evidence and an approved reference policy. It is not established by a public key or a “verified” label included in the downloaded bundle.

Hash links let readers check continuity between supplied batches. They do not create consensus, prove that an operator has disclosed the latest batch, or prove that no other history exists. Independently retaining known batch heads could strengthen monitoring, but that is a separate system property, not something the signature supplies automatically.

Consider a batch that reaches external storage, followed by a lost acknowledgment. Regenerating it with new metadata would complicate duplicate detection. TeeRouter freezes the signed bytes before upload and resends those same bytes on a later cycle.

A valid durable-storage acknowledgment permits releasing the uploaded raw details. It does not reset consumption or update user balances. Per-user, per-batch summaries remain until a separate settlement acknowledgment applies a balance update through an identified uploaded head.

That boundary is necessary because requests may finish while settlement is happening. Retiring consumption only through the acknowledged head preserves newer charges. Revisions and idempotent retries prevent repeating the same settlement from applying it twice.

The default upload cycle is five minutes. That is a delivery schedule, not a guarantee of completeness or immediate publication.

The optional accounting journal stores committed events and delivery state in SQLite WAL with authenticated encryption. Frozen batches keep their original signatures after restart; new batches use the new process identity.

It protects successfully committed records. It cannot reconstruct an in-flight request that never produced an event, recover usage that was never observed, or prevent deletion or rollback of an entire journal copy. On a journal write failure or capacity limit, the implementation closes new admission instead of silently discarding accepted events.

This makes durability a concrete, bounded claim rather than “nothing can ever be lost.”

Public batches exclude prompts, answers, credentials, and refusal text. They contain accounting fields and refusal categories. User pseudonyms rotate using a fresh public batch nonce and a private random identity, rather than hashing a short username.

That reduces direct linkage across batches but does not guarantee anonymity. Timing, model choices, merchant identifiers, and distinctive traffic volumes can still correlate activity.

The resulting ledger is useful because readers can check the bytes they received, inspect charge evidence, and distinguish estimates from observed usage. Deposits, withdrawals, externally managed balances, missing history, and provider-side execution remain separate questions. Keeping those boundaries explicit is what makes the evidence meaningful.

"The disk is full, I already deleted the big log file – but not a single byte was freed." A classic. The file is gone from the directory, but its content still takes up space.

On Linux, rm only removes the directory entry, the name. The file system releases the data only when no name points to the file and no process has it open any more.

If a service keeps a file open – typically a web server, a database or an application with its log file – the space stays allocated. Often the process even keeps writing to it. The file just can't be reached through a path any more.

That explains the disagreement between two tools: df asks the file system how much space is used and includes the file. du walks the directories and no longer finds it.

If the numbers are far apart, lsof shows the suspects. The +L1 option lists open files with a link count below one, i.e. already deleted:

The interesting columns are COMMAND, PID, FD, SIZE/OFF and the path marked (deleted). To limit the output to one file system, append the mount point:

Reload or restart the service. Usually the cleanest way. Many services reopen their log files on reload, for example:

A reload only helps if the service reopens its logs while doing it. Otherwise a restart is needed – on production systems, with care and at a suitable time.

Truncate the file through /proc. If a restart isn't possible right now, the deleted file can be truncated to zero bytes through its file descriptor. PID and FD number come from the lsof output (for FD 4w, the number is 4):

This is a stopgap: the content is gone afterwards, so be sure it really is a log file you can do without.

Not every gap between df and du has this cause. Other suspects: files hidden under a mount point mounted on top of them later, and on ext4 the blocks reserved for root. And if df -h shows free space but nothing can be written, check df -i – the inodes may be exhausted.

A full disk is often just the symptom. The cause is frequently a log nobody rotates – a good example of why the second look pays off.

What a TEE can actually prove about an AI API router

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 16 • Originally published at teerouter.hashnode.dev

An AI API router sits in an awkward place. It needs to read a request to select an upstream account, enforce permissions, and calculate usage. That also gives its operator opportunities to change parameters, misuse credentials, or report a different price from the one the client expected.

Publishing the router's source helps people inspect those decisions. It leaves another question unanswered: is that the program handling my request?

I am building TeeRouter, an open-source router that explores this question using trusted execution environments and independently verifiable receipts. This is a case study of its public implementation at commit 5c89b551, rather than an independent audit or verification of a live deployment. The interesting part is how narrow the useful guarantees need to be.

Suppose a service presents a valid hardware attestation report. If the report is unrelated to the TLS connection carrying your prompts, it tells you very little about where those prompts go. An untrusted frontend could show evidence from a legitimate enclave while processing requests somewhere else.

TeeRouter binds its current TLS certificate and a client-generated challenge into the attestation report. The binding in the channel implementation is:

The nonce gives the verifier a freshness challenge. The certificate digest connects the evidence to the certificate used for TLS. The protocol label separates this purpose from other uses of attestation data.

This only works if the client actually checks that binding and uses the corresponding authenticated connection. Downloading a report, displaying a green badge, and then sending traffic to an unrelated endpoint would discard the useful property.

An authentic report can still describe a program you should not trust. A verifier needs an independently chosen policy defining which measurements and security state it accepts.

For the TDX path, the project checks certificate chains, revocation and trusted computing base information, measurements, the relevant event sequence, and the report-data binding. The security document makes the independence requirement explicit: evidence supplied by the server cannot also be the sole authority deciding what counts as trustworthy.

There is an operational consequence here. Updating the router is also a trust-policy problem. A client must have a defensible reason to accept the new measured software. Automatically trusting every measurement announced by the service would weaken the point of checking measurements at all.

The application also matters. A correctly attested program with a credential-export endpoint would still export credentials. TEE isolation does not repair bad authorization logic, unsafe dependencies, or mistakes in the approved code. It reduces particular infrastructure trust assumptions under its hardware and software threat model; it does not remove the need for review.

A router administrator needs to change balances and permissions. That does not mean the administrator should be able to impersonate a user and spend their balance.

The current user authentication protocol requires a client-owned Ed25519 signature as well as a valid Bearer credential. A user ID is derived from the public key; the signing seed stays with the client.

The signature covers the exact body bytes, method, path and query, relevant headers, a timestamp, a random nonce, and the router's boot ID. Including authorization and routing headers prevents someone from preserving the body while silently changing the account-pool selection or other signed instructions. Including a boot ID makes proofs from an earlier process invalid after a restart.

This is a deliberately limited separation of powers. An administrator can still deny service, change permission settings, or alter externally managed balances. What administrator authority alone cannot do, under the verified implementation, is produce a valid signature for the existing user's key.

AI requests carry more financially significant settings than a model name. Cache controls, service tiers, reasoning options, and tool definitions can all affect behavior or cost.

TeeRouter's documented forwarding boundary preserves native generation parameters instead of silently adding its own cache or retention choices. Account egress is explicitly configured, official upstream HTTPS certificates remain checked, and a failed proxy is not supposed to fall back to a direct connection. These are application properties to inspect in the approved version, not properties hardware provides automatically.

There is also a boundary the router cannot cross: the upstream provider still receives the prompt. Attesting an intermediary does not attest the provider's internal inference service, prove which model weights it ran, or establish how it retains requests. A router can preserve a requested parameter; it cannot turn that parameter into independently observed behavior inside another company's infrastructure.

The project also signs consumption batches. Its standalone verifier distinguishes signature verification, record verification, and TEE verification.

That distinction is useful beyond this project. A mathematically valid signature says that a key signed particular bytes. Hardware evidence and an approved reference policy can establish more about that key's origin. Neither result alone establishes that every request appears in the supplied history or that the latest batch has been disclosed.
