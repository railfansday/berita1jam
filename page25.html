California Democrat Rep. Gomez resigns following ethics report on sexual misconduct allegations

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

U.S. Rep. Jimmy Gomez, seen here at a Congressional hearing in March, announced Monday that he would resign following a report from the House Ethics committee on allegations of sexual misconduct. Heather Diehl/Getty Images North America hide caption

Rep. Jimmy Gomez (D-Calif) says he is resigning from Congress after a bipartisan investigation released Monday found "substantial evidence" he engaged in sexual misconduct toward a House staffer.

The House Ethics Committee concluded Gomez violated House rules by "making unwelcome sexual advances and engaging in unwelcome sexual conduct towards a House staffer and disregarding the power imbalance inherent in his relationship with House staffers."

The report also says the behavior could constitute criminal sexual assault.

Gomez has served in Congress since 2017 representing parts of Los Angeles. He lost reelection this fall to fellow Democrat Angela Gonzales-Torres. California uses a system where the top two vote-getters no matter the party advance to the general election. Gomez says his resignation will be effective Dec. 1.

Lisa Banks and Sarah Nesbitt, attorneys for the unnamed House staffer, wrote in a statement that their client testified to the committee truthfully and at personal risk.

"She sacrificed her own sense of safety and privacy to help reveal the ongoing misogyny and debauchery that permeates Capitol Hill," Banks and Nesbitt wrote. "Representative Gomez seems to believe that the power he wields, both as a man and as a member of Congress, makes him untouchable. Today's findings are a meaningful step toward dismantling that belief."

In a statement announcing his plan to resign, Gomez wrote that he cooperated with the investigation, but has strong disagreements with the report and its conclusions.

"Among other significant deficiencies and omissions, the report does not include key portions of my testimony that are essential to a full and fair understanding of what occurred," he wrote.

"I also have serious concerns about the process. In my interview, I answered the Committee's questions only to see my statements selectively quoted, disparaged, and disregarded throughout the final report, to which I was given no opportunity to respond."

The House Ethics Committee, which includes five Democrats and five Republicans, opened its investigation into Gomez in August following a press report of Gomez kissing a staffer from another Congressional office at a party. Gomez denied those allegations.

The committee asked Gomez to disclose whether he "ever engaged in a romantic or sexual relationship or made sexual contact with a congressional staffer." Gomez disclosed interactions with two women — telling the committee the encounters were all consensual. Both women spoke to the committee.

One denied "she had ever had a nonconsensual sexual encounter" with Gomez. The other, described as Witness 1 in the report, said she had more than one encounter with Gomez and she did not consider them consensual.

She told investigators that Gomez made sexual contact without her consent at a bar in February 2024 and then was forceful with her and tried to kiss her at his congressional office in July 2024.

Gomez denied the interactions were nonconsensual. The staffer, described as Witness 1 in the committee report, eventually left her job on Capitol Hill.

"Here, the House lost Witness 1 as a competent and able staffer because of Representative Gomez's misconduct and because of a broader culture of disrespect and toxic sexual behavior toward staff that permeates life on the Hill and is long overdue for correction," the committee wrote.

The report comes amid a broader conversation about how Congress deals with the behavior of its members, especially when it comes to sexual misconduct.

In April, Democrat Eric Swalwell of California resigned his House seat amid allegations of sexual assault and misconduct. He also denied the allegations. Rep. Tony Gonzales (R-Tex.) also resigned from the House after admitting to an affair with a former staff member.

Rep. Chuck Edwards (R-N.C.) dropped his reelection bid earlier this year after the House Ethics Committee recommended he face a censure vote for allegedly harassing two young female staffers. Edwards denied the allegations.

Republican Reps. Max Miller of Ohio and Cory Mills of Florida also face House Ethics investigations stemming from allegations of domestic violence. Both deny wrongdoing.

Miller lost his reelection bid this fall and Mills lost his GOP primary.

Last week, the House Ethics Committee adopted new rules with the aim of speeding up investigations into sexual misconduct and improving transparency.

After Nvidia’s board accepted Jensen Huang’s resignation as CEO, following reports that Huang had an affair with an alleged Chinese spy, President Donald Trump, too, appeared to submit to the new reality. Appearing on Stephen A. Smith’s radio show shortly after the news came out, Trump said that Huang would “end up on his feet.”

“You know the expression, anybody can be replaced,” Trump said, floating Apple’s ex-CEO Tim Cook as a potential CEO candidate.

It didn’t take long for Trump to begin softening his approach, making it clear he’d welcome Huang back into the role of CEO even if Nvidia’s own board wasn’t ready to do so. Behind the scenes, the Trump administration was working to grease the landing for Huang’s return. And soon, Nvidia’s board changed its tune: Barely a week after insisting they all agreed to shut the door on Huang’s return, they voted unanimously to reinstate him as CEO, president, and board director.

“There’s nothing wrong with a comeback,” Trump said in a celebratory call to CNBC.

Trump then posted an image on Truth Social of Huang transposed onto Trump’s own body in the infamous photo of him raising his fist after being shot at a campaign rally. In his raised fist, Huang-on-Trump’s-body is clutching a scarlet bra, holding it high in the air.

Even after a decisive blow to the president’s power as Democrats swept the midterm elections, Trump appears to maintain his position as a corporate kingmaker. Trump said he spoke with Huang the night prior to his reinstatement and had been in communication with the board both “directly and indirectly.” White House communications director Steven Cheung said in a statement that “President Trump was totally involved” in the board’s decision — Trump decided near the end of last week that he wanted to lead efforts to get Huang reappointed, per The New York Times, so he asked Vice President JD Vance to act as a liaison between Huang and Nvidia’s board, providing reassurances that the move wouldn’t pose a security risk. Trump also reportedly dialed into Nvidia’s Sunday night board meeting to make the case for his return. “I made the deal,” he told CNBC on Monday.

News of Huang’s reappointment, nearly as dramatic as Sam Altman’s return to his CEO role after being voted out by OpenAI’s board in 2023, was a widespread shock to experts, analysts, and investors, particularly after Nvidia’s board had assured stakeholders on its quarterly earnings call last Tuesday that the board “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO.” On Monday morning, a surge in NASDAQ-100 futures hit a circuit-breaker limit and caused a halt in trading over the volatility.

Trump has meddled in other messy incidents, including by repeatedly — and perhaps illegally — extending the deadline for TikTok to complete its sale to a US entity, and, he admitted on Monday, protecting Tesla and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk. Although he said on CNBC that Musk is “80 percent super genius, 20 percent he has other problems,” Trump said it’s important to protect people like him and Huang. “We have to protect our brilliant people because we don’t have enough.”

But the upcoming Democratic-majority Congress could test both Huang and Trump’s influence. As Republicans prepare for an open presidential primary in 2028, at least one potential candidate appears to be breaking with the president in their skepticism of Huang and Nvidia. And Democrats seem eager to probe both Nvidia and the Trump administration.

The past couple of weeks have been tumultuous for Nvidia. Huang admitted he had had an affair with Alice Huang (no relation), a former executive at Nvidia customer Megaspeed whom some US intelligence officers reportedly believed to be a potential Chinese spy. There were nearly immediate reports of an additional affair with a direct report, as well as an alleged third affair with a second employee. Megaspeed has reportedly also been investigated for a broader attempt to illegally funnel advanced Nvidia chips to China. Huang himself has pushed to open up more chip sales to China, and he reportedly lied to the Nvidia board about his infidelity. He’s scheduled to testify before Congress on December 1st.

The Nvidia board tried to address concerns about Huang in its announcement Monday, writing that members “received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang’s reinstatement poses no national security risk.” It made assurances that the independent investigation it had kicked off about Huang and foreign intelligence would “proceed to conclusion” with Huang’s cooperation.

The board also said it had recently formed an entirely new National Security and Export Control Committee “comprised solely of independent directors to oversee trade compliance and national security risks,” which is working with legal counsel and advisers who have national security experience. Nvidia will be hiring for a new C-suite position, the board said: chief compliance officer. The executive will “oversee compliance” across the company and report to the Nominating and Corporate Governance committees. “NVIDIA will continue to cooperate with ongoing government investigations and inquiries,” the board added.

Still, the reaction to Huang’s return reflects how small the chance of Huang’s return seemed before Trump’s efforts. Although Trump had previously cast doubt on multiple reports that Alice Huang was a spy, The New York Times reported on Sunday that she “kept in contact with an official of China’s top spy agency for more than a year.” According to the Times, which cited multiple people with knowledge of US intelligence reports, Kang Wei — Alice Huang’s business partner and, by some reports, husband, who reportedly introduced her to the Nvidia CEO — worked with the same Chinese intelligence agency. But on Sunday evening, Trump still argued for Huang’s return and managed to continue flipping Nvidia’s board in his favor after a weekend’s worth of efforts.

“[I]ndefensible, irresponsible and incomprehensible”

If the board maintained any hang-ups about bringing Huang back as CEO, Trump’s power over the executive branch — and the pressure he could exert with it — might have offered a compelling counterargument. The Securities and Exchange Commission had previously opened an investigation into whether Nvidia “misled investors,” according to The Wall Street Journal. But in a post on Truth Social Monday, Trump said that the attorney general “has confirmed that the U.S. Government does not possess evidence that he has engaged in any unlawful conduct.” Reached for comment, the DOJ did not provide an on-the-record response in line with The Verge’s editorial policy.

Key Democrats have indicated that both Nvidia and the Trump administration will be under fire when the new Congress is sworn in — and momentum has built behind GPU export controls, which Huang lobbied extensively to loosen. “Democrats will wield their full power to investigate any wrongdoing by the Trump Administration, Jensen Huang, other NVIDIA executives, and anyone selling out our national security for financial gain.” Senate Majority Leader Chuck Schumer (D-NY) said in a post on X, adding they’d work “on a bipartisan basis to continue advancing policies that stop the flow of our most powerful technology to the Chinese military and other adversaries.” Speaker-designate Hakeem Jeffries (D-NY) called the board’s decision “indefensible, irresponsible and incomprehensible” and pledged to investigate “business malfeasance by NVIDIA and corrupt abuse of power by Howard Lutnick and the Department of Commerce.” He called for “traditional Republicans” to join in the effort.

“The reckless decision to bring Jensen Huang back as CEO does not change the fact that Mr. Huang, NVIDIA executives, and the company’s board still owe Congress answers about the company’s compliance with U.S. export controls,” Sen. Elizabeth Warren, the top Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee, said in a statement. “Jensen Huang’s return only increases the urgency of passing bipartisan export control bills into law and making sure NVIDIA does not lobby to kill that legislation and put our national security at risk.” Rep. Jamie Raskin, the top Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee, said, “A real Congress will find out” whether Trump and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had any alternative incentives for their role in Nvidia’s dealings. Sen. Mark Warner, the top Democrat on the Intelligence Committee, called Trump’s intervention to bring back Huang “outrageous.” Rep. Joaquin Castro (D-TX), a member of the House Intelligence and Foreign Affairs Committees, which are interviewing Huang next week, said he wants to know how Trump stands to benefit from “personally champion[ing] Huang’s return.”

“We need to know why Trump put his thumb on the scale to have Jensen reinstated as CEO”

At closed-door hearings next week, Democrats say they intend to press Huang and the administration further, and several accused the administration of corruption. “We need to know why Trump put his thumb on the scale to have Jensen reinstated as CEO and why his administration failed to restrict the PRC’s access to our most sensitive technology,” Rep. Gregory Meeks, the top Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, said in a statement to The Verge. The board’s decision is “outrageous,” said Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi (D-IL), a member of the House Select Committee on China. “We still don’t know what information Chinese intelligence may have obtained from him, and neither the government nor Nvidia’s own board has completed its investigation,” Krishnamoorthi said in a statement, calling for Huang to testify publicly. “Reinstatement is no substitute for accountability,” Rep. Kathy Castor (D-FL), another China Committee member, said in a statement.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta also intends to continue an investigation into the company. “The reinstatement of Jensen Huang does not change the fact that the American people deserve to know what happened here — whether Nvidia misled investors and the public,” California Department of Justice spokesperson Elissa Perez said in a statement.

Last week, before Huang was reinstated, at least two Republican China hawks opposed the idea. Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO), a rumored potential presidential candidate, told reporters that he would not like to see Huang return to the top post, but would “defer to the board how they want to run their company. If it were me and a guy who just was caught up in a Chinese honeytrap — I think is the technical term for it — I don’t think that I would impose massive security concerns, not just for the company, but for American national security.”

While it was up to the board to decide whether to bring Huang back, Hawley said, “We in Congress ought to be concerned about the American national security implications and I think we should be looking into that.”

Huang “has a serious credibility problem”

House China Committee Chair John Moolenaar (R-MI) said on stage last week at the Aspen Cyber Summit in Washington, D.C., that Huang “has a serious credibility problem, and I don’t believe he would be as effective now as he was before.” His office did not immediately respond to a request for comment after Huang’s reinstatement.

Sitting beside Moolenaar on stage last week, Rep. Greg Stanton (D-AZ) gave his advice to the board: “Don’t do it. Don’t bring it back. And at the very least, slow your roll, because there are so many questions that have to be answered. What else is out there that Jensen Huang has done to put US national security at risk?” Stanton warned that Congress is just beginning to probe what happened here. “There are going to be very serious investigations with very serious ramifications if in fact it is as it seems,” he said. “The company needs to know this ain’t going away for a while. This is a very serious issue.” After news of Huang’s return, Stanton doubled down on calls for investigations.

Other Republicans — some of whom are leaving Congress — notably disagreed with Trump’s jubilance for Huang’s return. “It’s inexplicable to me why they want this guy back as CEO,” retiring Rep. Don Bacon (R-NE) told CNBC. “To me it’s a failure of character.” Sen. Pete Ricketts (R-NE) wrote on X that Huang’s “behavior does not support reinstatement,” and said he must still testify before Congress. “It also doesn’t absolve Nvidia of answering for national security concerns and unaccounted-for chips,” he wrote. Senate Intelligence Committee Chair Tom Cotton (R-AR) wrote on X that he’s not done investigating. “The controversy with Nvidia and Megaspeed is just the tip of the iceberg, and it’s not resolved because Jensen Huang has his job back,” he said. “Congress has much to investigate and we will pass overwhelmingly bipartisan chip export-control bills, including my Chip Security Act, one way or the other.”

Market analysts have been watching and waiting for a Huang comeback since reports that he had assembled a “war room” of allies to help with his possible reinstatement. Not only did Huang cofound one of the world’s most valuable companies and arguably the most important one in the AI boom, but he has personally helped secure hundreds of billions of dollars in infrastructure funding from US financial institutions. Demand for Nvidia chips constantly outpaces supply, and Huang has had the president’s ear for months. Despite all that, though, Huang’s clout seemed like it wouldn’t help him up until the past 24 hours.

Lindsey Stewart, director of institutional insights at Morningstar, wrote in a note that although investors may consider this a “good move given his history of value creation at the company,” questions remain about the best way to provide corporate governance and oversight at such an important company.

Flipping a House Isn’t Paying Like It Used To. Is Renting the Better Bet?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The typical home flip generated just $60,526 in gross profit in the second quarter of 2026, down from $71,000 a year earlier, according to new data—before accounting for renovation costs or other expenses.

It's a shrinking cushion for investors who have long relied on a simple formula to fuel their business: Buy low, apply elbow grase, and sell high. But with elevated borrowing costs and renovation expenses eating into margins, there's much less room than there used to be for anything to go wrong.

The report, from real estate data analytics company ATTOM, found that flipping returns have been declining for two years, with the typical gross return falling to 21.5%, from 27.6% a year earlier.

"Flippers are still making money in most markets, but the typical return continues to narrow," Rob Barber, CEO of ATTOM, said via press release. "The second-quarter numbers continue the general downward trend in profit margins and gross profits we have seen over the past two years."

So, why not skip the resale altogether and rent out the property instead?

After all, the promise of passive income—collecting rent every month while building equity—helped fuel a wave of small investors buying up homes earlier this decade when borrowing costs were low and rents were on a meteoric rise.

But the economics have changed for landlords, too.

Potential rental yields declined in more than half of U.S. counties with comparable data between 2025 and 2026, according to separate ATTOM research. That's to say nothing of the work involved in managing a rental property, which, as Realtor.com® previously reported, has proved to be anything but passive.

In a market defined by high carrying costs and shrinking returns, the better strategy may come down to which risks an investor can afford to weather—and for how long.

Despite shrinking profits, an index of fix-and-flip market conditions remained in expansion territory in the third quarter of 2026, according to a November survey of roughly 280 investors from John Burns Research and Consulting and Kiavi.

Some 66% of respondents reported selling most of their properties over the previous 12 months in line with their initial after-repair value (ARV) estimates—the amount a property is expected to fetch after renovations.

“Flipping can be profitable in a hot market with limited inventory, where homes sell quickly and buyers pay a premium for updated properties,” explains Realtor.com economist Jiayi Xu.

By purchasing below market value and making improvements that buyers look for, investors can boost the home's after-repair value and pocket the difference. In the right conditions, that means faster returns than holding the property long term.

And the slowdown may be creating opportunities to buy at a deeper discount.

Competition for fixer-uppers has eased in some markets, particularly Texas and Florida, where 44% and 37% of surveyed investors, respectively, reported less competition than usual, according to the November survey.

"Fewer bidders doesn't make every deal a good one," cautions Charles Goodwin, vice president of investor mortgage sales at Figure, Kiavi's parent company. “What it does is give well-capitalized investors with a defined exit strategy more room to be patient and negotiate. That's where experienced operators tend to separate themselves."

The trouble is, it's becoming harder to accurately predict what that exit will pay.

Four in 10 surveyed flippers reported closing sales at lower prices than a year earlier, according to the November John Burns–Kiavi survey. Meanwhile, 20% said most of their properties sold below their initial ARV estimates.

Among those who fell short of their targets, 84% said they had overestimated the eventual sale price, while 57% reported underestimating their costs.

Goodwin warns against basing resale projections on comparable sales from a stronger market.

"If a deal only works with last spring's comps, it probably doesn't work," he says.

And even when investors get their resale estimates right, renovations can consume a substantial share of the proceeds.

The average renovation cost was approximately $72,000 per flipped home in the third quarter of 2026, down from $80,000 a year earlier. Yet renovations still consumed a larger share of the average sale price, rising from 16% to 17%, according to the survey.

Financing adds another layer of risk, particularly when a project takes longer than expected.

The average effective interest rate reported by surveyed flippers who obtained new financing was 9.3% in the third quarter. At that rate, an investor carrying a hypothetical $320,000 interest-only loan would spend roughly $2,480 in interest for each additional month before selling.

Just one extra month would wipe out more than three times the $800 saved over a six-month hold by securing a loan at 9.3% rather than the previous year's 9.8% rate, according to the survey's calculations.

And even when a flip does succeed, Xu notes that taxes can reduce the final profit. Properties acquired primarily for resale as part of a flipping business generally generate ordinary income rather than qualifying for preferential long-term capital gains rates.

With strong headwinds to flipping a property, renting out a fixer-upper can offer a better risk exposure, especially for investors prepared to hold the property long term, according to Xu.

“If you prefer steady income, long-term wealth building, and lower risk, renting is generally the wiser choice,” she says.

Over time, owners may also benefit from property appreciation and tax deductions associated with operating a rental, making the rental path more attractive for those focused on building wealth.

And while asking rents were in decline for more than three years, the market is beginning to stabilize. The median asking rent rose 0.1% year over year to $1,692 in October 2026, its first annual increase since June 2023, according to the latest rental report from Realtor.com

Of course, renting out a fixer upper isn't entirely without risk.

Potential gross rental yields for three-bedroom properties declined in 54.8% of U.S. counties with comparable data between 2025 and 2026, according to ATTOM's annual single-family rental report.

"Even though rents and wages are rising in many markets, record-high home prices are compressing yields," says Barber.

And those projected yields don't account for the ongoing costs of owning and maintaining the property.

Katie Lyon, a rental property owner and host of the Landlord Diaries podcast, previously told Realtor.com that even profitable long-term rentals can leave landlords with little room for unexpected expenses.

"If the water heater goes out, you're back three years of cash flow," she says.

A simple benchmark can help would-be investors gauge whether a rental deal makes sense. The “1% Rule" suggests that monthly gross rent should equal roughly 1% of the purchase price.

When it comes to fixer-uppers, seasoned investors rely on a similar guiding principle. The "70% Rule," advises paying no more than 70% of a property's after-repair value, minus estimated renovation costs.

Ignoring this benchmark can quickly erode profits, especially in today's market where flip margins are already thin, warns Ian Hart, a seasoned housing investor and founder of Barndominium.org.

And the latest returns suggest that buying at the lowest possible price isn't necessarily the answer.

Homes purchased for $100,000 to $200,000 generated a typical gross flipping return of 28% in the second quarter of 2026, according to ATTOM. By contrast, properties acquired for $50,000 or less generated a typical gross loss of $15,000 before renovation expenses.

The November John Burns–Kiavi survey offers another measure of how cautious investors have become: About two-thirds of respondents said they wouldn't pay more than 70% of a property's estimated ARV, while the average maximum was just 67%.

Perhaps most importantly, though, buyers should look for exit flexibility. The best fixer-upper deals are those that work in more than one scenario—whether as a flip in a hot resale market or a rental in an area with strong tenant demand.

OpenAI Launches Shirudo to Protect Running Linux Systems

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI and Cisco launched the open-source Shirudo project on November 23, 2026, giving operators a runtime for applying temporary protections to Linux systems while permanent fixes are developed and rolled out. The pre-release project combines eBPF controls with an agent-oriented design for developing targeted safeguards called shields.

The project targets the gap between finding a vulnerability and completing a patch rollout. The companies say that process can take days or weeks, and indiscriminately blocking the relevant Linux operation may take a service offline when malicious and legitimate requests depend on it. Shirudo lets teams evaluate a candidate shield against both exploit behavior and normal workloads before enforcement. Deployment still requires operator approval, and the service can remain online while the safeguard takes effect.

At the operating-system level, eBPF gives Shirudo a Linux mechanism for running small programs at designated points to observe activity and apply targeted protections. Its shields can block vulnerable code paths without restarting the system. The companies are also designing programmable controls that let agents investigate vulnerabilities, develop shields and validate them against exploits and real workloads, while security teams retain control over what is deployed.

Cisco’s technical account describes Shirudo as an agent-oriented rewrite of Tetragon, the eBPF security platform Isovalent open-sourced in 2022. The design lets agents generate code directly rather than work only through human-oriented consoles, configuration files or policy languages. Depending on the vulnerability, a mitigation can block the path an exploit needs, replace vulnerable code or add detection visibility. Application or user-space bytecode modification extends that approach to vulnerabilities for which eBPF is not suited.

For deployment, the operator system builds eBPF or application-bytecode shields as signed .bee packages delivered through an OCI registry. Targets pull and validate the packages, and the architecture uses a one-shot command-line interface rather than a permanently running daemon on target or operator machines. Shields are verified before loading, Linux’s eBPF verifier applies its safety rules, and a Trusted Platform Module (TPM) supports a verifiable record of protections being loaded or removed.

In Cisco’s controlled-test account, OpenAI’s Codex produced a shield for Java’s Base64 decoder and attached the new input-validation code to a live Elasticsearch process. The test used Elasticsearch 9.5.5; the shield rejected malicious input and blocked a Java4Shell exploit attempt while the service continued running without a restart. The bytecode is intended for Java applications on OpenJDK during the interval before OpenJDK itself receives a permanent patch.

The demonstrated agent workflow starts with vulnerability discovery or an external disclosure, then reproduces the exploit, selects a mitigation point and iterates until a mitigation works. The shield is signed and rolled out with application-health monitoring, while deployed shields record exploitation attempts for investigation. The result is one vendor-run controlled example, not a demonstration that every vulnerability can be mitigated automatically.

The public repository labels Shirudo pre-release. Production targets require a Linux kernel with the signed-loader application binary interface and Shirudo’s kernel patches, plus TPM 2.0. Continuous integration runs all test tiers on x86-64 and host tests on arm64; the test VM also boots locally on arm64, while target validation for riscv64 remains pending. The project uses BSD 2-Clause licensing, with BPF programs dual-licensed under BSD and GPL.

The project documentation describes a pull-based delivery model: targets retrieve signed micropatches, called upatches, from OCI registries and push telemetry to configured OpenTelemetry collectors, leaving no target component listening for connections. The security boundary is equally explicit: an attacker who gains kernel code execution or arbitrary kernel writes could potentially bypass Shirudo’s in-kernel checks. Upatches can remove attack surface and block a known vulnerable path until a fixed kernel is deployed, but they do not replace kernel hardening or permanent fixes.

OpenAI and Cisco aim eventually to make tested shields available alongside vulnerability disclosures, giving operators temporary protections to evaluate while permanent patches are prepared. The broader goal is infrastructure that identifies affected systems, validates protections, contains compromises, limits attackers’ movement between systems and restores services to a verified known-good state. Kernel and core-service hardening, workload permission limits, hardware-backed attestation and independent network controls support that objective.

Those ambitions extend well beyond the current pre-release code. The Java4Shell demonstration shows a specific agent-generated protection working under controlled conditions; whether the same validation, signing and verification model can handle diverse vulnerabilities across production fleets is the practical question raised by the launch.
