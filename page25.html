A Common Heart Failure Warning Sign May Actually Be Causing Dangerous Blood Clots

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A hormone used to detect heart trouble can also promote dangerous blood clots in experiments, pointing scientists toward a possible way to prevent blockages without disrupting normal healing.

Heart disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States, claiming a life nearly every 34 seconds. For people living with heart failure, a condition in which the heart struggles to pump enough blood throughout the body, doctors often rely on blood tests to detect signs of cardiac stress. Now, researchers have discovered that one of these familiar warning signals may also contribute to a dangerous complication: blood clots that can block circulation and trigger life-threatening events.

The substance is a hormone called B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP). Doctors routinely measure BNP and related protein fragments because elevated levels can indicate heart failure or other cardiovascular problems. Until now, BNP has largely been viewed as a marker of an overworked heart rather than a potential contributor to disease.

Researchers at the Texas A&M University Irma Lerma Rangel College of Pharmacy, part of Texas A&M Health, have found that elevated BNP can directly activate blood platelets and accelerate clot formation. The study, published in Blood, identifies a molecular pathway that could eventually offer a more targeted way to prevent dangerous blood clots.

“We thought that BNP might not only act as a passive marker but actively contributes to the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular disease,” said Dr. Zhenyu Li, a professor of pharmaceutical sciences who led the research.

Platelets normally protect the body from blood loss. When a blood vessel is damaged, these tiny blood components gather at the injury and stick together to help stop bleeding. Problems arise when platelets become overly active and form clots inside blood vessels where they are not needed. Those clots can obstruct circulation, depriving tissues and organs of oxygen, and contribute to emergencies such as heart attacks and pulmonary embolisms, which occur when a clot blocks blood flow in the lungs.

Li’s team traced BNP’s effects to a receptor on the surface of platelets called natriuretic peptide receptor-A (NPRA). Receptors allow cells to respond to specific chemical signals, and the researchers found that BNP activates platelets by binding directly to NPRA.

“It was previously unknown whether platelets even expressed this receptor,” Li explained. “We are the first team to investigate whether BNP directly activates platelets through the NPRA receptor.”

Once BNP attaches to NPRA, it increases the amount of a signaling molecule called cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) inside platelets. This sets off a chain of cellular responses that makes platelets more likely to stick together and promotes clot growth. In experimental studies involving blood-vessel injury and pulmonary embolism, elevated BNP markedly accelerated clot formation.

The researchers also examined what happened when NPRA was blocked or its gene was removed. Under those conditions, BNP could no longer increase cGMP levels or activate platelets, and the experiments showed protection against abnormal clotting.

“Knocking out the NPRA gene protected against these complications, proving the clotting process is mediated by the NPRA receptor,” Li said.

Blood samples from patients with heart rhythm disorders provided additional evidence. In these patients, higher circulating BNP levels were associated with greater platelet activity. The finding supports a possible connection between BNP and clotting risk in humans, although the association alone does not establish that BNP directly causes blood clots in patients.

People with elevated BNP are already known to face a higher risk of serious cardiovascular events, including heart attacks and pulmonary embolisms. The newly identified pathway offers a possible biological explanation for that relationship. Li emphasized that the discovery emerged from years of work examining platelet activity and cardiovascular disease.

“It has been an ongoing, gradual progression—not a sudden breakthrough, but a logical continuation of our research over the past two decades,” he said.

The findings could have implications for how dangerous blood clots are prevented. Existing antiplatelet medications reduce the ability of platelets to form clots, but their effects throughout the body can increase the risk of serious internal bleeding. By targeting the interaction between BNP and NPRA, researchers hope to develop treatments that interfere with harmful clot formation while preserving the normal clotting needed to heal injuries.

Such treatments remain experimental. Li suggested that, in the meantime, physicians may need to monitor patients with elevated BNP more closely and consider whether antiplatelet therapy could help reduce their risk of clot-related complications.

Several questions also remain about the mechanism. Although platelets appear central to BNP-driven clotting, the researchers still need to establish how much of the effect comes specifically from NPRA receptors on platelets rather than receptors on other cells. Future studies will selectively remove NPRA from different cell types to clarify their respective roles.

“Platelets appear to be major contributors, and to confirm this further, we need to develop tissue-specific models,” Li said.

The team is now working with Dr. Wenshe Liu, Regents Professor and Harry E. Bovay, Jr. Chair in Chemistry at the Texas A&M College of Arts and Sciences, to identify small molecules capable of blocking the BNP-NPRA interaction. Promising compounds will undergo further testing to determine whether they can prevent BNP-induced platelet activation and thrombosis without broadly disrupting healthy clotting.

“Because patients with high BNP levels face the greatest risk, targeting this pathway opens new opportunities to reduce thrombotic complications while minimizing standard side effects like severe bleeding,” Li said.

Pacific Power Begins New Customer Protections Against Data Centers

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Regulators approve new agreement to ensure data centers pay for their own energy costs

SALEM, Oregon — The Oregon Public Utility Commission has approved an agreement between advocates and Pacific Power. Now, Pacific Power customers have a strong suite of protections against data centers’ impact on Oregonians’ energy bills.

Starting Jan. 1, 2027, new Pacific Power data centers will be held responsible for their own energy costs. These new rules will only apply to future data centers and existing ones making significant investments, which regulators identified as the most pressing challenge to address. Right now, the utility has very few existing data centers on its system.

“With a long line of data centers waiting at Pacific Power’s door, I’m confident that we have the right protections at the right time,” said Bob Jenks, Executive Director of Oregon Citizens’ Utility Board. “We were able to stop more extreme impacts on power bills just in time.”

Data centers over 100 MW will also have to pay a small surcharge to fund energy efficiency upgrades for low-income households. This includes funding for deep home retrofits, lowering energy use across the grid while saving customers on monthly bills.

“Many of the households we serve are already struggling to afford their energy bills and risk having their utilities shut off. Data centers will help fund weatherization, energy efficiency upgrades, and the critical home repairs that often prevent our clients from accessing these improvements in the first place,” said Charity Fain, Executive Director of Community Energy Project. “Requiring data centers to cover the costs of the energy infrastructure they need, rather than passing those costs on to residential customers, is an important protection for low-income households, seniors, and people with disabilities.”

Under this order, if there is a power emergency, Pacific Power can prioritize critical energy use for other customers, like renters and homeowners, by curtailing service to data centers. Increased electric demand by data centers strains the grid and increases the potential for blackouts during heat waves or hard freezes.

“This is a big win that puts Oregon at the forefront of managing data center growth the right way,” said Joshua Basofin, Oregon Clean Energy Program Director at Climate Solutions. “It protects Oregonians from rising costs and strengthens grid resilience. Data centers should not harm our ability to access essential electricity to power our lives and keep us safe all year.”

Regulators also ruled that new data centers can only be connected to Pacific Power’s grid if there is enough emissions-free electricity available to serve them. Under HB 2021, Pacific Power is required by law to serve all customers with 100% emissions-free electricity by 2040. This new rule will help manage costs for other customers outside of data centers during the transition to clean energy.

“We have long known that clean energy is the most affordable form of energy,” said Ben Brint, Senior Climate Program Director at Oregon Environmental Council. “This ruling is a win for data center accountability, and for Oregonians across the state — especially frontline communities already bearing the brunt of climate impacts and rising energy costs. It will save ratepayers money in the short and long run, allocating costs appropriately while advancing Oregon’s 100% clean energy goals.”

This change also comes at a time when many Oregonians are calling for broader changes to how the state handles data centers. Impacts on data centers are poised to be an issue again at the Oregon State Legislature in 2027.

“The POWER Act demonstrated the people’s power to fight back against the rising energy rates and ecosystem harms that large data centers inflict,” said Emily Bowes, Policy Strategist & Acting Director at Sierra Club Oregon. “In 2027, alongside new and returning climate champions, we will continue to fight for a clean energy transition in Salem and statewide.”

In 2025, the Oregon Legislature passed the POWER Act to help utilities rein in data centers’ added costs to Oregonians’ energy bills. Last fall, Portland General Electric began the work to roll out this new law in practice. In January, Pacific Power started a similar process for its customers. The next step is to address the few existing data centers on Pacific Power’s system, starting as early as summer 2027. It is unclear if the final phase will include rate adjustments.

Democracy Depends on Being Able to Forgive

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

In a 60 Minutes interview broadcast this month, President Trump was asked a profound question that rarely comes up in political interviews: Could he forgive his enemies? If entering heaven required him to feel love and forgiveness for James Comey or Nancy Pelosi, could he do it?

“The answer is no,” the president said. “I want to be honest, no.”

We should resist the tempting thought that only Donald Trump feels this way. We all need to look in the mirror: How willing are we to forgive the people we believe have done us wrong?

For most of us, grievance has a powerful pull—whether it’s rehearsing an argument or replaying a hurtful remark over and over in our heads. In psychology, we can understand forgiveness as a voluntary change in how we feel and act toward someone who’s hurt us in the past. It can loosen the hold of resentment on our attention and, in turn, help us choose how to respond in the future.

These days, the word forgiveness can sound like a concept out of a Sunday school class or a therapy session. But it’s also a prerequisite for a functioning democratic society.

A country needs ways to respond to wrongdoing and still leave open the possibility of a shared future. When every injury becomes a reason for retaliation, the work of governing turns into settling scores.

Here’s the thing about democracy: it’s a continuing relationship among fallible people. It depends on our ability to correct errors, acknowledge wrongdoing and work together again. Forgiveness is a vital civic skill because democratic societies have to do more than settle who was right in the last conflict. Forgiveness makes healthy debate and cooperation possible.

I once asked the legendary Archbishop Desmond Tutu what forgiveness meant. He simply said, “It means I no longer demand payback from you.”

When he was chair of South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Tutu helped bring apartheid-era abuses to public attention. He underscored the principle that forgiveness isn’t blanket absolution. Truth, accountability and repair can coexist with refusing permanent vengeance. In our own lives, we can forgive someone while maintaining boundaries, including choosing not to resume a relationship.

When I interviewed former Colombian president and Nobel Peace laureate Juan Manuel Santos for my book On Belonging, he recalled advice to recognise an adversary’s humanity in war and peace. His 2016 agreement with the FARC opened a path for former guerrillas to enter into democratic politics.

This month, delivering the Nelson Mandela Annual Lecture in South Africa, Santos told the story of Pastora Mira. She cared for a wounded man who then confessed to torturing and killing her son. She embraced him, saying his acceptance of responsibility had freed her from the prison of lifelong hatred. It’s a breathtaking example. Such forgiveness remains a personal choice.

It’s important that we avoid thinking that the psychological grip of contempt is unique to any one country. Others—including my home country of Canada—shouldn’t think they’re immune to a politics of contempt. A 2025 Angus Reid Institute–Cardus survey found 87% of Conservative supporters regarded the Liberals as a threat to Canada’s well-being. And 84% of Liberal supporters said the same of Conservatives.

The philosopher Hannah Arendt understood forgiveness as a way to release people from being bound indefinitely to what they’ve done, allowing something new to begin. For her, forgiveness was inextricably linked to respect and to what I call belonging—that we honour people’s humanity, even if we disagree with their preferences.

Earlier this month, Texans elected James Talarico, a Presbyterian seminarian, to the U.S. Senate. He’s spoken eloquently about loving his enemies and seeing opponents as children of God. It’s a message that draws on a Christian tradition that asks us to extend the mercy we ourselves need—a tradition Martin Luther King Jr. brought into public life more than half a century ago.

Ultimately, forgiveness is a skill we can learn. In an international study of more than 4,500 adults, people given a short program to reflect on hurt and consider another person’s perspective reported greater forgiveness and fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety after two weeks than those waiting to begin. The study concerned personal relationships, but it offers reason to take forgiveness seriously as something we can actively cultivate.

We can all put this into practice. Name what happened and how it honestly affected your feelings. Try to consider the other person’s perspective, and think of a time you needed forgiveness for yourself. Give yourself time and space for your feelings to change. Choosing to forgive can precede feeling the emotion of forgiveness.

Let’s face it. In failing to forgive his enemies, Donald Trump isn’t so much of an outlier. But that needs to change. To build a healthy democratic society, we can’t have permanent enemies. We have to build a future together. That work can begin in our own relationships.
