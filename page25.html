10 Games That Will Feel Better To Play After GTA 6

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ronald is a video game journalist with more than 10 years of experience in the industry, working on several of the most important Spanish websites.

If you're like 32.5 million other people around the world, you've probably been dedicating a huge amount of time to GTA 6 lately, and you can't imagine what your life will be like when the experience is over.

All good things must come to an end, and eventually, your time in Leonida will reach a conclusion. However, crossing that frontier doesn't necessarily have to be a bittersweet farewell but rather an opportunity to re-evaluate everything that comes next.

Titles like this one help you appreciate the craft of video game development more than ever, as they fully exploit many of the aspects that make the interactive industry the most special of all those focused on entertainment.

Therefore, if these hypermodern times have already gotten to you and are making you nervous about what will come after Jason and Lucia's story, calm your anxiety by checking out this list of ten games that will feel better to play after GTA 6.

If you're wondering how on earth this list is going to start with one of the games most diametrically opposed to the philosophy of GTA 6, like GRIS, the answer lies precisely in that statement.

After an adventure brimming with stimuli, endless possibilities, lengthy explanations, and violence that ranges from the personal to the systemic, what better way to unwind than with one of the most focused and introspective titles ever conceived?

In just three hours, without a single word and in a straightforward manner, GRIS takes you on a soothing path of reflection, making peace with the circumstances, and reveling in an audiovisual experience that engulfs you to the point of baptizing you with its art.

Without bullets or socioeconomic problems that dictate your relationship with the law, but with platforms, elegance, and a creative intent so distinct that you won't be able to help but fall in love by the time the credits roll on the Sunday afternoon it takes to complete it.

The cliché of "best game ever" versus "best game today" strikes me as both amusing and illogical, but I do believe there's some value in considering The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake in light of GTA 6.

Both titles excel in their focus on player freedom, yet while GTA achieves this within a stunning range of action that opens up numerous possibilities, Ocarina of Time Remake does so in a much more concentrated and whimsical way.

The charm lies not in the immeasurable variety of options, but in the immeasurable creativity of the single alternative they offer for each sequence, demonstrating in the process how the notion of agency has evolved over the last 30 years.

They are two ends of the same spectrum, and experiencing the same sense of enjoyment through discovery in such different ways is what makes them complement each other far better than you might initially imagine.

Having spent its entire existence in the shadow of Rockstar's work, returning to Saints Row 2 now that GTA has taken a 180-degree turn in its approach has become more fitting than ever.

Jason and Lucia face a more realistic, weighty world with consequences, masterfully encouraging restraint and attention to detail, breaking the connection that pitted the two franchises against each other for so long.

Hence, whether you're among those who didn't fully embrace the change in philosophy or those who did, Saints Row 2's humor and arcade style are a perfect fit to reduce the pleasurable cortisol rush produced by GTA 6.

Don't get me wrong: I greatly appreciate Rockstar's courage in going against its own trend, though that doesn't mean I'm not much more eager to play a more relaxed game once I decide to stop wandering around Leonida, postponing saying goodbye.

Grand Theft Auto 6 puts a large emphasis on keeping your relationship sound, so you need to know what ways are the best to raise the romance between Jason and Lucia.

After GTA 6, it's quite clear the developer has mastered every imaginable variation of the driving genre, which includes the adrenaline-fueled and spectacular style they employed during the Midnight Club 3: DUB Edition era.

The fact that the vehicles in their latest product had to prioritize accuracy and composure was necessary to fit the rest of the game's structure, yet the primal thrill of getting behind the wheel and flying at 300 kilometers per hour in the most improbable way possible was sacrificed.

Consequently, even considering that the genre as a whole is today much closer to realistic simulation than arcade driving, dusting off your copy of Midnight Club 3: DUB Edition after GTA 6 would be in the instruction manual if GTA 6 had one.

Neither approach is inherently better than the other, evidently, but the desire to truly revel in the speed instead of worrying about the cost of a crash will grow stronger on you with each passing second.

Years go by, and it's incredible how no third-person shooter has understood that they are morally obligated to look to Vanquish to understand how to keep the genre from perpetually stagnating.

Gunfights in GTA 6 are technically impressive and an absolute marvel in terms of action sequences, and yet there isn't a second that goes by that I don't think of Sam Gideon and the thunderous perfection of his combat performance.

Instead of being a turn-based confrontation where both sides take turns behind a wall to exchange bullets, Vanquish is a spiral of destruction that doesn't even stop to reload, presenting a striking contrast to the game we're discussing here.

Would being able to slide at full speed and defeat giant robots in GTA 6 make sense? No. Am I dying to play through Vanquish for the thirtieth time for every occasion I have to stay put in cover? Absolutely.

While this might sound utterly personal, few things please me as much as revisiting a developer's older games after a long time, which brings us to Grand Theft Auto: San Andreas.

Being the franchise's installment I've sunk the most hours into and the reason I love open-world games, it creates an intrinsic incentive for GTA 6 because it allows you to appreciate the passage of time.

Many of the gameplay concepts we enjoy in the franchise today, such as character customization, routines, and minigames, were pioneered by CJ, and taking a step back to appreciate what San Andreas did over 20 years ago is magical.

This recommendation might be harder to justify theoretically because, generally speaking, GTA 6 is a vastly superior version, though the magic of nostalgia is something it won't acquire for another couple of decades, while San Andreas has plenty of that.

This is a brief guide that entails the location of the Fast and Furious green Eclipse in GTA 6.

Rockstar did an exceptional job, like few developers in history, of truly discouraging lethal attacks through a bloody, sensitive, and visceral presentation, though it's ironic that these same qualities are what elevate DOOM Eternal.

Being careful with how you drive or which body parts you shoot is excellent, but I'd be lying if I didn't admit that a part of me misses the raw brutality with which you can manipulate the Doom Guy to commit hellish extermination.

The two titles contrast sharply in both their approach to combat and the ease of movement that enables it, so just thinking about DOOM Eternal's levels, flying all over the place while leaping from execution to execution, makes my mouth water.

Furthermore, being able to shoot an enemy entity in the face with a shotgun without being completely dismayed by my own lack of humanity is a significant bonus, because I do miss being able to disconnect from the consequences of my characters' actions.

GTA 6 is a game of distance, in the sense that its experience focuses heavily on trajectories, the separation between spaces, and the distinct biomes, doing everything Yakuza 0 refuses to do despite also being an open world.

Kamurocho and Sotenbori occupy a tiny fraction of Leonida's map, and there's no vehicle other than your own two feet to traverse them, though this allows you to connect with every back alley and building in a way that no other title has been able to instill in me.

That familiarity and closeness with which Yakuza 0 relates to its inhabitants, locations, and activities is invaluable, and although GTA 6 manages to generate identical emotions, the difference in scale creates an impressive sense of belonging to Kiryu and Majima's journeys.

There are many points of convergence, but the size breaks down the comparison, allowing their divergences to become apparent as the hours pass, thus highlighting the asymmetries that each offers to fans of the genre.

It's clear after just a couple of hours of gameplay that Red Dead Redemption 2 is the primary influence on GTA 6, so we can all agree that we wouldn't be here if it weren't for Red Dead Redemption.

Replacing the noise of neon lights and social media with horses and forests is something Arthur Morgan's game could easily do, but what even the legendary outlaw can't achieve is to also hark back to a time when even design was more contemplative.

Unlike its sequel, Red Dead Redemption is a prototype whose full expression was prevented by technological limitations, resulting in a much quieter, more poetic, and simpler journey, but with the hints of grandeur that its direct and spiritual successor would achieve in 2018 and 2026, respectively.

Therefore, riding back through its empty landscapes, conversing with its few interactive characters, and firing its limited guns is the handbrake for the Pegassi Vitello inside which we find ourselves in GTA 6, letting us breathe fresh air and appreciate the beauty of slower times.

If at any point between 2013 and now you had told me that GTA 6 would be much more like Shenmue than any other video game, I would have thought you needed a psychiatrist, but here we are.

If you're surprised by the attention to detail in drinking a beverage, observing the routines of dozens of NPCs, having to stay fit and healthy, or enjoying complex minigames, let me inform you that Yu Suzuki was already doing it all back in 1999.

While Shenmue is more similar to Yakuza 0 in scale, it also resembles GTA 6 in that both embrace a life simulation design, where walking, talking, playing, interacting, and other such actions aren't driven by the sheer volume of fun but rather by the experience of placing yourself in someone else's shoes.

That talent for immersing you in a reactive, living world that transcends your existence is reserved for the legends, and if you can tolerate the difference in technological advancement and size, Shenmue will easily show you why it feels like a great-great-grandfather of GTA 6.

The latest trailer for Avengers: Doomsday gives us our best look yet at the Avengers in their fight against Doctor Doom. But it's not just the Avengers showing up to fight. With the entire multiverse at stake, pretty much everyone is throwing their hat or pointy helmet into the ring at this point. As well as the main Avengers team, the former Thunderbolts, now known as the New Avengerz, will also suit up alongside the Fantastic Four and the X-Men from the early Fox movies.

It's no wonder that Thor seems a bit confused by all these newcomers. "I don't even know what half of you do," he says in the trailer. "I don't even know who half of you are..." He's probably not the only one either. If you haven't been keeping up with the MCU post-Endgame, a lot has changed since the Infinity Stone saga came to an end in 2019.

While that's par for the course if you're familiar with the comics, casual viewers might be struggling to understand or even care about this vast roster of heroes now. That's especially true given just how long some of these Avengers have been hanging around. Endgame was supposed to bring Steve Rogers' story to a close, yet here he is hogging all the attention again, 15 years after Chris Evans first picked up the shield.

With Chris Hemsworth's Thor and Robert Downey Jr. also returning, albeit in a different role for the latter, this old guard are still pulling focus, which isn't ideal for all the new heroes Marvel has tried to win us over with in Phases Four, Five and Six especially. Endgame was sold as… well, as an endgame, yet that couldn't have been further from the truth.

Things do seem different this time around, though. Given the sheer volume of heroes assembled in Avengers: Doomsday, it stands to reason that some won't survive the film's entire runtime. It's hard enough fitting them all on screen at once if the many, many crammed group shots already shared online are anything to go by.

That's ok though, because Marvel has already lined up replacements for all the Avengers who have outstayed their welcome.

First up are the New Avengerz, Val Cooper's ragtag group of ex-villains who have banded together to redeem themselves. Sentry aside, it's hard to imagine any of them surviving for long against Doctor Doom – good luck to Yelena Belova and her pointy sticks – but survive they will. Or at least, most of them will.

Thunderbolts* wasn't the biggest hit Marvel's ever had at the box office, but some of these characters clearly remain popular nonetheless, and their anti-hero outlook brings a fresh new perspective to the Avengers as a concept. While the team has enlisted heroes with a dodgy past before – think Black Widow, Winter Soldier – few took so much joy in wrongdoing as the majority of these former Thunderbolts did.

It's not just the New Avengerz gunning for a permanent seat at the table. After twenty years of comic book storytelling, it looks like the Young Avengers will officially form at last too, either in Doomsday or its follow-up, Avengers: Secret Wars.

Created by Allan Heinberg and Jim Cheung in 2005, the Young Avengers started out as a group of super-powered teenagers with various ties to longtime members of the main Avengers team. The same is true of their MCU counterparts, including a number of heroes who have been introduced in various films and shows before they (presumably) come together here.

At the end of 2023's The Marvels, Kamala Khan, aka Ms Marvel, explained to Kate Bishop that she's recruiting "kid" heroes to start a team of their own. There haven't been any updates since, aside from news that Kamala was "visiting friends" in California, according to her father during a guest appearance in Daredevil: Born Again. But with Hailee Steinfeld now confirmed to reprise her Hawkeye mentee role in Doomsday, the pieces are all coming together.

Kathryn Newton's Cassie Lang, who just so happens to reside in California, made her debut alongside Kate in the most recent Doomsday trailer, sowing the seeds for an imminent team-up between Ant-Man's daughter and Hawkeye's surrogate daughter.

Plus, it's surely no coincidence that founding members (and brothers) Wiccan and Speed were united at the end of VisionQuest just a few weeks back. The WandaVision spinoff also tied into Doomsday directly with Doctor Doom's surprise recruitment of their mother, Wanda Maximoff, in a post-credits scene. Family reunion, anyone?

Meanwhile, Iman Vellani's Ms Marvel isn't traditionally associated with the Young Avengers, but her plans for a team-up make this version of the character essential to the team's future onscreen. Don't be surprised if Vellani's involvement in the film is officially confirmed at the end of her Countdown to Doomsday podcast, which revisits every Marvel movie in the lead-up to Doomsday.

Three other Young Avengers have already appeared in previous MCU projects too: Kid Loki (Jack Veal) on Loki season 1, America Chavez (Xochitl Gomez) in Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness, and Elijah Bradley/Patriot (Elijah Richardson) in The Falcon and the Winter Soldier.

Any one of them could make their return in the Young Avengers lineup, especially as multiple members showed up last year in concept art by designer Mushk Rizvi (that's been conveniently taken down since). Don't forget Hulk's son either, Wil Deusner's Skaar, who made a brief, silent cameo in the finale of She-Hulk: Attorney at Law.

Whatever happens, it's clear that we don't know the whole story just yet. After all, Marvel is notorious for holding back and even misleading us with fake-outs in their biggest trailers. Many fans even suspect that the final act for Doomsday has been kept out of promotional material entirely so far, and it's almost certain that various characters have been edited out of the shots we have seen.

As such, it's not much of a stretch to imagine that some of our faves might snuff it or retire to make way for a whole new slate of heroes ready to do some avenging on their behalf. And it's about time too.

The longer Marvel remains fixated on replaying their greatest hits, the more desperate and tired the MCU will feel. Thankfully, change is most definitely in the air. Marvel head Kevin Feige says (via EW) that both Doomsday and Avengers: Secret Wars are "passing the torch from one universe to another," describing the latter as "a launching pad for our films in 2028 – the 20th anniversary of the MCU," which will launch "a very new era at that point."

While we know that "new era" will kick off with a new X-Men movie, followed by a third Black Panther film and a new Ghost Rider, three other unspecified Marvel movies remain on the slate. How much do you want to bet a Young Avengers film will be among them?

Thor might not know who these kids are, and casual Marvel fans might not either. But that's going to change real quick once Doomsday and Secret Wars reshuffle the MCU entirely, paving the way for a whole new generation of heroes we can rally behind again.

Culture Is Driving New Interest in Travel for Ramadan

Longer stays and a rise in cultural visits suggest Ramadan is becoming a more significant season in Abu Dhabi’s tourism calendar. The Department of Culture and Tourism - DCT Abu Dhabi now sees an opportunity to turn the holy month into a more deliberate travel proposition for both visitors and the global travel trade.

Ramadan has traditionally been viewed as a quieter month for travel in the Gulf. However, early data from DCT Abu Dhabi’s Ramadan 2026 season suggests that old assumptions about the holy month may be changing. The average length of stay across Abu Dhabi increased 3% year-on-year, while domestic hotel guests stayed 31% longer. Companies selling robot-training video used to call about privacy once a month. Now it’s several times a week

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Simon Randall is the founder and CEO of Pimloc, which makes video-anonymization software.

Earlier this month, India’s technology ministry probed home-services platforms about cleaners wearing head-mounted cameras inside customers’ homes. The footage trains robots, and officials wanted to know how it was collected, what consent was obtained and whether it was going to AI developers overseas. They won’t be the last to ask.

Training robots this way is new (ish), but the questions aren’t. Over a decade ago, I helped build Autographer, one of the first wearable cameras. Back then, the focus was: “how do I know if I am being filmed?” so we designed privacy into the product. Today, because we’re filmed all the time, people (and their governments) ask: “Where is that footage, who has access and what are you doing with it?”

Today I run Pimloc, which sells software to anonymize sensitive video. Much of our work involves police body-camera footage: shaky, often low-light and full of people who aren’t part of the incident. It’s remarkably similar to what robotics firms now collect. We’ve spent years developing technology to protect people in footage where they move quickly, turn away or appear at unusual angles.

A year ago we were approached by companies collecting or preparing footage for physical AI training roughly once a month. Now it’s several times a week – businesses working across autonomous vehicles, delivery robots, factories and drones.

AI chatbots learned to communicate by reading the internet. A robot has as much chance of learning to fold clothes by trawling search engines as I have of becoming a Michelin-starred chef by reading Julia Child’s Mastering the Art of French Cooking.

So developers are recording real life instead. One startup recently told Fortune it had collected more than a million hours of first-person footage in its first month, and we’re hearing similar figures. Robotics and physical-AI companies had raised nearly $48 billion by early October, according to PitchBook data.

Many of the companies that first approached us were using open-source tools to try and redact footage – far from perfect, but it got them through non-public development and testing.

Now things are different in two ways. First, many of those companies are preparing to sell their footage to large AI developers with exacting standards. A buyer spending serious money on a dataset wants assurances about how it was collected, whether permissions cover its intended use and whether the people in it are protected. Suppliers know they will have to stand behind those claims.

Second, governments are paying attention. India’s inquiry is one example in one territory, of growing scrutiny over how AI training footage is collected and used. Big tech companies and labs looking to develop physical AI products and services have little choice but to push those same questions down to the organizations selling them the footage they need.

One company that approached us recently put it simply: “We need to solve this problem. Redaction is important to us.”

Some companies were created specifically to collect training footage. They can build consent and anonymization into their processes from day one. As India shows, that doesn’t settle every concern for workers or the people they film, but it gives them a headstart

Others have recorded video for years for a different purpose, and are only now discovering its new value. But having captured the footage isn’t the same as having the right to sell it.

Recently, an established business that helps people with everyday tasks asked us about anonymizing years of footage captured by their customers for AI training. We had two immediate questions: could permissions realistically be established, years later, for a use nobody – not least the consumer – had envisaged when the footage was recorded? And could every identifying detail – from people in shot to letters on the fridge – be reliably found and protected?

That discussion is still on-going, but it shows how difficult it can be to turn an archive into an asset after the fact.

For any business sitting on a digital archive, my advice is to start by asking: did the people you meant to film agree to the footage being sold? And is everyone else in it protected? You can’t assume the value without identifying those gaps and addressing them.

Buyers are best placed to make those checks standard. Privacy rules and expectations differ from country to country, as India’s scrutiny of overseas transfers shows, and footage is already crossing borders. If the AI companies paying for it insisted on the same proof of permission and protection wherever it was recorded, every supplier would have to meet that bar.

I’m optimistic about what physical AI could do. But the footage that is increasingly being used to teach it captures far more than the task – people nearby, inside homes, documents, voices and screens in the background. Autographer taught me that what a camera picks up by accident matters as much as what it’s pointed at. Physical AI is learning that lesson at a far greater scale, and buyers have both the power and the incentive to act on it.

The Trojan War was in its tenth year. A soothsayer had foretold the Greeks that they would win the war only by retrieving the famed archer, Philoctetes, whose bow was from Heracles and whose arrows never missed their mark. The major problem, though, was that Odysseus and his men had abandoned Philoctetes on their way to Troy those many years earlier. On a stop before they reached Troy, Philoctetes had inadvertently wandered into a sacred area and been bitten by a snake. He developed a wound so suppurative and foul-smelling and cried out in such agony that no one could stand to be with him, and Odysseus ordered his shipmates to drop him off on the deserted island of Lemnos (Sophocles; Heaney, 1961).

For all that time, Philoctetes, miserable and alone, suffered with a virulent, gangrenous, and oozing wound that did not heal, "an insatiable sickness" (Sophocles). All the while, he nursed a grudge against Odysseus and his men, and he was obsessed with the betrayal (Lansky, 2003; Wilson, 2018).

There are passing references to this myth in both Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, as well as in Ovid's Metamorphoses and Virgil's Aeneid, but the main extant source is Sophocles’ play, Philoctetes. The story has also been immortalized in many works of art.

Odysseus — the clever manipulator and far more devious than depicted in Christopher Nolan's recent film, The Odyssey — devised an elaborate plan: He and some of his men would land on Lemnos with Neoptolemus, the young son of Achilles, and trick Philoctetes into coming, with his magic bow, back with them to Troy.

Commanded by Odysseus and threatened with punishment for treason if he would not comply, Neoptolemus was to befriend Philoctetes and explain that he, too, had a grievance against Odysseus. He had reason, therefore, to abandon the Greeks and take Philoctetes home, all the while plotting to bring him to Troy.

But Neoptolemus was a young man of character: "It is not in my nature to practice treachery...Don't you think it shameful to lie?" he asks. "It goes against all I was ever brought up to believe," he adds. (Heaney). "Not if the lie brings deliverance...Troy can be taken only with his bow," responds Odysseus.

Neoptolemus ultimately agrees to accept his commander's duplicitous plan that includes the possibility of stealing the bow and leaving Philoctetes to starve.

Seeing the extraordinary anguish of Philoctetes moves Neoptolemus, as he says, "My heart is bursting with grief at your pain." With renewed courage and honor, he refuses to acquiesce to Odysseus' deception (Segal, 1995). "What is disgusting is when a man abandons his own true nature and acts shamefully," says Neoptolemus.

Though Neoptolemus confesses his real intentions to Philoctetes, it will take a deus ex machina in the form of the appearance of Heracles to convince Philoctetes that his destiny is to accompany Odysseus, Neoptolemus, and the men to Troy. Philoctetes, as foretold, will have his wound healed and will be "hailed as the bravest of all the Greeks" with the fall of Troy: Philoctetes will kill Paris, and Neoptolemus will kill King Priam (Wilson).

Sophocles’ play depicts conflicts between the individual and society (Segal, 1995) and poignantly illustrates the effects of chronic, unremitting disease for which someone is ostracized and abandoned. Most significantly, it reflects the high moral cost of compromising one's integrity (Rangell, 1974), as Neoptolemus had, and experiencing moral dissonance (ter Heide and Olff, 2023; VanderWeele et al, 2025), or moral injury (Shay, 1991; 1994; 2002; 2011; Griffin et al, 2019; Litz, 2025; Litz and Walker, 2025).

Psychoanalytically, this compromise of integrity results from a conflict between the ego, the rational part of the brain, and the superego, one's conscience (Rangell, 1974).

Jonathan Shay is credited with introducing the term moral injury in the context of the military. It is a betrayal of what’s right and a “shrinkage of the moral and social horizon.” Moral hypocrisy occurs when immoral actions occur in otherwise moral people (Naso, 2006).

Shay compared Homeric soldiers with American soldiers: Homeric soldiers respected, even honored their enemy, without derogatory name-calling or dehumanizing them, as was commonly encouraged by some in the American armed forces.

Shay thought of moral injury as psychological wound contamination, a kind of leadership malpractice. There are three parts to his definition: there is a betrayal of what’s right, a high-stakes situation, and someone who holds legitimate authority over a person (2011). Moral distress results from "culturally imbued shared and internalized values" (Griffin et al.).

Others have extended the use of the term to situations beyond the military (Litz, 2025), though there is no consensus definition or gold standard of measurement (Litz and Walker, 2025). Further, research suffers from small sample sizes and even disagreement about what is a potentially morally injurious event (Griffin et al., 2019).

“The human story is replete with individuals, groups, and nations wittingly and unwittingly perpetrating cruel and pernicious acts of inhumanity against others." Psychological consequences ensue. “People can be consumed and haunted by these inhumane acts” (Litz and Walker). Moral injury now includes acts of commission or omission, being the victim of, or bearing witness to acts “that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations” (Litz and Walker).

Situations that can evoke moral injury include the My Lai Massacre in March 1968, where American soldiers slaughtered at least 300 unarmed Vietnamese civilians (Borch, 2018).

The torture and abuse of prisoners by Americans at Abu Ghraib in Iraq in 2003 is another example.

Though the effects of moral injury can overlap with PTSD, the two are distinct. PTSD is often fear-based and involves flashbacks, exaggerated startle reflex, memory loss, nightmares, and insomnia (Griffin et al.).

The syndrome of moral injury, in distinction, does not require a life-threatening event (Litz, 2024). It is "persistent moral distress" (VanderWeel et al.), more likely involving feelings of guilt, shame, anger, anhedonia, social isolation (Griffin et al.), loss of trust in others or institutions, failure to forgive oneself or others, and loss of meaning in life (VanderWeel et al.).

The damage from moral injury can be irreparable and exceptionally difficult to treat. Cognitive-behavioral coping approaches, such as habituation and reappraisal, often fail to relieve the distress (Griffin et al.).
