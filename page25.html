The Airlines With The World's Newest Premium Economy Seats In 2026

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Around the world, premium economy is growing and more airlines are expected to increase their capacity in this class, although it can take years for an airline to fully transition to a new cabin type. Broadly speaking, premium economy is confined to widebody aircraft assigned to longer-haul routes.

However, there are exceptions like American Airlines' transcontinental A321XLRs and Emirates' widebodies that are used for short-haul routes. Widebodies generally fall into three categories as far as rolling out a new seating class is concerned. One category is new aircraft being delivered, which are typically received from the manufacturer with the latest airline cabins.

Another group of aircraft is those already in service with years of service ahead of them. These are retrofitted as they are cycled through their scheduled maintenance. Finally, there are older aircraft in service that are scheduled to retire that an airline may not bother retrofitting.

Originally, all commercial fares were effectively first class, as flying was only something the elite could afford to do. Over time, economy class emerged, and then in the 1970s, business class emerged as a bridge between first and economy. For many business class passengers, first class was excessively expensive, while economy was not befitting of business representatives on business trips.

The class also introduced the notion of working on the aircraft. As air travel became mainstream and everyone could fly, flying lost its prestige status symbol. Private jets may be the domain of the wealthy, but not commercial flying, and no longer did passengers dress up for the occasion and treat it as something of a first-class voyage.

Without the status of the past, the first class became seen as excessively expensive, while also being squeezed by an improving business class. This has led to the decline of first class around the world. Meanwhile, pressures mounted in two directions. One was the increased demand for ultra-cheap and utilitarian flights, which gave rise to low-cost and ultra-low-cost carriers.

Another was the demand for a class better than economy, but not as prohibitively expensive as business class. This led to the rise of premium economy as a 'sweet spot' between affordability and comfort. It fits today's mainline market, where flying needs to be comfortable and affordable, but not overly lavish.

Premium economy is the youngest of the four classes, and many airlines were reluctant to introduce it. Some airlines saw it as a threat that would undermine their profitable business class, as premium economy works if it gets economy-class passengers to upgrade, but it is counterproductive if it convinces business-class passengers to downgrade.

Some mainline carriers, like Emirates, were also concerned about how it would affect their brand image as a luxury mainline airline. Now, however, that dam has burst, and airlines are rushing to retrofit their aircraft with premium economy. Emirates CEO Tim Clark said that "our Premium Economy product was carefully developed in keeping with Emirates’ brand positioning as a full-service airline of the highest quality."

He has also described the demand for Emirates' premium economy as "tremendous" and "shocking" while noting he could sell the seats multiple times. Emirates is a great example of a holdout that has now fully embraced the class.

In 2026, Emirates has not been introducing a new premium economy, as it already exists, but it is rolling it out for more and more routes as aircraft are retrofitted, such as Tokyo to Cairo via Dubai. Lufthansa has gone so far as to call it a "money-generating machine" and claim it generates 33% more per square foot than economy class and 6% more than business class.

While Emirates has its own official Premium Economy, both Qatar and Etihad have their own unique approach to an elevated economy experience.

United Airlines is upgrading its Boeing 787-9's international cabins with a refreshed premium economy class as part of its broader 'Elevated Interior' launch. In May 2025, the carrier said that "United expects to take delivery of the first 787-9 with the Elevated interior before the end of 2025, ​​​​​ with the first international passenger flights planned in 2026 from San Francisco to Singapore and San Francisco to London."

American Airlines' new premium economy debuted in late 2025 on its new Being 787-9 Dreamliners (789P) and A321XLR transcontinental aircraft, while existing 777-300ERs are being retrofitted with the new seats. This is part of American's cabin refresh, and it is the last US-based airline to finally ditch first class, while it rebalances with more premium economy options.

Delta Air Lines is enhancing its premium economy class at the expense of economy class seats, while JetBlue is introducing its Junior Mint seats in 2026. While not typically classified as traditional premium economy, Junior Mint is still presented as a bridge between economy and business.

One holdout appeared to be Alaska Airlines. Its Hawaiian-bound Boeing 787-9 Dreamliners were initially only configured with business and economy class. However, it announced in October that, from 2028, 787s, A330s, and select 737 MAX 10s will get "international-grade premium economy with 38 inches of pitch, leg and calf rests and 16-inch 4K screens."

In Europe, Virgin Atlantic is taking delivery of new A330neo aircraft with expanded premium cabins (56). Elsewhere, Lufthansa is now accepting its new Boeing 787 Dreamliners after delays with the new Allegris cabins, while more A350-900s with Allegris premium economy cabins are delivered. Older Lufthansa aircraft, like 747-8s, are to be retrofitted.

In December 2025, Business Travel News Europe reported that "TAP Air Portugal in summer 2026 will introduce a new premium economy cabin on long-haul flights for its Airbus A330 and A321LR aircraft." It added that this will include 12 seats in three rows of four on these narrowbody aircraft. British Airways is also in the process of refreshing its cabin interiors, including its premium economy seats.

By now, the vast majority of major European full-service airlines with significant long-haul networks have adopted premium economy. However, there are a few holdouts, most notably Ireland's Aer Lingus, Spain's Air Europa, and, until now, Turkish Airlines. Turkish Airlines is, however, relaunching its premium economy after a lengthy hiatus on long-haul flights in 2028.

The refreshed premium economy seats are more comfortable, aesthetically pleasing, and technologically advanced than older seats.

EVA Air unveiled its fourth-generation premium economy seats in early 2025. As a side point, EVA Air is widely credited for having launched the world's first true premium economy class in 1992, which, at the time, was called Evergreen Deluxe. Singapore Airlines and Air France are also among the airlines rolling out refreshed premium economy seats in 2026.

Philippine Airlines has unveiled its first Airbus A350-1000 with a premium economy cabin, and more of these aircraft have arrived in 2026. As stated, one of the biggest stories for premium economy is Emirates, which is estimated to be adding 10 more premium economy routes, bringing the total to over 84. It is rolling out premium across its refreshed Boeing 777s and A380s, while, as a first, its new A350-900s are not being delivered with first class seats in favor of premium economy.

Air New Zealand is among the airlines doubling down on premium economy, introducing increased premium economy capacity on its retrofitted Boeing 787 Dreamliners from late 2025 into 2026. The carrier stated in mid-2025 that "customers travelling to North America will have access to more than 34,000 additional seats, alongside a boost of 20,500 premium seats across the wider long-haul network."

Elsewhere, Thai Airways has plans to add a new premium economy cabin to its 777-300ERs and incoming 787-9s starting around 2027. Meanwhile, new premium economy announcements in 2025 included LATAM saying it would introduce a new Premium Comfort cabin on its Boeing 787-8 and 787-9 starting in 2027.

Premium economy is now the fastest-growing class, having grown by 8% in recent years, ahead of economy's 5.9% growth. In 2027, Germany's leisure carrier, Discover Airlines, is introducing a premium economy cabin known as 'Ocean Blue' on its A330 widebody fleet. flydubai is also adding premium economy for the first time on its new Boeing 787 Dreamliners in 2027.

In contrast, first class seating is down by around 40% compared with 2019. In Q4 of 2025, Delta Air Lines reached a new milestone, as premium cabin revenue exceeded economy revenue. The final quarter saw main cabin revenues fall by 7% from $6 billion to $5.6 billion.

Meanwhile, premium revenues jumped by almost the same amount for the quarter ($473 million), from $5.2 billion in 2024 to $5.7 billion in 2025. Overall, the full-year main economy cabin revenues exceeded those of the premium economy cabin, but it seems that 2025 was the tipping point for Delta.

Royal Navy stands up first autonomous boat squadron

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Force deploys an operational squadron to gain experience in uncrewed systems as part of its Hybrid Navy vision

Britain’s Royal Navy has formally commissioned its first uncrewed boat squadron, operating seaborne drones on front-line operations as part of its “Hybrid Navy” strategy.

The force says the 1st Autonomous Squadron officially entered active service on November 5 at a ceremony in Portsmouth Naval Base. It comprises around 180 personnel, about the same as a Type 23 frigate, split into two watches to enable operations around the clock.

This active squadron replaces Project Beehive, the navy’s experimental program to build a fleet of autonomous and remotely operated vessels, through which it obtained 20 uncrewed boats from UK-based Kraken Technology Group.

The force is understood to be using both Kraken’s K3 Scout, and the SM400 from SYOS Aerospace, known by the Royal Navy as the Rattler.

The K3 Scout is an 8.4 m boat capable of 55 knots and can carry a payload of up to 600 kg. It has a range of up to 650 nautical miles and  endurance of up to 7 days. The maker describes it as capable of a range of missions, including anti-ship and anti-submarine to detecting and countering other drones and even casualty evacuation.

The Rattler boat is 7.2 m and has a 500 kg modular bay, with a range of up to 950 nautical miles. It was used last year in a demonstration during which 5 of the uncrewed boats were remotely-piloted to escort patrol ship HMS Tyne and mine countermeasures mothership HMS Stirling Castle

Part of the new squadron’s mission will be to act as pathfinder for drone operations, working on bringing additional types of uncrewed surface vessel (USVs) into fleet service and conducting a range of operations alongside other organizations – both as single vessels and as part of a task group.

The “Hybrid Navy” concept was outlined by First Sea Lord General Sir Gwyn Jenkins earlier this year, who made the case that uncrewed vessels would be vital in filling the gaps left by the Royal Navy’s much depleted surface fleet.

Speaking at the launch of the autonomous squadron, Fleet Commander Vice-Admiral Steve Moorhouse hailed it as an historic occasion, but warned that the seas are becoming more contested, more dangerous and harder to operate in.

“A hybrid navy is about giving our people better tools. It means more reach, more striking power and more options,” he stated.

Last week, the force also launched strike drones from one of its aircraft carriers for the first time. Nyan one-way drones made by BAE Systems were launched from the deck, as well as a Tekever AR5 fixed-wing uncrewed aircraft, the same kind the British Army has selected to replace its troublesome Watchkeeper fleet.

Elsewhere, the Ministry of Defence (MoD) says it has launched a British-built satellite called Titania into orbit, claiming it “gives the Armed Forces a new pair of eyes in space.” However, the original purpose of the orbiter was understood to be to test high-speed optical communications from low Earth orbit directly to Earth, using laser beams that are harder to intercept than radio links.

Titania is the second satellite in the UK’s growing constellation of military satellites in orbit, following the launch of Tyche in October, and will be operated by UK Space Command as part of the ISTARI* program.

The latter aims to deliver satellites and supporting ground systems to expand space-based intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance, the MoD said.

However, while the satellite is British-made, it got into space via an American rocket, riding on SpaceX’s Transporter-18 mission. ®

President Donald Trump says the Iran War is effectively over, but Bitcoin and financial markets face another potentially turbulent week. Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller has warned that stubborn inflation could justify a 50-basis-point rate hike in December. Meanwhile, Kalshi traders have raised the odds of a US Treasury Bitcoin purchase this year to 13%.

The three developments put geopolitics, monetary policy and Bitcoin adoption attentions being rekindled. Trump’s latest comments could ease concerns about prolonged military escalation, while Waller’s warning raises questions about borrowing costs. Bitcoin, meanwhile, is attracting attention from prediction markets betting on a possible government purchase.

Trump told reporters on Monday that the Iran War was “militarily effectively over,” suggesting Washington sees little immediate need for further military action. Speaking at the White House after meeting Qatar’s prime minister, Trump said negotiations were moving in a positive direction. He also suggested that an agreement could bring the conflict to an end fairly soon.

However, Washington has not completely ruled out further strikes. Trump said American forces remained ready to respond if circumstances changed, keeping some uncertainty around the negotiations.

Oil prices follow with a decline. Brent crude fell 5.85% last night before slipping another 1.7% to around $90.99 per barrel this morning. The retreat reflects easing concerns over prolonged supply disruptions in the Middle East.

How could it affect crypto? With the Iran war ending, cheaper oil could offer some relief for Bitcoin by reducing inflationary pressure and giving the Federal Reserve more room to eventually lower interest rates. Energy prices influence Bitcoin primarily through inflation expectations, monetary policy, and market liquidity.

Before Trump’s statement, the conflict had already created significant pressure across energy markets. Higher fuel prices have contributed to inflation concerns, complicating the Federal Reserve’s efforts to bring price growth back toward its 2% target.

An end to the Iran War could remove one important source of energy-market uncertainty.

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller has introduced another complication for investors. Just last night, Waller said the Fed could consider a 50-basis-point increase in December if October inflation data show persistent pressure in core prices.

He warned that delivering only a 25-basis-point increase could undermine the Fed’s credibility if core inflation remains stubbornly high. However, Waller currently leans toward a smaller increase if inflation improves.

A 50-basis-point move would represent two standard quarter-point increases delivered in a single meeting. It would also show that policymakers remain prepared to prioritize inflation control over easing financial conditions.

The possibility matters for Bitcoin because higher interest rates generally make speculative investments less attractive. Investors may favor cash and yield-bearing assets when borrowing costs rise, and liquidity becomes more expensive.

The Iran War adds another layer to that calculation. Even if military tensions ease, energy prices and inflation could keep the Fed under pressure.

Its planned November 26 presale conclusion is approaching, leaving buyers with a narrowing window before the next stage of the project.

The combination of early-stage pricing, staking incentives, and Bitcoin-focused infrastructure gives the project several potential demand drivers. Its upcoming launch could become an important test of investor appetite for the next generation of Bitcoin Layer 2 networks.

For now, investors have three competing forces to watch: the possibility of an Iran War resolution, a more aggressive Federal Reserve, and growing speculation about government Bitcoin accumulation.

Kim Hellberg issues emphatic response to Crystal Palace links and questions over Middlesbrough future

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Middlesbrough sit 2nd in the Championship table as they battle for promotion to the Premier League. Boro have spent big on star men like Will Lankshear, but a lot of their success is put down to the work of manager Kim Hellberg.

The Swede’s success has not gone unnoticed either, earning wide praise and drawing interest from elsewhere. Crystal Palace are set to sack Pierre Sage and Hellberg has been named among the contenders, but speaking to Fotbollskanalen, he insisted there is nothing in the rumours and he remains committed to the job at the Riverside Stadium.

“Absolutely nothing, absolutely nothing,” Hellberg said when asked about the interest. “It’s always fun to be on lists if that’s the case, it shows that you’ve done something good. After that, I think it’s mostly talk and for me it’s about doing a damn good job here.”

Hellberg is approaching a year in charge of Middlesbrough, having arrived in late November 2025 after Rob Edwards’ departure to Wolves. There were justified worries when Boro had their boss poached, but the Swede has been quite the hit on Teesside.

His entertaining style of play encourages positional freedom and it makes for a great watch. Most importantly though, it delivers the results needed too.

At 38, Hellberg is one of Europe’s most exciting young bosses and that means interest from the likes of Crystal Palace is inevitable. However, he’s happy at an ambitious club that hopes to match his upward trajectory.

Hellberg is a massive asset for Boro, and they know it. It is why they were willing to back him with expensive deals to sign Lankshear, Ashley Phillips and Radek Vitek, and why players of that calibre were willing to move to the Riverside Stadium.

Hellberg’s comments show he is happy at Middlesbrough and committed to their bid to return to the Premier League. It’s little wonder he’s content – the boss has been heavily backed financially, has a playing squad invested in his ethos on and off the pitch, and has the adoration of the Boro faithful.

However, given his potential in the dugout, the club have to keep progressing to keep him happy. If Hellberg’s progress maintains at this trajectory and Middlesbrough can not match it, opportunities elsewhere will start to become more tempting.

It means promotion is key this season. Boro are on course to achieve that feat as they sit 2nd in the table, but avoiding further heartbreak is a must if Hellberg is to stay committed for the long term.

Australia plans AI safety regulator after OpenAI agent breach

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthony Albanese accepted all 18 recommendations of a review into how an OpenAI agent accessed a Medicare data portal.

Australia will set up a regulator for AI safety and force AI companies to report hacks and other serious incidents to the government, Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said on Tuesday. The move comes after an OpenAI agent got into a government website holding Medicare data earlier this year.

Speaking at a press conference in Canberra, Albanese said the rules would form part of Australia’s upcoming AI law. The new regulator will also be able to impose large fines.

Under the plan, AI developers will have to build security into every stage of making their models and manage serious risks. They will also have to alert the government quickly when something goes wrong, let outside experts test their systems and publish their safety plans.

The government also released the 18 recommendations of a rapid review into the OpenAI incident and said it had accepted all of them.

“This incident was completely unacceptable as I outlined to OpenAI’s CEO at the time, and it can’t be allowed to happen again,” Albanese said.

The review says AI firms should report when their systems are hacked or act in ways they were not built to. It also calls for tougher criminal laws and closer work with other countries and with the AI companies themselves.

Deputy Prime Minister Richard Marles, who appeared alongside Albanese, said the OpenAI case was the first incident of its kind anywhere in the world to be reported to a government. The main lesson, he said, was that Australia had to work closely with the companies building the most advanced AI.

“This is not a technology that we can run away from,” Marles said.

He added that new models should be tested independently, which in Australia would be done by the AI Safety Institute. The review also wants the institute to work more closely with the country’s cyber agencies. Still, Marles said the review had found the country’s cyber defences to be strong.

Home Affairs and Cyber Security Minister Tony Burke, meanwhile, warned that the real danger lies in how fast AI can find weak spots in software.

“Artificial intelligence doesn’t of itself create vulnerabilities, but it finds them, and it changes the pathway to exploiting them,” he said.

According to Burke, AI has cut the time needed to exploit a security flaw from about two and a half years to about 10 hours. That is why companies must tell the government straight away when an AI system ‘goes rogue’, he said. He also wants criminal law updated to cover AI systems that act on their own.

“It should never be the case that there’s an argument that someone didn’t know the computer was loaded,” Burke said.

In addition, he said AI companies should be able to share technical details of an incident with the government without legal risk, as cybersecurity law already allows. When asked about Chinese AI models, he said that was a matter for the security agencies, and pointed to Australia’s early move to restrict DeepSeek on government devices.

The breach itself happened in June, when an OpenAI agent accessed a statistics portal. OpenAI discovered it in August and first emailed a general government inbox on September 10.

No personal details were taken, according to Albanese, and the agent was looking for data on how much medicine was being used, and Marles said those figures were totals that have since been published.

Asked whether OpenAI had broken the law, Albanese did not answer directly. Instead, he said the law had to keep up with a fast-changing world. The full review will be published, with national security details removed.

It is not the first time OpenAI has faced such questions. Earlier this year, the company confirmed that one of its AI systems had broken out of a test environment and got into systems at Hugging Face.

Albanese was also asked about Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang’s admitted affair with a woman accused of spying for China. He called the claims very serious and said they should be properly investigated, adding that Australia was not directly involved.

Jensen Huang and the end of the unchecked CEO

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Good morning from Washington. I’m here for the B20 USA Summit, hosted by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, which is also partnering with us for the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit next week. I’ll bring you insights from conversations with leaders here tomorrow. Today, let’s look at the fallout of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s affair with an alleged Chinese spy. I suspect we’ll look back at this moment as the capstone on an era of staggeringly poor judgment and self-interest at the highest levels of leadership.

The cautionary tale starts with the CEO, a former dishwasher at Denny’s who created one of the most consequential and valuable technologies in history, only to tarnish that legacy with a colossal failure of judgment that put his company, career, and possibly the security of both his countries at risk. (Huang is a dual citizen of Taiwan and the U.S.) It also includes board members who expressed “confidence in Jensen” when announcing their independent review. Add in the political leaders who’ve reportedly known about this potentially catastrophic conflict of interest for at least six months.

In any other period, at almost any other company, Huang would likely have been fired or put on leave for a lapse of this scale. Investors are no doubt furious as the stock dropped 4.3% yesterday and has lost more than a fifth of its value over the past month. The board is trying to find out the extent of the deceit. And tech CEOs may find Washington now demanding the kind of oversight that defense contractors take for granted. The lessons?

Beware the cult of personality. There’s much to admire about Jensen Huang, from his humble beginnings to his energetic keynotes, cool leather jacket and patina of common sense in a world where his peers can come off as a little odd. But focusing on a CEO’s personality can distract us from their actions. (Exhibit A: Elizabeth Holmes.) Huang’s so-called founder mode, with 60 direct reports and no 1:1 meetings, also creates a structure that makes him hard to replace. How do you develop a leadership bench when everyone reports to you? Nvidia is built around one man.

Beware the enamored board. Nvidia’s board appears to have failed in its primary task of keeping a strong CEO in check while having a succession plan in place. I was chatting with Yale’s Jeffrey Sonnenfeld yesterday, who described it as a “board imbued with idolatry” that appears to have no Plan B should Huang be judged unfit to lead. That’s not how this process is supposed to work. Done well, you get a board director like former Johnson & Johnson CEO Jim Burke, who, as an independent director at IBM, was key in replacing failing CEO John Akers with Lou Gerstner in 1993. Done badly, you get the debacle at HP, which in 2010 fired CEO Mark Hurd over a close personal relationship with a contractor—and falsified expense reports related to it—only to reach for outsider Leo Apotheker, who lasted less than a year.

Beware unfettered AI leaders. Huang sits at the center of an ecosystem that shapes national competitiveness, security, and potentially the future of humanity itself. This scandal could reinforce the argument that AI companies should be regulated along the same lines as defense contractors like Lockheed Martin, RTX, and General Dynamics, with standardized security clearances and protocols. Did Huang ever wonder how a former TV journalist in Beijing suddenly became managing director of an obscure little cloud company that also suddenly had the means to buy millions of dollars of sophisticated Nvidia chips? Imagine the panic at learning the U.S. government knows you have a mistress and suspects she’s a spy for China’s Ministry of State Security. Did any government leaders feel an urgency to take action?

73 Years Later, John Wayne's Greatest Final Quote From 'Hondo' Still Officially Defines the Western Genre

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

What defines a genre? Is it a set of pre-established or agreed upon conventions? Is it a specific aesthetic or sort of character followed? When it comes to the Western, the genre is recognizable by those who know it best—and sometimes, that includes the characters within it. If there’s a single concluding line spoken by John Wayne in his big-screen horse operas that most perfectly defines the genre, it’s undoubtedly Hondo Lane's final remarks about the Apache in Hondo. In just a few short words, the hero makes it clear what this genre is truly all about.

As the titular hero watches the Apache take off in retreat, hoping to regroup before their next conflict with the U.S. Cavalry, Buffalo Baker (underrated Hollywood talent Ward Bond) notes that this will be the end of the tribe. “Yeah. End of a way of life. Too bad, it's a good way,” Hondo replies before urging the wagons forward. It’s a small moment of pause and reflection that, on the surface, appears to be nothing more than Hondo acknowledging the fading dominion of the Apache. However, upon further examination, we can see that it’s a line that speaks rivers of truth into the genre itself. The Western is largely about a period in American history that bridges the gap between the untamed West and the civilization that followed. Countless Western protagonists, Hondo included, are caught between the gun-toting life they’ve always known and the coming progress that threatens to strip down that identity for the sake of a more "tamed" society.

Many a Western teases the “end of the Wild West.” It's a common theme in the genre that reaches from some of the Duke's earliest pictures to his last. In this regard, perhaps Hondo is no different. The Western hero, unless domesticated, has no place in this new, "civilized" world. This line uttered by the Duke reflects the constant struggle that genre heroes—including folks like Alan Ladd’s Shane, Paul Newman and Robert Redford’s Butch and Sundance, or even Clint Eastwood’s “Man With No Name”—endure with each new interpretation. Hondo may be one of the best examples of the basic horse opera plot—one based on a short story by famed Western author Louis L'Amour—but it concludes with a sober-minded reflection that speaks to the Western genre as a whole, no matter what form it takes. (Consider reading the novelization, by the way, which L'Amour penned himself and can easily be finished in a weekend!)

The Western "way" of living was always best embodied on the big screen by John Wayne. Hondo is just one of many examples of Duke-led horse operas that could easily define the genre. The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance, Stagecoach, The Cowboys, Rio Bravo, True Grit, and, of course, The Searchers are the most obvious films that come to mind. But none of them conclude with these simple words that remind the viewer just what the genre is about — and just like the Wild West era of history came to an end, so too does the Western film continue to remind us that this brief window into American history is a limited period, and the Western heroes are no more. Perhaps this side of Wayne's decades-long career, we can best understand that idea. After all, the Western genre is not at the same height that it was when it dominated film, television, and radio in postwar America.

Perhaps there's something to the fact that the genre's golden age concluded in the mid-1970s, around the same time John Wayne himself rounded out his career. In 1975, Gunsmoke, the long-running television series that Wayne helped launch in 1955, came to an abrupt end the same way the Wild West era did itself. A year later, the Duke's final on-screen role as J.B. Books in The Shootist premiered in theaters. This was a far cry from Hondo, but there's no doubt that, by looking at the past, the 1953 picture was able to predict the Western's future. Of course, the Western itself has never completely died. Its popularity may have waned over the years, but the horse opera continues to captivate viewers today for the same reason it did back when Hondo hit theaters — folks are looking back at a time long past, hoping it will reveal something more about our future.
