The market bloodbath just exposed Wall Street’s true fears about U.S. debt, top economist warns

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There have been competing theories to explain why Treasury yields have risen so much this year, but last week’s brutal stock selloff debunked one of them, according to a top economist.

The 10-year yield has eased in recent weeks as investors seek shelter in Treasuries. But before the latest dip, it shot up more 100 basis points, soaring above 5% and at one point hitting the highest level in 24 years.

The run-up began after the Iran war started in late February, sending oil prices up and raising expectations that the Federal Reserve will hike rates to fight suddenly resurgent inflation.

At the same time, hyperscalers have been flooding the bond market with fresh debt to finance massive capital spending and the AI infrastructure boom, which has kept the U.S. economy running hot and inflation sticky.

That’s competed for investors’ dollars as the Treasury Department taps the bond market to finance the federal government’s $2 trillion budget deficit—and pay interest of the $40 trillion in U.S. debt.

Some on Wall Street have downplayed the spike in yields, saying the deluge of AI debt has crowded out U.S. bonds and forced the Treasury to offer investors higher returns at auctions.

But Robin Brooks, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution, doesn’t buy that explanation. In a Substack post on Saturday, he said the past week’s market selloff represented a “natural experiment” that puts the competing arguments to the test.

“On one end of the spectrum are people like me who think this is a rising fiscal risk premium as our public debt grows out of control,” he wrote. “On the other end are folks who say it’s hyperscaler debt issuance that’s pushing up yields, so there’s no sign fiscal policy’s in trouble.”

Brooks said corporate borrowing is nowhere near where it was in the IT bubble of the early 2000s, while public borrowing is historically high and more likely driving up yields.

He pointed to the bond market’s reaction to the latest monthly jobs report, which showed payrolls grew by more than double the consensus estimate.

Compared to previous upside job surprises, the two-year yield rose more than what hot economic growth could explain, undercutting the idea that U.S. growth depends on AI as well as the argument that AI is responsible for higher yields.

Brooks also cited the market-implied yield for a 10-year security 10 years from now, which gauges the premium investors demand for the risk of holding Treasuries long term. Such risks include the trajectory of U.S. debt and deficits.

If the AI bubble was pushing up long-term yields, then this “10y10y forward yield” would’ve fallen alongside the 10-year and two-year yields.

“It didn’t and that in my mind further cements the case for the fiscal risk premium,” he said.

“The midterm elections and the AI sell-off that’s ensued have given us the perfect natural experiment to disentangle what’s going on with long-term yields. They’re up because of mounting fiscal stress, not because of AI.”

U.S. debt warnings have been piling up recently. Last month, the Treasury Department’s final tally on fiscal year 2026 showed that the federal budget deficit widened to $1.99 trillion, 12% worse than the $1.78 trillion gap in fiscal 2025.

That came as revenue increased 3% to $5.4 trillion, lagging behind the 6% bump in spending to $7.4 trillion. Social Security and Medicare were the biggest line items, but in third place was interest payments on the national debt, topping defense spending and Medicaid.

Gross interest costs for fiscal 2026 came in at $1.36 trillion, Treasury data show, with an average interest rate of 3.53%. That’s more than double the rate in 2021, just before the Federal Reserve embarked on an aggressive tightening cycle to rein in inflation.

Net interest payments rose 8% to $1.05 trillion, outpacing increases in total spending, revenue and GDP. In fact, it exceeds real GDP growth of about 2% as well as nominal GDP growth of about 6%.

Fiscal 2026’s net interest costs translate to about $2.9 billion a day, and the Peter G. Peterson Foundation estimated they represent 3.2% of GDP, breaking the previous record set in 1991.

“Higher interest begets more debt, and greater debt begets higher rates and higher interest. Eventually, the whole thing could come crashing down,” Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget President Maya MacGuineas said in September.

“That’s why Washington needs a fiscal intervention now, before it’s too late. The President, the Treasury Secretary, and Congressional leadership need to understand the severity of this issue. There are no easy fixes, and we can’t just wish this problem away.”

This X-Men-Esque '80s Sci-Fi Series Launched Courteney Cox's Career

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The premise of James D. Parriott's 1985 superhero/comedy series "Misfits of Science," which featured a young Courteney Cox, will sound very familiar to fans of Marvel Comics' X-Men titles.

The series follows a human scientist named Dr. Billy Hayes (Dean Paul Martin), who has taken an interest in what he called "human anomalies," which are people who have been born with a variety of unpredictable superpowers. In the show's pilot episode, he assembles the main cast of powerful castoffs with the intention of studying them, but also helping them come to terms with their supernatural abilities.

The team was small but mighty. It included Dr. Hayes' lab partner, Dr. Lincoln (Kevin Peter Hall), who can shrink for a limited period. Additionally, Mark Thomas Miller plays "Johnny B" Bukowski, a rocker who can suck up electricity and who wears shades to cover his glowing eyes. He can throw lightning bolts.

Most strikingly, Courteney Cox, future star of "Friends" and "Scream," plays Gloria, a troubled teen with "Carrie"-like telekinetic powers. "Misfits of Science" wasn't Cox's first credit: She had appeared in an episode of "As the World Turns" back in 1975, when she was still a kid, and famously danced with Bruce Springsteen in the "Dancing in the Dark" music video in 1984. "Misfits of Science," though, was the first time Cox starred in a TV series.

"Misfits of Science" is not currently available to stream, but resourceful media hunters will be able to find episodes online easily enough. It's a whimsical, lighthearted show that prodded at the conventions of superheroes as much as it vaunted them. It's definitely worth a glance.

"Misfits of Science," it should be noted, only lasted for a single 16-episode season, so it's okay if you don't remember it.

The series does have something of a superhero legacy, as R. Timothy Kring, a.k.a. Tim Kring, wrote an episode of "Misfits of Science," and he would go on to create the hit series "Heroes," the first season of which remains a must-watch (the later seasons, though, less so). Kring's "Misfits" episode, called "Twin Engines," is about a young man (Joel Polis) who is plagued with uncontrollable visions. It's eventually revealed that he was sharing the visions of his long-lost twin brother.

Indeed, the entire show was based on a long series of super-powered shenanigans and investigations. Mostly, the protagonists get involved in run-of-the-mill spy plots. The main characters aren't costumed vigilantes, but they do seem to operate as freelance spies. Famed drummer Mikey Jones appears in the show's pilot as a character with freezing powers named Ice Man. According to Giant Freakin' Robot, that character was abandoned after Marvel Comics "raised objections" over its own Ice Man character. And speaking of Marvel, Courteney Cox's character was very similar to the X-Men's Jean Gray.

Some fun trivia: Shortly after the time "Misfits of Science" was being made, Dr. Lincoln actor Kevin Peter Hall (who played the original Predator in John McTiernan's sci-fi action classic) was in contention to play either Data or Geordi La Forge on "Star Trek: The Next Generation." /Film has previously talked about all the finalists for the roles, and Hall eventually lost the roles to Brent Spiner and LeVar Burton, respectively.

As mentioned, "Misfits of Science" was not successful. Thanks to low ratings, the series was canceled before all of its episodes could even air, leaving one of them unaired in the United States.

Courteney Cox continued to work undeterred, of course. In 1987, she appeared in the low-budget, high-profile Cannon adaptation of "Masters of the Universe," a movie so goofy, it has been subjected to a "How Did This Get Made" oral history. That same year, she began playing the recurring role of Lauren Miller on the hit sitcom "Family Ties." By 1994, she was appearing in the comedy "Ace Ventura: Pet Detective" and began playing one of the six lead roles on "Friends." "Misfits of Science" was her first starring role, but it certainly wasn't her last.

The safest way to store your laptop for a long period of time

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Maybe you're jetting off on a much-needed vacation. Maybe you've upgraded to a new machine but want to keep the old one on hand. There are plenty of reasons you might need to stop using a laptop for a couple of weeks or months. When you do find yourself in that situation, take a few precautions to keep the computer at its best whenever you're ready to use it again.

First things first: back up all your data before you put your laptop in storage. There's no need to risk losing valuable on the off chance something does go awry while it's in hibernation. So before you say goodbye to your laptop for a significant stretch of time, make at least one backup.

The main reason a laptop needs some TLC ahead of storage is the lithium-ion battery it contains. These power sources need to be properly maintained in order to keep functioning. If you can safely remove the laptop's battery and store it separately, do it. If you can't remove the battery, there are still straightforward steps to keep it in the best condition.

Temperature is one of the main factors that can mess with a battery, and problems can arise if the fluids inside get too hot or too cold. That means you'll want to store your laptop in a temperature-controlled place. That includes keeping it away from a furnace, radiator, or other appliances that might emit a lot of heat. Moisture is also bad for your computer, so pick a place that isn't damp or humid.

Another important aspect of long-term battery maintenance centers on how much it discharges and how quickly. Even if the hardware is completely powered off, a lithium-ion battery will still lose power over time, and it can over-discharge if it sits unused for too long. The best way to handle long-term storage is to have the battery charged to about 50 percent before you put it away. Essentially, you're putting it back in the same state it was when the laptop first shipped to you from a warehouse.

Ideally, even if you aren't planning to use the device for a long time, it's best to occasionally check on the laptop and give it a little juice. Every six months, plug the computer back in and charge the battery back to about 50 percent. It's a small bit of maintenance that can help prevent long-term damage to the battery.

Beyond battery care, a few common-sense protections matter when storing a laptop. It can be tough to find enough space, but you'll want to put the laptop in a location where nothing else gets placed on top of it, especially not anything that might be considered heavy. Even for a short time, an object pressing down on a laptop can damage the lid or the screen below it.

In a similar vein, it's a good idea to clean the screen and keyboard before storage. Wiping down the laptop's inner surfaces will ensure that nothing gets trapped while it's shut for a long time, which can also potentially cause screen damage.

Finally, completely power down the computer and disconnect it from its AC power source. Storing it in a case or in the packaging you bought it in can offer a little added protection while it's out of sight. With a little bit of preparation and maintenance, your laptop will be ready to roll whenever you bring it out of storage.

‘Passing Strange’ Comes to Pasadena Playhouse: Creators Stew and Heidi Rodewald on the Rock Musical Reaching L.A. Almost Two Decades After Tony-Winning Broadway Run

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Senior Music Writer and Chief Music Critic

“Passing Strange” will seem just a little less strange to theatergoers now than it might have when it opened on Broadway in 2007 and scored big wins at the Tonys, the Drama Desk Awards and the New York Drama Critics’ Circle Awards. Without naming any names, there are plenty of successful shows since then that have taken cues from the rock musical, from how it put social and racial issues up front for its fouth-wall-breaking protagonist to having a live band on stage. The only truly strange thing, maybe, at this late date? Why it took so long for the show to get an L.A. production. After all, Stew and his creative partner Heidi Rodewald used to be prominent figures on the Los Angeles music scene, as members of the critically hailed band the Negro Problem, before the development of this show took them to New York in the early 2000s. So they’ve always a loyal audience of rock fans in SoCal eagerly waiting for the show to take root here, too, on top of Angeleno theater buffs who never got to see the show back east. Now it’s finally happening, thanks to director Zhailon Levingston, who won the Tony this year for best direction of a musical for “Cats: The Jellicle Ball.” When he sat down with the top folks at Pasadena Playhouse to discuss what he might like to do here, giving “Passing Strange” its proper SoCal premiere was at the top of his list.

Stew and Rodewald will not be performing in the show in Pasadena, as they did throughout its entire Broadway and pre-Broadway runs. (Neither will Colman Domingo, who had a supporting role, pre-fame, on Broadway. You can see all of those performances in the film version Spike Lee directed.) Vintage Trouble singer Ty Taylor stars in this production as the Stew stand-in known only as Narrator, along with Brittany Campbell (pictured above), Britton Smith and Saycon Sengbloh. Stew and Rodewald are as eager as anyone to see what Levingston has done with a project that takes elements of Stew’s real coming-of-age and sets them to rock ‘n’ roll. The pair Zoomed in with Variety to discuss the history and impact of the show, as well as reveal some projects now in the works.

The two of you aren’t directly working on this Pasadena Playhouse direction, but more like watching appreciatively from afar how it’s going, right?

Stew: Oh, yeah. No, we wouldn’t dare. Zhailon Levingston was sweet enough at one point to invite us to peek in, but he’s a total visionary, so all we could do at this point would be drag it down. First he knows the play better than we do. Secondly, if you’ve ever seen his work, like the work he did in “Cats: The Jellicle Ball,” he’s honestly the last person I would give any advice to.

Rodewald: The way he talks about “Passing Strange” just makes us think: Whatever you’re going to do, you get it.

Stew: As I was just telling him just a few minutes ago, it was made for him. We don’t make art for the contemporary world. The stuff that’s moved me and Heidi for the most part has always been those early morning moments when you go home with somebody and they wanna show you that they’re cool, so they put on a record that you’ve never heard before. I call that 3 a.m. music, and I think that’s what we make. I think the Negro Problem are a 3 a.m. band. “Passing Strange” is a 3 a.m. musical. It’s not something you’re gonna get fed by the mainstream, but somebody very cool is gonna turn you onto it. You don’t necessarily make a lot of money that way, but that’s not why we’re in this, obviously.

Here in L.A., remembering your band as an L.A. band originally, I remember hearing of the show when it was first playing at the Public and then coming to Broadway, thinking that it’d be great to see when you brought it to L.A. I couldn’t have realized that would be a 22-year journey.

Stew: I know. We didn’t either. “Passing Strange” has been done in almost every major city except L.A. It’s been done in South America; it’s been done in Europe — so, yeah, it was starting to stick out in a kind of a weird way. But, see, we’ve always had a adversarial relationship with both Los Angeles and to the L.A. press, particularly the L.A. Times. So we were sort of not surprised at all, but other people would always come to us and go, “What’s up? The play won a Tony! Why aren’t you guys in your hometown?” And we’re like, “Uh, I don’t know.” … It’s certainly nothing we’ve lost sleep over. Having a London production to me was a dream come true, so it’s all good. But the good news is that this amazing director is doing it in L.A., so who cares about time? It’s gonna be worth the wait.

Going back to the very beginnings of the show, weren’t you kind of developing some kind of preliminary version of it in clubs?

Stew: Yeah, we had this thing called “Travelogue” that we did at some places in New York, which was sort of a cabaret version of us talking about different places that we had lived and different experiences — a very primitive version in Joe’s Pub and a couple of other clubs where we did stuff in New York, and that’s when the word started to get around. We were approached by the Public Theater. We weren’t petitioning to make a play. They approached us and said, “Would you like to make it a theater (piece)?” We said no three times, and then finally said yes.

You’ve said that Broadway was not an aspiration of yours, ever. It was not your dream come true.

Rodewald: No, and that’s why it ended up being what it is, because we weren’t thinking that way at all. We weren’t trying to please people. We were just pleasing ourselves. I think we really held onto that. … It was this crazy surreal thing. And then we did have choices of doing it a different way or being on Broadway, and we went with that. We didn’t have to go to Broadway; we could have been one of those cool shows that goes on and on forever, off-Broadway. But it wasn’t like “Oh my God, this is all we’ve ever wanted.”

Stew: It really helped being a rock band, because I guess there must be some rock bands that get really overly excited about things, but I think a lot of rock bands are like us: we’re very jaded. Don’t forget, we’re old enough to have experienced the implosion of the music business itself. So we remember the point where people were trying to get record deals, and then we also were there when people are like, “Fuck a record deal. Let’s make the record ourselves.” Which is what we did.

We were in the office with Lenny Waronker, talking about a record deal, and they did give us $10,000. Woo, right? And we took that $10,000 and instead of making their demos, we took that $10,000 and made our record with it. So technically our second record is a DreamWorks project, but they don’t know it. Their accountant probably knows it, but they don’t.

At what point did you go east from L.A. to New York?

Stew: Yeah, we were playing in New York a lot when we were touring a lot, but I guess 2000 was probably where we really started spending more time. The best thing you can do in New York is not tell people you moved there. When you’ve moved there, you tell people you’re just visiting so they treat you better.

Rodewald: We haven’t talked about this in a long time. With “Passing Strange,” we get this commission, and then we’re working at the Public Theater, and they’re sending us away to do workshops and it’s like, “Wow, they’re giving us money, so we can just keep going.” In the middle of this, Stew and I are still making albums and touring, so we’re not banking everything on it; we’re just doing this other thing. And one of the great things that happened was, by the time Broadway happened, we still didn’t officially live in New York, so they had to put us up. Otherwise, how would we have done that?

Stew: Sort of like all the luxuries we never got with a big record deal, suddenly theater people were giving it to us, and they were much nicer.

Rodewald: At one point, we were at Sundance, and I remember looking at you and going, “They actually care! Like, no, they actually do.”

And that was not your experience with the record business.

Stew: I feel like record business was just a lot of suits pretending that they get you, but not really. Theater people are really into lyrics, for instance. So that for us was something very astonishing. People liked our stage show and our outlandish name and our ambitious music or whatever, but people in L.A. didn’t really say much about lyrics. Some people did, but not like press and stuff. Then suddenly we go to New York, and the theater people are like, “Whoa, we love these lyrics.”

Also, it’s a touchy subject because I know a lot of our L.A. people feel funny about the fact that we really did have to go to New York in order to make a living from this. Because we were selling out Spaceland on Saturday night, but there was a glass ceiling, and we couldn’t get that big record deal that was no longer around, or no longer around for a band like us. That wasn’t a possibility anymore. Suddenly in New York we had a career and could make a living. It’s always weird when hometown people leave and go elsewhere, and things happen.

I have a lot of friends, and Heidi has a lot of friends, who, back in the day, would get a big advance from a record company, and they’d be crawling the carpets with all the cocaine and Gil Turner (high-end liquor) deliveries. And the thing is, theater doesn’t give you Gil Turner money. It doesn’t give you cocaine money up front. But it does provide you with everything else you need to create. “We’re gonna send you away on a nice retreat in the cool countryside or the hills or Berlin.” They give you everything you need in assistance, but you’re not gonna become a coke addict on a theater budget. And that’s good!

Rodewald: It was amazing, but kind of embarrassing, to tell peole, “Well, we have a week, and we’re working on this, and we’re getting paid fairly.”

Let’s talk about the impact “Passing Strange” had on Broadway. Did you arrive at a point where you were able to recognize the influence it had?

Stew: For sure. After us, there were two things that were very clear. One is that every show wanted to have a band on stage. That was almost immediate. This year marked the 15th anniversary of the gala, which honors artists and filmmakers while raising funds to support the museum’s programming. The evening, sponsored by Gucci and co-chaired by Eva Chow and Leonardo DiCaprio, would celebrate Latvian artist Vija Celmins and filmmaker Denis Villeneuve. The art world and Hollywood are not always natural bedfellows, although they have certainly become more comfortable with one another over the years. I was interested to see how the two would behave over dinner.

I arrived fashionably early, both because I am extremely punctual and partly because I have developed an aversion to the chaos that accompanies certain Los Angeles events. Anya Taylor-Joy was making her way towards the photographers in an archival Pierre Cardin sculptural gown and jacket looking like a beautiful futuristic loofah. I spotted the sparkle of her Tiffany & Co. Schlumberger Stars and Moons necklace before I even registered who was wearing it. The necklace was doing its own job of announcing her.

As she approached the carpet, which was lined with photographers and press, I was surprised that nobody was yelling. The atmosphere was remarkably calm, a far cry from some of the movie premiere assignments I’ve attended, where you can barely hear yourself think over the screaming of fans and photographers. Behind the carpet was the backdrop that has become something of a celebrity in its own right: Chris Burden’s Urban Light, the monumental installation made up of more than 200 restored cast-iron antique street lamps. It is a Los Angeles sight that never loses its magic.

In the distance, I heard a woman having a meltdown with security. “Am I not famous enough?” she yelled.

I never established what the issue was. Perhaps she had been denied access to the carpet, or she simply wanted to take a photograph and was being told no. Either way, she was making a scene in sparkles. I hurried past the publicists who were watching with mild horror. “Never a dull moment,” I said. One or two laughed as I continued inside.

During cocktail hour, I stopped to say hello to Ed Ruscha, whom I had met a few nights earlier at a birthday party. He would be seated at Larry Gagosian’s table that evening. I made my way around the room, passing Julia Garner, who is invariably one of the nicest people in it. She was wearing Loewe with sculptural gold earrings. She always asks me if I’m an actress, which makes me blush slightly, despite the fact that I have never once given her reason to believe that I am. Nearby, Iris Law was wearing a beautiful pearlescent tiled dress by a brand she told me was called Amaramara. Up close, the dress looked almost as though it had been constructed from mother-of-pearl shells. She explained that it was lined in leather, making it surprisingly soft against the skin.

I spotted Jonas Wood in the distance, along with Lauren Halsey, who had recently created the most extraordinary art direction and stage design for Erykah Badu at the Hollywood Bowl. Looking back, it is easy to see the connection: The enormous, mysterious spacecraft in the film has something of the quality of a Turrell light installation.

It was a wonderful line, but his larger point was more affecting. He spoke about the importance of continuing to make art, particularly now, and about wanting to pass that belief on to his children.

Vija Celmins, Denis Villeneuve, Al Pacino, Ed Ruscha: These are people who have spent their lives taking creative risks and changing the way we see things. Thankfully I have grown out of the kind of FOMO that comes with knowing a party is happening somewhere in Los Angeles without you. I headed home to crawl in bed with my cat.

On the drive back, I found myself thinking about Villeneuve’s speech. It is easy to get swept up in the spectacle of an evening like this. The clothes, the jewelry, the famous faces, the endless choreography of who is sitting with whom. But every so often, you’re reminded why these people are interesting in the first place.

After his speech, I will make it a point to see the final installment of Dune next month, and I can’t wait to see what he creates next. For now, I had a purple satin dress to hang up, photographs to look through, and a story to write. I fell asleep thinking about art, ambition, and all the things I might make if I were brave enough. All in due time.
