There are many features to consider when shopping for the best LED strip lights, from color and size to intended use and brightness. Before shoppers purchase an LED light strip, they will want to keep the following factors in mind to ensure their choice meets or exceeds their expectations for the intended application.

It’s essential to determine where the LED light strip will be installed before deciding which product to purchase. Some designs are for specific purposes, such as stair lighting or television backlighting. This specialization differentiates those products from standard LED light strips.

For example, a product intended for backlighting a TV may come with four precut pieces and television-specific syncing options that change color to mimic the colors on the screen. Other location considerations include water resistance for patios, dimmer compatibility for bedrooms, or motion sensing for a hall, bathroom, or stairway.

Light strips are available in a variety of lengths, typically from 1 to 32 feet, though some strips run up to 50 feet long or more. The brightness and intended purpose usually correlate to their length. For example, LED light strips intended for television backlighting or use on stairs tend to be shorter for easier installation.

Low-brightness LED light strips can be up to 32 feet long with about 300 LED lights. High-brightness LED light strips may have the same number of LED modules, but they’ll usually only be about 16 feet long. However, most flexible LED strip lights can be cut using scissors to accommodate small areas. If longer LED tape is needed, check to see if you can connect pieces to create the desired length.

Most light strips feature a flexible circuit board that can bend around corners or run over curved surfaces. Some LED light strips are so flexible that they can be bent up to 90 degrees without damaging the circuit board.

However, as the power of an LED light strip increases, the flexibility decreases, with some light strips being completely rigid in design. These inflexible products tend to be much shorter, sometimes only 1 foot long, and significantly brighter than the average light strip. They can also feature motion-sensing technology, making them ideal for lighting stairs.

As with LED light bulbs, the brightness of an LED light strip is measured in lumens and is dictated by the density of the LED lights within 1 meter.

Ideal brightness can be challenging to figure out, and some manufacturers don’t include this information. By taking note of the LED density, you can determine the average brightness of the light strip. Most light strips come in two densities: standard density (SD) of 30 LEDs per meter (9.1 LEDs/foot) or high density (HD) of 60 LEDs per meter (18.3 LEDs/foot).

SD light strips produce an average of 540 lumens and need 27 watts of power. They can be up to 32.8 feet long. HD light strips provide an average of 1,080 lumens, need 40 watts of power, and come in a maximum length of 16.4 feet.

Some light strips come in only one color, while others can have color selections that range in the millions. The color option chosen is entirely dependent on personal preference.

Color temperature in various types of light bulbs is measured in degrees Kelvin (K) and describes how warm or cool a light’s color appears; lower numbers indicate warmer color temperatures. The soft glow of an average incandescent light bulb is a warm temperature of 2,700K, while natural daylight is much cooler, averaging 6,500K.

We interviewed interior decorator Vivianne Chow, the owner of Viv and Tim Home in Chicago, on this subject. She shared some expert advice. “Make sure that the color temperature of the LED light strips matches what you currently have at home. I recommend sticking to 2700K or 3000K for a warm white look instead of 5000K, which is a little bit too cool-toned.”

Wattage measures how much energy the light strip requires to function at its optimal brightness. If a light strip requires more energy than the power source can provide, it won’t work correctly, causing the light produced to be weak. LED light strips generally operate using batteries or power adapters plugged into wall outlets, but some options can be hardwired directly into the home’s electrical system.

LED light strips with lower brightness requirements, such as a 540-lumen strip, will also have reduced wattage requirements. As brightness demands increase, power demands also rise, with a 16.4-foot SD light strip requiring 27 watts of power and a 16.4-foot HD LED light strip requiring 40 watts.

In our interview with Dara Greaney, he also mentioned that “LEDs are 90 percent more efficient than incandescent and 60 percent more than CFL/fluorescent, so anytime we use LEDs we are saving money and going green.” LED lights are certainly something to consider for energy-conscious consumers.

In years past, LED light strips were hardwired into the electrical system to work with a light switch or plugged into a power outlet and controlled with a button. However, LED light strips have come a long way from their original design.

Light strips are available that connect to Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, or remotes. With these controls, you can adjust brightness and color, program the lights to sync with music, or set schedules for waking up, arriving home from work, or going to sleep.

LED light strips that connect with smart-home devices like Amazon Alexa or Google Home can even be controlled using voice commands, while more straightforward options with just one color may only have a remote to turn the light on or off.

LED light strips come with many pros and a few cons. Whether you’re looking for LED strip lights for bedrooms, LED strip lights for kitchen cabinets, or bedroom LED light strips, the answers to these common questions may help.

LED light strips are safe as long as they’re installed and operated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For example, indoor lights shouldn’t be installed outside, and some lights should not be installed where they can get wet.

LED light strips can run for hours on end without being shut off. They get warm, but they’re designed to underdrive the LEDs, meaning they don’t put out much heat. This makes them nice ambient or task lighting but not ideal as LED grow lights.

Yes, you must plug in most LED light strips. We did test one battery-operated cordless model, but it was a short 16-inch strip. However, this non-electric option would be a reliable light during a power outage.

Almost every LED strip is compatible with a dimmer switch. To make an LED light strip dimmable, you must first connect its input wire to a dimmer unit and then connect the unit to the power supply.

To create ambient light in your home with LED strip lights, the optimal locations are under or above cabinets and counters in the kitchen, behind mirrors in the bathroom, or even around large pieces of furniture like bookshelves and home entertainment units.

LED strip lights can be disposed of by taking them to a local recycling center.

LED lights are built to last, with most LED light strips lasting for up to 50,000 hours of use, which is roughly 10 to 20 years of runtime.

Portable Business Ontologies for AI Agents

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The term “ontology” is now en vogue. Why? Agents, and the LLMs that power them, need relevant business context.

Before you buy into an ontology, ask the vendor: “When the ontology gets our business wrong, how do our people correct it—and what still depends on you?” The answer will tell you a great deal about whose model of the business you are buying into. It also points to the larger stakes: your ability to improve that model as the business changes, and to keep that investment useful if you change platforms.

It helps to distinguish an ontology from a knowledge graph, even though the two often overlap.

Ontology: What kinds of things exist, what relationships mean, and what rules apply. Equipment, components, teams, roles, processes. For example, an ontology can define an “Equipment” type and a “Contains” relationship to a “Component”. A knowledge graph can then assert, “Pump P-101 contains Seal S-7”: two specific entities and their relationship.

Imagine you tell an agent, “Compute same-store sales growth, but show me a side-by-side where we show all stores, then another where we exclude stores open less than 2 years; then break both sides of that up into domestic, international, and combined.”

That ask depends on details an agent cannot guess:

A policy might exclude stores until they have been open a year; the requested two-year group adds a further filter. Even then, do you compare calendar months as soon as a store qualifies, or include it only after a full prior fiscal year?

The ontology needs to connect those stores to the policy that governs the calculation. Finding related facts helps; applying the right rule is what makes the answer useful.

An agent can use relationships in the graph to retrieve relevant context before answering a question. And no wonder they are useful, when knowledge domains can easily get very complex.

But extraction can also encode mistakes. Teams may use different names for the same entity, creating duplicates. Sources can disagree or become outdated. Even a clean source can be misread by an LLM. Making those mistakes look authoritative does not make them less dangerous.

Those risks grow when a model of the business becomes a system that acts on it. A wrong relationship can then trigger a wrong action, not just a wrong answer.

First, be wary of inflexible models. In the name of making something “batteries included,” a vendor may make its own entities first-class citizens while limiting what yours can do. Customization is not enough if custom types lose search, validation, or action capabilities.

Second, look for a path from discovery to governance. A tool should let you bootstrap a graph from what an LLM sees, then validate it against explicit constraints. You should be able to escalate exceptions to people and preserve exploratory knowledge without treating it as approved business data.

The largest risk is tying business processes to a platform you cannot leave. Your ontology represents your business: its knowledge, processes, and relationships. That investment in curation and business logic should stay useful even if you change tools. Why should a vendor get to put that under lock and key?

As knowledge graphs grow very large, it can be worth choosing a tool or vendor that supports graph embeddings. These are close cousins of the semantic embeddings that power many RAG applications. There are two useful benefits to consider.

First, a few hops through a densely connected graph can yield too many entities to process. By learning numerical representations of entities and relationships, embeddings offer another way to find relevant parts of the graph. For the right task, this can be more efficient than walking the graph.

Second, embedding models can rank candidate missing relationships. The same learning can reveal patterns in the attributes and relationships typical of an entity type, helping to build a more complete graph.

To own your own destiny, avoid lock-in, and model your actual business instead of a vendor-dictated caricature of it, how do you begin without an army of forward-deployed engineers? This is where I think modern AI shines, if the tooling supports a path from discovery to verification.

First, use automated discovery and extraction. From business documents, policies, email, or Slack messages, you can construct an initial view of a domain. The critical part is that you cannot trust it as accurate until it is verified.

Verification is the second part. Your tools must give you a smooth path from extracted candidates to claims grounded in sources, and to knowledge validated by a responsible person or authoritative system. Preserve each claim’s source.

Freshness is ongoing work. The process for handling a trouble ticket, or the answer to “how many stores are there in New Jersey”, may not be the same today as it was three years ago. But updating today’s knowledge must not erase yesterday’s reality. When a fact was recorded and when it applied can differ; a useful ontology needs to support both current answers and an accurate historical view. Someone still must keep that knowledge current.

The scope can be massive. Start with a smaller corpus, map a useful domain, and build toward a comprehensive picture while creating value along the way.

Customer support is one useful slice: inbound calls, emails, a ticketing system, automated resolutions, a support team that can respond to customers, and a path to escalate true bugs to engineering. It connects knowledge to action and exposes the same risks and rewards in a manageable domain.

ServiceNow’s Common Service Data Model illustrates the constraint. Its CSDM 5 white paper warns, “Without this data in prescribed tables, you may not receive the full value of the ServiceNow AI Platform.” The warning itself creates a design and operational constraint: lean into the vendor’s model or accept a risk. What if that means cramming your round peg into their square hole?

Now suppose an ontology platform also supplies ingestion, transformations, actions, and business rules. You may be able to export entities, attributes, and relationships. But can you also port transformations and action pipelines that execute changes in your databases, call webhooks, and create other side effects? Tightly coupled integrations can leave the business itself locked in.

Software is eating the world, and AI is eating software. As teams use AI both to build software and to power it, more business logic can become tied to these platforms. Each useful automation can also become another migration dependency. It’s dangerous to let a vendor stand between you and agility.

It’s an amazing time to be alive! Little wonder people are exploring ontologies to provide context to agents. Extending that context into processes and integrations can create real value. Come back to that opening question. Your people’s corrections should make the model a better reflection of your business. That accumulated understanding is too valuable to be stranded when you change platforms. It’s also a terrible time to be painted into a corner.

New Jersey Drops Coinbase Staking Order After Three Years

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

New Jersey’s securities regulator has rescinded its 2023 cease-and-desist order and $5 million penalty against Coinbase’s staking service.

New Jersey’s securities regulator has withdrawn the 2023 cease-and-desist order that barred Coinbase from offering staking to new customers in the state, ending a three-year standoff that cost residents an estimated $15 million in forgone rewards.

The Bureau of Securities notice, dated Oct. 30 and signed by Acting Bureau Chief Keith A. Alt, rescinds the order and the $5 million penalty that came with it. The withdrawal is without prejudice and without a ruling on the merits.

Coinbase disclosed the development in its third-quarter report filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission, which states that the regulator informed the company of its intent to rescind on Oct. 29.

Coinbase CEO Brian Armstrong said New Jersey users would regain access to staking “very soon.”

As of Monday, the exchange’s eligibility page continued to list New Jersey among the states where customers cannot stake new principal, and no reopening date has been announced.

The order traces to June 6, 2023, when the New Jersey bureau joined securities regulators in nine other states — Alabama, California, Illinois, Kentucky, Maryland, South Carolina, Vermont, Washington and Wisconsin — in a coordinated action against Coinbase’s staking program.

The states alleged the service amounted to an unregistered securities offering under state law.

California led the task force; its action covered more than 600,000 accounts. In July 2023, Coinbase agreed with California, New Jersey, South Carolina and Wisconsin to block customers in those states from staking new funds pending final adjudication. Maryland reached a similar agreement in October 2023.

Customers who had staked before the orders kept earning rewards, but could not add principal or compound the rewards they received.

The state cases ran parallel to the SEC’s June 2023 lawsuit against Coinbase, which named staking among its claims. The SEC dismissed that case in February 2025. Alabama, Kentucky, Illinois, South Carolina and Vermont dismissed, vacated or withdrew their own actions in March and April 2025. New Jersey is the sixth of the original ten states to step back.

Rescinding the order ends the restriction on new staking in New Jersey. It does not resolve the underlying legal question. The bureau made no finding on whether Coinbase’s staking program is a security, and the “without prejudice” language preserves its ability to file a new action. Coinbase’s own filing calls the state matters “preliminary” and says an adverse resolution “could have a material impact” on the business.

The filing describes a planned rescission rather than a completed one, which explains the gap between Armstrong’s statement and the unchanged eligibility page. The change for New Jersey residents arrives when Coinbase updates its systems, not when the notice was signed.

Coinbase has framed the state restrictions as a competitive disadvantage. In an April 2025 post, the company’s Ryan VanGrack argued the orders singled out one provider while residents could stake through other platforms. Coinbase put forgone rewards across the four restricted states at more than $130 million as of October 2025.

Four states remain. California, Maryland and Wisconsin continue to bar new staking on Coinbase while their cases proceed; Washington permits staking but is pursuing a $4 million fine. Coinbase estimates California residents have missed more than $89 million in rewards since 2023.

The decision lands as federal policy has shifted but not settled. The SEC has withdrawn from the registration-based cases it brought in 2023, and the CLARITY market structure bill passed the House in 2025 but stalled in the Senate. State securities regulators are not bound by the SEC’s enforcement priorities, which is why the staking cases outlived the federal one by more than a year and a half.

A future SEC, or a future bureau chief in Trenton, could revisit the question.

Guided Learning first arrived in Gemini on August 6, 2025, using probing and open-ended questions, step-by-step explanations and multimodal responses such as diagrams, videos and interactive quizzes.

In Classroom, a teacher assigns the activity and the AI acts as a one-to-one study tool anchored in class material. Google’s education report says the activity can be grounded in teacher-uploaded materials. Focus Mode keeps students on task and redirects the conversation toward teacher-specified learning objectives if they try to move off topic. Teachers receive real-time class-level and individual insights, including full chat transcripts, to identify where students need additional support. Google tested the Classroom feature with schools and educators before launch and incorporated their feedback.

It’s designed to allow you to mix and match as you see fit, expanding your creative options beyond the standard.

The Bambu Lab H3C starts at $2,500 and comes with the nine-filament system and Automated Material System. I’ve spent the last few weeks using it, and while I haven’t finished my review yet, I already have quite a few thoughts.

This is not a small machine by any measure. I originally had it on my workbench, but the H3C sits so tall that opening the lid and loading the filament was arduous, even for a 6-foot-1 person like myself. Bambu Lab also sent me a metal rolling cart, sold separately, that lowers the machine to a usable height. That said, it still stands 63 inches tall on the cart, so yeah, it’s a big’un.

Despite its size and complex design, setting it up was simple. I would recommend having a second person around to do it, as it’s cumbersome — but not difficult to plug in all the parts. The AMS 3 that sits atop the machine is different from most of the best 3D printers because the H3C requires it operate. It’s not a separate add-on but an integral part of the machine.

You can tell from every metric that the H3C is designed to be a Bambu Lab flagship. It has all the top-of-the-line hardware, including a heatbed that can reach 120C and an optional but very cool bird’s-eye camera to monitor prints from above. It’s fast, too, with a top toolhead speed of 1,400 mm/s. Of course, that isn’t the print speed, but it does show you the type of speed it could reach if you pushed it to the max.

Printing at high speed does have some disadvantages, though. I have had several print failures on tall prints where the supports or purge tower have fallen due to accidental touches or vibration. The H3C moves fast, prints fast and fails fast if you aren’t careful. Most of those failures occurred at 125% speed, but that’s the standard setting I use for large prints on other machines, and they handle it just fine.

Bambu calls its nine-nozzle system, which heats using conductive heating, the Swaptek system. This isn’t to be confused with another nine-nozzle system you may have heard of: INDX, which uses conductive heating. INDX and Swaptek are extremely similar in execution but differ in a few areas. INDX is purely mechanical, whereas Bambu says its Swaptek system supplies data and power to each tool, allowing the nozzles to start heating faster than those in a standard INDX system — in around 6 seconds per nozzle.

In practice, there isn’t a huge difference between what I’m seeing on the H3C and the Prusa Core One + INDX in terms of speed. The Core One has the advantage that it’s calibrated to avoid a purge tower, and it doesn’t purge as much waste as the Swaptek system. The H3C has its own neat tricks, though: Not only can it print with different nozzle sizes and materials, but it can also print with materials of different diameters.

One of the biggest roadblocks with printing TPU — the very soft, rubbery material that 3D-printed shoes are made from — through an AMS is that it’s too soft to push through the AMS feeder. To combat this, you have to use a harder TPU that isn’t nearly as flexible as you would need for shoes or things that bounce. Adding toolheads capable of printing 2.85mm-diameter materials lets Bambu create TPU that’s more flexible after melting but thicker and easier to feed in the AMS. It works really well.

And it doesn’t stop at TPU, either. You can use 2.85mm PLA with a 0.8mm nozzle to print the infill of a model, while using a 0.2mm nozzle and 1.7mm PLA to print the detailed outer walls. It gives you so many options. The AMS even has two material slots with active heating chambers, so you can print materials that need to be warm and dry, along with other, more shelf-stable materials.

Given the $2,500 price and the H3C’s flagship status, it would be shocking if the print quality were bad. All of the models that didn’t fail due to purge tower collapse look great, and the nine separate nozzles make light work of just about any color project. I love watching this machine print as it swaps across the nozzles at blistering speeds.

Some of them did fail, though, and the H3C was good at catching the errors, pausing the print and letting me know. I chose to keep one of them going despite the failed tower, and it turned out fine, though a little damaged. I had some fun 3D printing my scanned head at life size, using silver for my greying beard — and I love the way it came out.

The CNET test print wasn’t nearly as good as the one the H2C produced, which pretty much maxed out every test, but it wasn’t the worst either. Accuracy was good, and despite the printer’s speed, ringing was kept to a minimum. It struggled on the 0.2mm tolerance test, but it did well on bridging and overhangs, showing that its temperature control is excellent. I still have more of these to do before I’m ready to give a complete score, but so far, I’m happy with the result.

In video games, there’s a concept called endgame content: the harder challenges you tackle after completing most of the story. It’s not for the faint of heart. The H3C is endgame content. It isn’t hard to use, but it’s designed for someone who has gone as far as possible with machines such as the P2S and A1 combos of the world. If you’re looking to go beyond what you thought you could do with a 3D printer, the H3C is the machine to get you there.

Swaptek, INDX and multi-toolhead printers are here to stay. And, like all 3D printers, they can come in many sizes and budgets. The Core One INDX is smaller but cheaper, but the H3C has a lot of options right out of the box to make some incredibly complex models. We live in interesting times and the landscape can only get better.

Infrared rays are uniquely capable of cutting through empty space and bringing warmth to the objects they strike; in fact, that’s how the sun heats the earth. Today’s infrared heaters bring warmth to objects, like clothes and skin rather than the surrounding air—an efficient mechanism in stark contrast to convection heaters, which warm the surrounding air before heating objects in that space.

Although heating the air may distribute heat more uniformly throughout a room, much energy is wasted heating unoccupied space, such as high ceilings or the outdoor air. Infrared heaters use 100 percent of the heat they produce to bring focused warmth to the objects in the path of its rays, making them much more energy efficient. A drug-free diabetes treatment? An implant lowers blood sugar in mice

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The device banishes extra blood glucose by converting it to a compound excreted in urine

Excess blood glucose (shown in white in this illustration) is the target of a new experimental device. In mice, the sugar gets converted into a compound which the kidneys can excrete in urine.

NEMES LASZLO/SCIENCE PHOTO LIBRARY/Getty Images

A wireless implant that clears excess sugar from the blood could someday offer a drug-free way to treat type 2 diabetes and obesity.

In mice with both conditions, the under-the-skin device brought high blood sugar back to normal within 60 to 90 minutes of being switched on, researchers report October 22 in Science. After six weeks, the treated mice lost up to 10 percent of their body weight and had lower triglycerides, a type of fat in the blood.

We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday.

Diabetes affects about 830 million people worldwide. Conventional type 2 diabetes treatments like insulin and GLP-1 drugs require careful dosing. Even experimental diabetes-controlling implants still rely on the hormone insulin, which clears sugar from the bloodstream but doesn’t rid the body of it, says Martin Fussenegger, a bioengineer at ETH Zurich. The new implant instead removes glucose from the body without added drugs, genes or living cells.

The 3-centimeter-long device contains a catalyst made of gold flakes and platinum particles on spongelike graphite. When electricity powers it, the catalyst converts glucose into gluconate, a compound that is easier for the kidneys to flush out in urine. With less glucose available for energy, the body may turn to its fat stores instead, Fussenegger and colleagues suspect. In mice, that shift showed up as higher blood levels of chemicals released when fat breaks down, such as ketones. “This system kind of targets the root cause, which is the energy surplus by glucose in the body,” Fussenegger says.

The implant works like an automatic sugar-removal system: A wearable glucose monitor detects high blood sugar levels and sends readings through a smartphone that switches the implant on. When levels return to a safer range, it switches off. The automated system ran for two weeks in eight mice. The catalyst itself remained able to convert glucose after up to three months under their skin, though the study didn’t establish how long the entire device would last.

And it’s still unknown how well the device would work in people. “This is a potentially promising method,” says David Klonoff, an endocrinologist at Sutter Mills-Peninsula Medical Center in Burlingame, Calif., who was not involved in the study. “But the risks are significant and must be carefully ruled out before it can be safely tested on humans.”

Researchers need to determine how much gluconate the kidneys can clear, Klonoff says. If it builds up, it could disrupt the body’s acid-base balance, causing symptoms such as nausea or confusion; excreting it could also deplete electrolytes such as sodium and potassium, he notes.

Scaling up the device is another challenge. Humans consume about 250 grams of carbohydrates daily, compared with two grams for mice, Klonoff estimates. Meeting that demand could require a much larger implant or multiple devices, he says. In a lab test, a larger device cleared nearly all glucose from about 5 liters of solution, roughly an adult’s blood volume, though not a full day’s glucose intake.

Fussenegger’s team plans to test the device in pigs and sheep before considering human trials. If proven safe and effective, it could offer an alternative to existing diabetes treatments and a new weight-loss option for people regardless of whether they have diabetes, he says.
