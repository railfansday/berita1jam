Why Does The 21,000-Pound Force On A Boeing 737 Door Keep Blended-Wing Jets Out Of Airline Service?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

alexanderon | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

Since the dawn of modern commercial aviation, there have been many different planemakers and jetliners that have taken to the sky, nevertheless, there has been one constant theme that can be found in every single airframe to date. That is the conventional tube-and-wing design, which is optimized to hold cabin pressure, scale aircraft size, simplify airport operations, and simplify evacuating passengers in an emergency.

Meanwhile, the revolutionary blended wing body concept has been championed for years as a next-generation jetliner template for its 20-30% fuel efficiency, yet it inherently conflicts with all four of these design priorities. One of the main design hurdles for a BWB is simply holding in the air while cruising at altitude.

Pressurization systems maintain roughly 8.35 psi (0.58 bar) inside and exert about 21,000 lbs (93 kN) of force against a standard jet entry door. As noted in Engineers Ireland's overview of blended-wing airliners, flattening that cabin cuts drag and could burn up, but a BWB's flat walls experience much more intense loads under that same pressure.

Pressurized air tries to bend flat floors and ceilings outward into a round bubble. Reinforcing the frame against that bending adds heavy internal beams and thick carbon walls, eating away fuel savings while complicating emergency exits and preventing easy fuselage stretches.

For the ZEROe hydrogen program, Airbus first displayed a 200-seat BWB in the hopes that the broad body would accommodate large fuel tanks that were chilled to -423°F (-253°C). However, in 2025, Airbus abandoned that concept for more research and went back to a traditional tube-and-wing configuration with hydrogen fuel-cell pods.

Airbus executives realized there was too much financial risk involved in certifying a pressurized passenger cabin while negotiating airport gate constraints and non-stretchable tooling, a risk that commercial airlines declined to finance. Rather, blended-wing concepts are being introduced to military forces first by aerospace developers.

Through the Defense Innovation Unit, the US Air Force awarded startup JetZero $235 million in August 2023 to construct a full-scale demonstrator using Northrop Grumman's Scaled Composites for flight testing in 2027. Designed for next-generation aerial refueling and military cargo missions, the JetZero demonstrator mounts two commercial high-bypass engines above the rear wing body.

While high-angle takeoffs can push turbulent airflow into top-mounted engine fans, this layout cuts ground noise and protects engines from runway debris. More significantly, gasoline tanks and freight pallets do not require windows, never experience airsickness, and completely circumvent passenger evacuation regulations. Before going back to passenger aircraft, developers intend to reduce the financial and engineering risks.

They will do so by first demonstrating composite pressure structures and flight-control software in military cargo and tanker operations. Commercial passengers will continue to board well-known round jets until military flights demonstrate that internal bracing can safely retain cabin air without significant weight penalties or emergency evacuation bottlenecks, leaving the BWB to earn its wings in uniform.

Over 80% of travelers inside a 250-seat BWB will sit in windowless center bays, forcing airlines to install heavy, maintenance-prone digital wall screens instead of simple windows. Additionally, passengers at the outboard edge have a rougher ride during turns because the outer seats sit 40-50 feet (12-15 meters) away from the centerline.

During a standard 30-degree banked turn or a bumpy crosswind landing, outer-row flyers do not just tilt gently. They will feel a swing through wide arcs that may trigger airsickness much more frequently. Planemakers manufacturing a BWB will have to divide the cabin with permanent structural support walls in order to contain the immense outward air pressure inside a wide BWB.

To prevent flat panels from protruding outward, these vertical composite ties secure the floor directly to the ceiling. Although this interior grid prevents blowout without adding undue weight, it divides the cabin into cubicle-like bays that make it difficult for airlines to later modify legroom or move seats. On the land side, guiding 250 or more travelers through a standard jet bridge into a wide, multi-aisle grid creates bottlenecks at the entrance.

Standard airport jet bridges only connect to one or two forward left-hand doors. The unique shape and depth of the belly hold will also fundamentally alter the way that baggage and cargo handling is done. Ultimately, even when the first BWB does take flight, there remain many unresolved dilemmas to be overcome before the first arrives on the flight line at an airport near you.

Mainline passenger jets like the Boeing 737 MAX, Airbus A321neo, and Boeing 777-300ER are iconic examples of a typical tubular-and-wing design with minimal dead weight. The optimized, thin skin of each plane's fuselage stretches uniformly like a balloon as internal pressure pushes outward equally in all directions.

This design premise is upset when that barrel is flattened and morphed into the cabin of a BWB. A cylindrical hull allows even composite aircraft, like a Boeing 787 Dreamliner, to withstand decades of pressurized flight without the need for a more complex airframe. Engineering studies show that internal bending can multiply structural stress more than tenfold compared to a simple round tube.

Panels bow from air pushing on the concentrated points of floors and ceilings. Stopping the cabin from bursting at the seams forces makers to add bracing and load-bearing bulkheads. Building a BWB cabin strong enough to hold normal cruise pressure therefore requires exceptionally thick carbon skin and heavy framing.

The weight of additional support then directly reduces the 20-30% fuel burn reduction claimed by BWBs, complicating manufacturing and operating trade-offs. Another issue with blended-wing aircraft is that they are nearly impossible to scale up to larger models, even if engineers can overcome the significant cabin weight issue.

On July 20, 2007, the subscale Boeing-NASA X-48B demonstrated that flight computers could control a tailless flying wing above Edwards Air Force Base. Through 2010, the 500 lbs (227-kg) test plane had 92 successful flights, thanks to three small jet engines producing 52 lbs of thrust (0.23 kN each).

Despite NASA's X-48B passing the flight tests, building a full-size airplane provides serious day-to-day flying challenges. Standard jets mount their tail fins roughly 100 feet (30 m) behind the aircraft's balance point, giving tail flaps high leverage to lift the nose during takeoff. Without a long tail, a BWB relies on large rear wing flaps to pitch the nose upward.

Deflecting those flaps sharply during takeoff actually spills lift right when pilots need it most on the runway. Despite these hurdles, developers are actively testing practical engineering solutions. According to CNN's report on the JetZero program, the US Air Force-backed startup uses lightweight composite panels and internal ribs to tame cabin pressure without excessive weight.

Meanwhile, Nautilus avoids pressure penalties on its unpressurized KONA cargo plane while giving its Horizon Evo passenger jet a narrower, double-deck center body that limits bending stress and fits standard 737 airport gates. Still, an even bigger problem for airlines and aircraft makers is fleet growth.

Manufacturers build profitable aircraft families, like stretching the Airbus A320 into the A321neo or growing the Boeing 737 into the MAX 10, by simply inserting round barrel sections ahead of and behind the wings. By using the same basic wing design, pilot training, and replacement components, airlines are able to carry more passengers.

Meanwhile, they maintain fair purchase and operating costs thanks to this simple production technique. That straightforward stretch option is eliminated by a BWB, which integrates the cabin directly into the wing's curved lifting shape.

The smooth airflow of the wing is ruined, and the plane's balancing point is shifted when the middle body is cut apart to accommodate additional rows. In order to create a larger version, producers would have to completely redo the flight-control software, redesign the exterior shell, and rework the internal wing beams, which would cost billions of dollars for each size of seat.

Several companies, including Airbus, JetZero, and Boeing, are actively developing prototypes.

One of the greatest functional hurdles for blended-wing designs is that every commercial airport on Earth is built for a traditional tube-and-wing jet. A 250-seat blended-wing plane would span nearly 200 feet (61 meters), which is on par with the Boeing 777-9 and Airbus A350-1000, bringing higher airport fees and slower turnarounds.

Conventional large airliners, like the 747 and A380, were already met with major resistance as their wingspans forced airports to widen taxiways and rebuild gates. As detailed in Leeham News' analysis of blended-wing airliners, wide-wingspan planes are vital for lowering drag and improving fuel burn, yet the A380 directly suffered in sales due to its limited destination portfolio.

That restriction on airport access keeps airlines cautious, as it is a hard bottleneck on network versatility and therefore business utility. To date, no carrier holds a firm, deposit-backed order for a BWB. Given that commercial carriers will not fund the certification risks upfront, developers have turned to military applications to make the BWB a reality.

When JetZero's full-scale demonstration flies for the US military in 2027, aviation leaders will be present at Edwards Air Force Base. These test flights will assess computer flight controls, aerial refueling management, and composite fabrication. Military missions may demonstrate that internal composite bracing can hold cabin air without adding excessive structural weight.

If so, commercial designers researching new passenger airliners for the late 2030s may eventually profit from that flight data. Until planemakers demonstrate that a BWB can withstand rigorous 90-second emergency evacuation testing without incurring substantial structural penalties, customers will continue to board conventional jets.

At 37,000 feet (11,278 m), the physics of maintaining breathable air remains relentlessly demanding. The basic tube will continue to dominate aviation economics because a simple metal or composite cylinder can withstand 21,000 lbs (93 kN) of outward door force with little material weight.

47 Years Later, ‘The Land Before Time’ Creator Reveals Why He Left Disney

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nowadays, Disney has no shortage of rivals in cinematic animation. Sony, DreamWorks, Illumination, and more have made their mark on the medium with blockbuster stories and techniques that have pushed the boundaries of what's possible. Before any major competition stepped up to give the Mouse House a run for its money, though, there was Don Bluth. The former Disney animator famously left the company behind in 1979 to create his own studio and challenge what animation could be, free from a more bureaucratic system that had lost its hand-drawn touch. His exodus and ambition not only led to classics like The Land Before Time and An American Tail, but also spurred Disney to revitalize its own machine upon realizing its place in entertainment could be contested.

Today, a new documentary is exploring the journey of the man who fought for the soul of animation and ultimately sacrificed his dream to preserve its future. Don Bluth: Somewhere Out There follows Bluth from the moment he walked out of Disney to the animated achievements that defined him as a distinct mind in the medium, and the challenges that threatened his ambitions as he built a transformative legacy. With a mix of newer and archival interviews, it reflects on the unique path carved by its subject as he took video games to new places with Dragon's Lair, helped build up Ireland's animation industry, and attracted the eye of visionaries like Steven Spielberg and George Lucas. To commemorate its release on digital platforms, Collider can exclusively share a new teaser featuring the man himself as he outlines his hopes for Don Bluth Animation.

The footage looks back on an interview with Bluth shortly after his departure from Disney with a handful of other animators, sparking "animation's greatest rivalry." The question on everyone's mind at the time was why leave behind such a beloved studio, though the answer is simple to him — "Our interest is to be able to produce animated films that are a high enough quality to where we can keep the state of our art alive." As he learned from his time at the Mouse House when Walt Disney was alive, classic animation is all about bringing real life to the characters drawn so that audiences can identify with them. In essence, he was both trying to compete with Disney and recapture the heart of the company's early films, though it was all out of love of the medium and a realization of its potential.

Dave LaMattina and Chad Walker wrote and directed Somewhere Out There, having worked together as an independent filmmaking duo for well over a decade now, stemming back to 2010's Brownstones to Red Dirt. Their first proper documentary effort came in 2014 with the acclaimed I Am Big Bird, centered on legendary Sesame Street performer Caroll Spinney. Most recently, they spearheaded Summer of '94 about the grueling rise of the U.S. Men's National Team to a true competitor at the 1994 World Cup as recorded by the ragtag team themselves. In their director's statement, the duo described how working with Bluth gave them a greater understanding of what went into his projects through his own notes, artwork, and other remnants of his creative process.

"In our debut documentary, I Am Big Bird, we discovered our deep passion for working with archival material. We love how the ability to peer behind the curtain with the benefit of hindsight allows us to bring fresh meaning to decades-old footage, uncovering raw emotion and new insight. Bringing Don’s story to life afforded us that exact opportunity, as his entire archive now lives at the Savannah College of Art and Design (SCAD). Working closely with the team at SCAD, we unearthed never-before-seen footage from some of our favorite childhood works, including An American Tail, The Secret of NIMH, The Land Before Time, and the groundbreaking arcade game Dragon's Lair.

Getting up close and personal with original hand-drawn animation, Don’s handwritten notes, outtakes from live-action reference shoots, raw footage from promotional videos, and gorgeous art from unrealized development projects gave us profound insight into his constant struggle between art and commerce—a journey we deeply identify with as independent filmmakers ourselves."

Collider's Ross Bonaime spoke highly of the documentary in his review after seeing it at the 2025 SCAD Savannah Film Festival, calling it "a compelling look at one of animation’s most daring voices, a man who fought against a Goliath and made the world of animation better for it." LaMattina and Walker had a strong team to honor a legend, including having Fergal Lawler of The Cranberries handle the music. Lavalle Lee, Mary LaMattina, and John LaMattina served as executive producers.

Don Bluth: Somewhere Out There debuts digitally on Prime Video on November 20 before opening on other major VOD platforms on December 4.

This Everyday Vitamin May Ease Depression In Some People

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Vitamin D supplements may ease symptoms in some people with depression, according to a review of the evidence.

Several small trials found improvements, including in people who started out with low vitamin D levels.

Other trials found little or no benefit, and one large study found that supplements did not lower the chances of developing depression.

The link is plausible because vitamin D may influence inflammation and chemical signalling in the brain, both of which are relevant to mood.

Depression itself could also lower vitamin D levels if it leaves people with less energy to go outdoors.

That makes it important to ask two separate questions: whether correcting a deficiency can help someone who is already depressed and whether taking vitamin D can prevent depression in the first place.

Researchers at the University of Oradea reviewed studies measuring vitamin D levels in people with and without depression, as well as trials testing whether supplements changed their symptoms.

One small trial recruited people who had both major depression and a vitamin D deficiency.

Both groups continued with their usual care, but after about three months, depression scores were lower in the group given a vitamin D injection than in the placebo group (Vellekkatt et al., 2020).

Differences in who took part, the doses they received and how symptoms were measured made the trials difficult to compare.

A trial designed to test prevention followed more than 18,000 adults aged 50 and over who had no clinically relevant depressive symptoms when they joined.

Over about five years, participants who took daily vitamin D were about as likely to develop depression or clinically relevant symptoms as those who took a placebo (Okereke et al., 2020).

Vitamin D receptors are found in parts of the brain such as the hippocampus, which is involved in memory and the response to stress.

The vitamin can also influence immune signals involved in inflammation.

This is one route through which it might affect mood in some people with depression.

Vitamin D levels also reflect habits that can change during depression, including what people eat and how much time they spend outdoors.

Someone who stops going for regular walks, for example, may get less sunlight and make less vitamin D as a result.

A blood test taken during an episode of depression cannot tell which change came first.

The question now is whether correcting a vitamin D deficiency can improve symptoms in people already receiving treatment for depression.

Answering it will take larger trials that recruit people with both conditions and follow them long enough to see whether any improvement lasts.

The study was published in the journal Pharmaceuticals (Roșian et al., 2025).

The Tutu Is the Tactile Miracle Fashion Needs Right Now

The tutu’s fashion legacy just keeps going.

Distinctly fluffy, feminine, and over-the-top, the tutu has an unrivaled fashion legacy. Is it any wonder that in the past few weeks, celebrities have been joyfully committed to wearing the frilly staple? It may be peak Nutcracker season, but even so, Balletcore will never die. The aesthetic has experienced its original iteration (dating back to the 1800s), its first fashion wave in the ’50s and again in the ’80s, followed by a darker take, a more grounded look, and yet it still persists. At the center of it is the tutu: Nicole Kidman recently wore a Matthieu Blazy Chanel dress with a perfectly layered tutu tulle confection of a skirt that would give Carrie Bradshaw a run for her money. Elsewhere, Rosalía, Tinashe, and Olivia Rodrigo have all recently donned the tutu. The look right now reads as more punky Y2K or ’80s couture.

The spring/summer 2027 runways were littered with echoes of the tutu, too. Both Chanel and Dior showcased voluminous skirts with tutu-like shapes; Dior’s were made of cut fabric petals. Rising label Agro Studio integrated ethereal watercolor tutus into its recent collection. And it’s impossible to forget Cecilie Bahnsen’s undying dedication to the garment. Her fall/winter 2026 collection was a tribute to movement and dance and repositioned the tutu as an everyday item versus occasionwear, pairing it with fleeces and down jackets as live dancers wove throughout the venue wearing tutus. “They brought a beautiful femininity and softness to the more utilitarian pieces, creating an interesting contrast,” Bahnsen tells ELLE. “And as the dancers moved through the show, the tutus came to life, adding a wonderful sense of volume, lightness, and movement to the silhouettes.”

In 2026, wearing a tutu is an undeniable endorphin booster. Perhaps it’s deeply and satisfyingly impractical, the exact opposite of wearing a pair of denim jeans and a T-shirt or button-down, the kinds of items that typically dominate capsule wardrobes. “I love the femininity, the sculptural quality, and the beautiful bounce and movement of a tutu,” adds Bahnsen. “There is something so joyful, light, and full of positive energy about it.”

Given the tutu’s broad history, it’s no wonder vintage tutus are experiencing a bit of a revival too. According to Haile Lidow, founder of Lidow Archive, there’s been a surge in demand. “The tutus I can’t keep in stock right now are lighter and more ballerina-focused,” she tells ELLE. “I have this David Dalrymple for Patricia Field white tutu skirt that was made to be like the famous Carrie Bradshaw title sequence look, and that piece has been renting out constantly. I’ve also been obsessed with tattered tulle lately and the line it tows between innocence and darkness.”

Like Bahnsen, she sees an inherent joy in the silhouette and also links the current wide appeal to the extra textural feeling. “There’s something very tactile about tutus, and in an age of digital-everything, I think we’re craving pieces that seem to reach out and ask us to interact with them IRL,” she adds. In a season of pared-back dressing, the tutu’s frothy excess feels like a joyful rebellion.

Last month, the U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) released a chilling and info-packed report judging the scope and breadth of threats posed by the nation's adversaries in space.

The new report focuses on China, Russia and the emerging space powers of Iran and North Korea. It looks at what's happening in Earth orbit and beyond, including cislunar space, the region between our planet and the moon.

The report — the 2026 edition of "Challenges to Security in Space" — is the third iteration of the DIA's unclassified intelligence assessment on space-related challenges and threats to U.S. national security, as well as the extent to which space is treated internationally as a warfighting domain.

Information within the report, which you can read here, is current as of July 2026. DIA's mission is to provide intelligence on foreign militaries to help prevent wars, and win the ones that are fought.

The report's up-front finding is that China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea are developing, testing, deploying and using space capabilities "in a way that threatens the security of all space systems and their associated space benefits."

Indeed, China and Russia are developing "a formidable spectrum" of offensive capabilities to degrade or destroy space services of other countries, from kinetic destruction to sophisticated jamming and cyber operations.

The DIA report also notes that "the production of cheaper, smaller, and simpler satellites, which are faster to develop and deploy, along with the expansion of commercially available launch services, are driving the rapid integration of commercial space alternatives to support national security."

While space commercialization is making previously government-centered space services widely available to interested parties, the DIA report adds, that availability also includes state and non-state actors with malign interests.

A major item dealt with by the DIA is "counterspace" — and it's not the kind that you'll find in your kitchen.

Counterspace operations can be either offensive or defensive. They can be used on purpose to reversibly or irreversibly deny, disrupt, degrade, damage or destroy a space object or component of a space system.

The DIA report underscores that Russia is developing a satellite designed to carry a nuclear weapon.

Russia is doing so despite its obligation to comply with the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. That U.N. agreement prohibits the placement of any objects carrying nuclear weapons, or any other kinds of weapons of mass destruction, into orbit around Earth.

"A space-based capability such as this could cause rapid and sudden loss or degradation of thousands of satellites operated by countries and companies around the world," the DIA report states, "as well as to the vital communications, navigation, scientific, meteorological, commercial and national security services they provide."

A nuclear detonation in space could be tailored to impact satellites in most orbits, the DIA report warns, from low Earth orbit for commercial and reconnaissance satellites, geosynchronous orbit for critical communications, and medium Earth orbit for navigation and timing satellites.

The newly issued DIA report also observes that exploring cislunar space is of increasing interest to spacefaring nations for potential scientific and economic opportunities. However, nations such as China "may be exploring military applications from cislunar space," the report notes.

China has stated its goal of developing a cislunar space economy by 2045.

"China is also the first country to attempt sustained operations on the far side of the moon, with numerous projects slated to support that effort through the next decade. China portrays its interest in cislunar space as peaceful, but PRC [People's Republic of China] officials have also discussed pursuing military capabilities in cislunar space," the report states.

Placing a keen eye on the DIA report is Peter Garretson, a senior fellow in defense studies at the American Foreign Policy Council and a strategy consultant who focuses on space and defense.

According to Garretson, the most significant positive change from earlier DIA appraisals is the explicit assessment that Russia is developing a satellite designed to carry a nuclear weapon. He told Space.com that the DIA's expanded treatment of the threat of a nuclear weapon on orbit reflects concerns in the Congress and the Trump White House.

However, Garretson also flagged what he said is a consequential omission in the report: the disappearance of the prior treatment (in the 2022 version of the report) of research competition, surveillance beyond geosynchronous Earth orbit and the potential military use of deep space for staging reserve forces.

In Garretson's view, the DIA needs to help put the puzzle pieces together for Congress and the attentive public about what exactly China is doing, and how it fits together in the nation's strategy to achieve comprehensive space dominance.

According to Garretson, "everything they are doing, from launch cadence, reusable launch, commercial space investment, investments in power, propulsion, repair, manufacturing, to commercial assets working together" supports both an economic expansion toward new resources and what China hopes will be overwhelming military power and strategic depth.

Garretson senses the moves by China to develop extractive and in-space industries "is truly the strategic threat that could crush American space power. Now is the time to be making the investments to ensure we have a 'freedom's forge' and 'arsenal of democracy' in space that can protect the open space commerce that is the nation's strategy."

As for the DIA's outlook for the future: The agency concluded that space almost certainly will remain an increasingly competitive and crowded environment as space capabilities improve and other countries seize opportunities in the final frontier.

"The increase of Chinese, Russian, Iranian and North Korean counterspace capabilities, rapid growth in the number of space objects, and the proliferation of requirements for space-enabled services," the DIA report states, "will all complicate operations in space in the coming decade."

Now there's something new. You can also build interactive websites from your notebooks.

That might not immediately seem like the most obvious fit, turning your research notes into a mass of HTML code. But it gives you another way to look at your material and to get insights from it. Website building is rolling out now for Gemini Notebook's web app for all users, and is "coming soon" to the mobile app.  You won't see a website option in the Studio panel alongside Audio Overview, Reports, Quiz, and the rest; instead, you type something along the lines of "make me a website" in the prompt box.

The finished website pops up in the Studio panel, and in my case it took around five minutes to put something together from 26 different sources. Click on the website, and it opens in a new window—you can use it from there, open it in a separate tab, or download the code to your computer.

I was honestly impressed by how much Gemini Notebook managed to pack into a single website: I got plot summaries, analysis, critical context, and some of the main themes and motifs outlined. There was an interactive quiz at the bottom of the page to test my knowledge, and even a graphic I could click through to reveal different elements of how the stories fit together.

Everything was well laid out and functional, albeit with the standard "sheen" you get from anything made by AI (which tends to always pick the same colors and fonts). For something like my project, a website really works well as a study aid, since it offers a different way of interrogating the information. You can already do that in the main chat, and with tools such as the audio and video overviews, but this is different. It's a bit like having a mini-Wikipedia focused just on whatever you're researching.

The interactive elements that are included—so in my example, I could click from story to story, and theme to theme, within the same page—make a site more engaging than a standard report document and a static wall of text, and should help the information stick if you're trying to study it.
