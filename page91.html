Seattle tech vet and Sounders FC minority owner Chee Chew joins front office to lead AI push

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Kurt Schlosser on Nov 17, 2026 at 11:02 amNovember 17, 2026 at 11:02 am

Chee Chew helped build the Windows 95 Start menu, started Google Hangouts and ran consumer engagement at Amazon. Now the Seattle tech veteran is taking on a new job with the Sounders and Reign FC, where he’ll lead how the soccer clubs use technology, data and AI.

Chew, a minority owner of Sounders FC since 2019, is joining the organization’s executive team as chief technology and transformation officer, reporting to majority owner Adrian Hanauer, the club announced Tuesday.

“His knowledge of and passion for the Sounders and Reign allow him to hit the ground running,” Hanauer said in a statement. “His experience leading teams through major shifts in technology gives us a unique opportunity to strengthen both the soccer and business sides of our organization.”

Chew most recently was chief product and technology officer at Zapier, where he helped steer the workflow automation company toward AI. Before that he was chief product officer at mParticle and at Twilio, where he oversaw the communications company’s $3.2 billion acquisition of Segment.

He spent four years at Amazon, nearly eight years as a vice president of engineering at Google, where he also led the company’s Seattle and Kirkland offices, and 14 years at Microsoft working on Internet Explorer, Windows Mobile and Xbox Live.

Chew said his direct team will stay small, but its work will reach every corner of the business, including HR, ticket sales, customer service, match-day planning, corporate partnerships, legal, and player recruiting and scouting. He also wants every department to have its own “builders,” staffers who can create AI tools and automations for their own teams.

“In order to do a change like this, it’s more than technology,” Chew told GeekWire. “Technology is in many ways the easiest part. Driving change, getting deep, building side by side with folks, I think is what it takes.”

Chew didn’t grow up playing soccer. “My family was a very traditional Chinese family of study and play piano,” he said. “It was a stereotype, but it was true for my family.” He caught the bug in college, where he played a ton of intramurals, and later from his wife, Christine, who played through college, and their two kids.

The couple joined the Sounders ownership group in 2019 as one of 11 Seattle-rooted families, many with deep ties to the region’s tech industry. Former Microsoft executive Terry Myerson pulled the group together, and it included Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella, CFO Amy Hood, and others.

A group of owners has met every other Friday since 2019, and “leveraging technology has always been part of the strategy” for the club, Chew said.

The job grew out of a demo Chew built for Hanauer about two and a half years ago: an AI agent that could handle complicated fan requests, such as a season ticket holder with four seats who wants to buy a block of eight. The club later brought in consultants to train staff on AI, but the effort never quite gained momentum, he said.

Talks about a bigger role started early this year and came together by midyear. “Adrian and I talked about it, and we agreed for me to come on board and take an operational role and hack on some code,” Chew said.

“I think I’ve created more code in the past six months than I did in the past 20 years. It’s pretty amazing. I’m having a ton of fun.”

Chew describes Hanauer as a visionary on “the art of the possible” who comes in daily asking, “Could you do this?” Recently, as Chew was boarding a flight home to Seattle, Hanauer called from a meeting where staff doubted they could scale up one effort tenfold without hiring more people. Could AI help?

On the flight, while using the plane’s Wi-Fi, he built a prototype with Anthropic’s Claude. “By the time I landed, I sent it to them and said, ‘OK, give this a try,’” he said. The club used it in an experiment to scale up performance marketing before a match.

It’s the kind of hands-on building Chew says he’s been doing a lot of lately.

“I think I’ve created more code in the past six months than I did in the past 20 years,” he said. “It’s pretty amazing. I’m having a ton of fun.”

He recently ran an AI analysis of one player’s game, based on stats such as touches, expected goals and distance run. It pointed out strengths and areas to improve. The player was “very hungry for it,” Chew said, and asked if they could do it every game.

The club’s performance analytics group, led by data analyst Tyler Cox, was already ahead of much of the organization, and Chew said he’s helping speed up that work with AI.

The bigger challenge is getting people to change how they work, Chew said. At Zapier, he saw many companies struggle to get results from AI, with some spending “massive token budgets” without a big win. He said the answer is blending AI with traditional automation and fitting it into how people already work.

“It has to be approachable for folks,” he said. “You can’t go in and just throw everything up and start from scratch.”

The Sounders host Real Salt Lake in the wild-card round of the MLS Cup Playoffs on Thursday at Lumen Field. Asked whether AI could help figure out a win, Chew passed.

“I don’t want to get involved and interfere with our coaching staff,” he said.

Sean Connery Wasn’t the First James Bond: Meet Barry Nelson in the ‘Casino Royale’ You’ve Never Seen

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ever since George Lazenby first replaced Sean Connery as James Bond in 1969’s “On Her Majesty’s Secret Service,” fans have argued over who makes the best 007 — and those arguments are likely to resume once Robert Pattinson takes the screen as the latest Bond in director Denis Villeneuve’s new iteration. Although Craig has his partisans, Connery tends to be the consensus choice for the best Bond, though I’ve argued here for the merits of 1970s and ’80s Roger Moore. All the Bonds have their virtues, but Connery benefited from being there first — for anyone who discovered the series in its initial heyday, Connery simply is James Bond. Yet Connery actually wasn’t the first actor to play Bond. That honor goes to journeyman MGM contract player Barry Nelson, who played Bond in a “Casino Royale” adaptation eight years before Connery made the character famous in “Dr. No.” Connery may have been the first 007 to grace the big screen, but Nelson established Bond on the small screen in an episode of the CBS anthology series “Climax!” opposite Peter Lorre in the Le Chiffre role that would later be inhabited by Orson Welles (in the goofy 1967 “Casino Royale”) and Mads Mikkelsen (in the 2006 adaptation that introduced Craig to the series).

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is a fascinating artifact that includes elements that would go on to characterize future James Bond movies, while also going in a few other very different directions. The key difference is in the scale, stripped down by necessity for television production. The hour-long “Climax!” episode is all interiors, taking place entirely in the hotel and casino of the title. It’s more a James Bond play than a movie, with Bond and Le Chiffre facing off in a series of verbal — and ultimately physical — jousts in confined spaces, and it’s surprisingly effective.

It’s also a very strange beginning for a franchise that would come to be known for its globetrotting locales and elaborate action set pieces. Not that this “Casino Royale” was officially part of the James Bond franchise as we know it. In fact, although there have been three adaptations of Ian Fleming’s first James Bond novel, only the third incarnation with Daniel Craig was from EON Productions, the company that controlled the character from “Dr. No” through the Craig era before selling the rights to Amazon.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” came about when producer Gregory Ratoff optioned the rights to Fleming’s novel in May of that year. The novel wasn’t yet a hit, and Fleming was sorely in need of cash, which led him to make one of the worst deals in movie-rights history when he sold the book outright to Ratoff for $6,000 after the TV episode aired. This left “Casino Royale” out of the deal when EON secured the screen rights to James Bond as a character, so Bond producers Albert Broccoli and Harry Saltzman could only sit back and grit their teeth when Ratoff turned “Casino Royale” into a comedy in 1967 and cast Peter Sellers, David Niven, Woody Allen and others as rotating Bonds.

EON ultimately made a deal to bring “Casino Royale” under their umbrella and used the novel to reboot the franchise with Craig, but in 1954 it served as the source for a much more modest affair. The biggest similarities between the 1954 “Climax!” episode and the Daniel Craig movie come in the time spent on extended gambling sequences, and the centering of a brutal torture scene. Given censorship restrictions of the time, Nelson’s torture in a bathtub is considerably less graphic than the mallet to the testicles inflicted on Craig, but it’s rough enough stuff for a 1950s TV production.

Certain aspects of Nelson’s characterization, like Bond’s mix of sexual swagger and icy indifference, would later be adopted by most future 007s, to varying degrees. The biggest difference between Nelson and future Bonds is a simple one: He’s American. Although Broccoli and Saltzman flirted with the idea of an American Bond throughout the series’ history (going so far as to cast James Brolin in “Octopussy” before Moore decided to return at the last minute), to date they’ve never actually pulled the trigger on casting outside the UK.

Nelson is probably best known to modern audiences as the affable hotel manager Stuart Ullman in Stanley Kubrick’s “The Shining,” but in the 1950s he was a sturdy character actor and occasional leading man who bounced around television after an initial sojourn as a studio contract player. As a young man, he appeared in several important MGM films, including “Shadow of the Thin Man,” “Bataan,” and “A Guy Named Joe” (the source material for Steven Spielberg’s “Always”). Later on, he popped up in “Airport” and had a successful Broadway career. He was nominated for a Tony acting opposite Liza Minnelli in Martin Scorsese’s 1977 production “The Act.”

In 1954, though, he was in the middle of a prolific and productive era as a TV stalwart, both as a series regular (on “The Hunter” and “My Favorite Husband”) and a frequent guest star. He doesn’t exhibit a ton of range as American “Combined Intelligence” agent “Jimmy” Bond, but that too is in keeping with the overall conception of the character, at least until Craig tried to bring a little more emotional depth to the role.

In Americanizing Bond, the “Climax!” edition of “Casino Royale” ends up making a number of related changes that are small but make the film feel just a bit off for fans of the EON productions. American Felix Leiter (later played by Jeffrey Wright) becomes Brit Clarence Leiter, for example. Although there’s none of the Bond gadgetry that would become famous later, there are hints of what’s to come in the form of props like a villain’s cane that hides a gun, and the opening credits for the “Climax!” series itself feature a gun barrel pointed at the viewer that predicts the iconic graphics of later Bond title sequences.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is noteworthy for another reason: its composer, Jerry Goldsmith, who would ultimately score hundreds of major films, including “Planet of the Apes,” “Chinatown,” “Basic Instinct,” and most of Joe Dante’s work. Yet in some ways what’s fascinating about “Casino Royale” is how unremarkable it is — it’s a decent, solid piece of entertainment from an era when proliferating anthology shows provided similar programs every week.

If Fleming had his way, it would have only been the start for 007 on television; he wrote several outlines for episodes of a James Bond series before CBS canceled the project. Luckily, he repurposed most of what he wrote as short stories for his 1960 book “For Your Eyes Only,” and television’s loss was cinema’s gain. By 1962, James Bond belonged to the movies — and to Sean Connery.

“We're not saying they're perfect” says architect fighting to save Melbourne's public housing blocks

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Photography by Ben Hosking, Michael Pham

Architect Simon Robinson has been leading calls for the Victorian state government to rethink its plan to demolish all of Melbourne’s public housing towers. In this interview, he sets out why he believes retrofit is the smarter approach.

Announced by the Labor-run state government in 2023, Homes Victoria’s High-Rise Redevelopment Program (HRRP) will see all 44 of Melbourne’s public housing towers and many other lower-rise blocks razed and replaced with new housing by 2051.

Officials argue that the buildings, constructed between 1962 and 1975, have reached the end of their useful lives. They claim the towers do not meet modern standards and cost too much to maintain, and that the redevelopment programme will triple housing capacity and improve the quality of homes.

But the policy has proved highly controversial, with critics particularly angry about a lack of transparency or consultation with residents, who are being relocated.

It is now subject to an ongoing legal dispute, has prompted concern from the UN’s special rapporteur on housing, and become a political issue in the build-up to the Victorian state election later this month.

Among the voices leading calls for a change of course is Melbourne not-for-profit research and design practice Office.

Office has worked pro bono to make the case for refurbishing the towers, after learning from residents that the government has not published feasibility studies for keeping any of the condemned estates.

“We thought that was pretty odd that you wouldn’t do a refurbishment study on these estates,” Office director Simon Robinson told Dezeen.

“So we took it upon ourselves to start to do these feasibility studies, along with public housing residents.”

“We’re not saying that they’re in perfect condition,” he added. “We’re also not saying that all of them should be refurbished – perhaps some are beyond repair. All we are saying is that these rapprochement studies need to occur.”

In fact, feasibility studies have been conducted by the government but not been made public, despite requests by the Victorian Legislative Council.

By avoiding the relocation of residents, Office estimates that a retrofit and infill approach would cost around $300 million less than demolition and rebuild.

“As all the estates are occupied, the cost of relocating and housing the residents during construction work needs to be included in the project cost; this is consistently excluded and never disclosed,” said Robinson.

“We have estimated that these costs are very significant, with reports indicating that the government is spot purchasing or head-leasing properties from the private market.”

The practice has put together a counter-proposal based on a strategy that it calls Retain Repair Reinvest (RRR).

RRR focuses on retaining buildings without displacing residents, carrying out renovation work to improve housing comfort and energy efficiency while reducing construction waste and carbon impact.

Robinson agrees that most of Victoria’s existing public housing buildings are currently not fit for purpose – buildings are rundown, cold in winter, and sweltering in summer. But he believes comfortable living standards can be achieved through renovations.

Office has applied its RRR strategy to three Melbourne estates in hypothetical case studies – Ascot Vale Estate, Barak Beacon Estate and the Flemington Estate.

Formerly home to approximately 10,000 people, Flemington Estate’s four towers make up about 10 per cent of Victoria’s public housing stock.

Only about 32 households remain, though an injunction prevents Homes Victoria from serving further eviction notices while legal challenges are pending.

Office has proposed retaining the estate’s 720 dwellings and building an additional 577 infill social housing units to increase density.

New facades and private balconies would be added, with other upgrades including integrated underground parking, a central communal green space, new heating and cooling systems, improved insulation, new kitchens and bathrooms.

Energy efficiency is another key focus. Double-glazed windows, photovoltaic cells, and water conservation systems would provide a 55 percent reduction of global warming potential compared to the demolition approach, according to Office.

Despite the seemingly obvious benefits, the Victorian government has so far been unmoved by the studies presented by the practice.

Robinson believes the reason lies in the fact that the state government no longer wants to manage public housing.

“When the state government manages public housing, they are responsible for maintaining the buildings and organising new tenants – all the admin that comes with owning a building,” he said.

As well as the demolitions, also attracting concern is the type of housing proposed for redevelopment under the HRRP.

Rather than like-for-like public housing, the new homes will be “community housing” – developed by private developers, and split between below-market and market-rate rental homes.

Despite still being classed as social housing by the state government, community housing is slightly more expensive, and the tenancy is less secure from eviction.

Robinson believes the term was created to misdirect the public.

“What they’re building is community housing, not public housing, so there’s no longer any public housing being rebuilt in Victoria,” he said.

“The government is now calling everything social housing, because they don’t want the public to know that it’s no longer public housing,” he continued.

“Victoria has the lowest percentage of public housing stock in Australia, and we’re currently demolishing it all and turning it into private and community housing.”

The issue is particularly charged in the context of Australia‘s housing-affordability crisis. There are currently over 60,000 people on the waitlist for social housing in Victoria, where social housing accounts for three per cent of all dwellings, below the national average of 4.2 per cent and the OECD average of 7 per cent.

“Public housing serves as foundational social infrastructure that treats shelter as an essential human right,” said Robinson.

“By offering rents permanently capped to income and delivering unmatched security of tenure, it provides an unconditional safety net for society’s most vulnerable households.”

Office’s work has gained political attention. In November 2023, the Victorian Legislative Council debated the merits of the RRR approach, with the council’s Legal and Social Issues Committee urging the government to reconsider demolition and explore all refurbishment options.

The team can already claim a small victory. At Ascot Valley Estate, the first RRR case study released by Office, the development was quietly removed from the planned demolitions list on the Homes Victoria website and part of the estate has since been refurbished.

The estate is a collection of 57 three-storey brick housing blocks, built in 1947 and designed by renowned modernist architect and public housing advocate Best Overend during his time with the Housing Commission.

This gave Robinson and his team hope that the government would consider the alternatives if presented with the option.

“We have presented to Homes Victoria, we’ve presented to different ministers as well, and we’ve kind of heard that they are starting to look at retrofit some of their existing building stock,” said Robinson.

“It kind of suggests that perhaps they’re gonna prefer we show all of them. So we released the following studies on Barak Beacon Estate and the Flemington Estate.”

However, Barak Beacon Estate has since been demolished, and the construction of the redevelopment is due to complete by the end of the year.

Now the fate of Flemington Tower awaits a ruling from the High Court of Australia following a hearing last month.

A class action filed by Inner Melbourne Community Legal on behalf of public housing residents in North Melbourne and Flemington is on its last appeal, after the Supreme Court of Victoria and Victorian Court of Appeal ruled in favour of demolition.

Meanwhile, the Liberal opposition has pledged to pause the HRRP if it wins office at the upcoming state election.

Robinson hopes he and his team have done enough, and continue to raise awareness about the issue.

“We’ve gotten to a point where we feel we can’t just keep putting out these reports,” he said.

“The papers we produced are quite dense and not for the general public. So what we’re now doing is translating that work into a series of publications, putting it in bookshops, and making it more accessible for the general public.”

“We continue interviews with different residents, different advocates, different professionals, speaking with journalists and doing lectures. The idea is to bring awareness to what’s occurring, rather than us doing more reports.”

He is also calling for a frank discussion in the architecture community about the redevelopment projects, which many of Australia’s prominent studios are working on.

“All the big-name architects are working on these estate renewals, some have even won awards as exemplar projects for these architects to promote their practice in terms of doing public good,” said Robinson.

“It’s really hard to find anyone who’s willing to talk about why they’re doing it, or is it good, the displacement and environmental issues. We’re alone.”

Dezeen put Robinson’s claims to the Victorian state government, but was told it could not respond in the lead-up to the election.

T-19A Kestrel: Meet The US Air Force Academy's New Unlimited Aerobatic Aircraft For Cadets

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

On November 12, four new composite planes swept low over the Cadet Wing assembled at the US Air Force Academy in Colorado. The flight marked the official arrival of the T-19A Kestrel light trainer at Davis Airfield in Colorado Springs. Instead of wading through decades of defense bureaucracy and drawn-out congressional appropriations, USAFA secured its next-generation aerobatic aircraft in just 10 months, cutting through red tape with the help of a private donor group led by Andrew McKenna.

The T-19A is a derivative of the commercial GB1 GameBird. By skipping the traditional procurement, leadership bypassed the budget overruns and timeline slips that have paralyzed programs like the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk. The lightweight plane is purpose-built for agility thanks to its designer, German aerobatic champion Philipp Steinbach. He is a co-founder of Game Aerospace Composites in Bentonville, Arkansas, which produces the off-the-shelf civilian variant certified to pull a staggering load of ten G-forces.

The Kestrel went from concept to delivery in 10 months using $3 million in private donations, whereas the Red Hawk has spent over a decade in development using billions in taxpayer funds and still faces multi-year delays. The T-19A will fill a major gap in modern USAFA flight training by giving cadets early exposure to high-G, unlimited aerobatics and complex energy management before they ever step into Undergraduate Pilot Training.

The T-19A, or GB1 GameBird, is the only American-built two-seat aircraft approved for Unlimited-category competition aerobatics. Its all-carbon monocoque airframe lacks traditional mechanical wing-attach joints, relying on a continuous composite spar cured inside an autoclave. This gives the plane an operating empty weight of roughly 1,290 pounds (585 kg) while enabling symmetric flight loads of up to 10-G at its aerobatic maximum limit.

Under the hood, a 583-cubic-inch Lycoming AEIO-580-B1A flat-six piston engine delivers 303 horsepower (226 kW) to a four-blade composite MT-Propeller system. An inverted oil and fuel system keeps the engine running under sustained negative-g flight and zero-g push-overs. Cadets command the airframe from tandem seats equipped with Garmin G3X Touch glass displays, combining edge-of-envelope stick-and-rudder forces with modern electronic flight screens before stepping into standard turboprops.

To make the transition, USAFA pulled six active-duty instructor pilots from the 306th Flying Training Group to stand up the first training cadre. These officers will shape a new syllabus to train cadets and establish a demonstration and competition team. The initiative comes as the wider military struggles with a persistent shortfall of roughly 2,000 operational pilots, forcing leadership to seek shortcuts to build stick-and-rudder skills earlier in pilot development.

The Academy's existing fleet jumps from unpowered TG-16A gliders and basic T-53A/T-41D piston trainers straight to the UPT's T-6A Texan II turboprops after graduation. USAF Chief of Staff General Ken Wilsbach and Academy Superintendent Lt. Gen. Paul Moga secured the four airframes thanks to a $3 million donation coordinated by philanthropist Andrew McKenna. Meanwhile, the USAFA paid $400,000 in operational funds for initial tooling, spares, and flight gear, along with a projected annual operating budget of $1 million to maintain the fleet.

The Kestrel will act as an intermediate high-performance step, teaching cadets how to manage high power-to-weight ratios, rapid throttle response, and aggressive energy management. Standard trainers are built for stability and prohibit extreme maneuvers. Because the Kestrel is certified for unlimited aerobatics, instructors can deliberately put cadets into inverted spins, vertical stalls, and disorienting attitudes, teaching them instinctive recovery skills that directly prevent real-world loss-of-control accidents.

The Air Force is facing a persistent pilot deficit and needs to push students through UPT faster without lowering standards. By giving top cadets advanced stick-and-rudder proficiency during their junior and senior years at the Academy, they arrive at UPT needing fewer remedial rides and moving through the syllabus faster. Additionally, building an aerobatic competition team around the Kestrel will push the selected cadets to fly under public scrutiny with the precision and discipline they will further refine in operational flying squadrons.

Operating the Kestrel at the USAFA in Colorado Springs comes with real performance trade-offs. The Academy's Davis Airfield sits at an elevation of 6,572 feet (2,003 meters), and summer heat often makes the air feel like it is over 9,000 feet (2,743 meters). That means the T-19A's non-turbocharged engine loses roughly 22% to 26% of its power. That thinner mountain air requires longer takeoff runs and leaves pilots with less excess engine power during demanding maneuvers.

Weight limits create another hurdle for daily operations, as the Kestrel can weigh up to 2,200 pounds (998 kg) for standard point-to-point flights. Its maximum takeoff rating for extreme 10-G aerobatics only applies at 1,940 pounds (880 kg) or less. Subtracting the plane's 1,290-pound (585 kg) empty weight leaves just 650 pounds (295 kg) for people, gear, and fuel. Once an instructor and a cadet strap in, only enough weight allowance remains for 25 to 30 gallons (95 to 114 liters) of fuel.

That limits intense flights to roughly 45 minutes, requiring ground crews to refuel and turn the planes around quickly between sorties. Moving cadets from unpowered gliders into a 200-knot (370 km/h) airplane is also a major operational adjustment. Because rapid spins and heavy G-forces can easily cause motion sickness or make new pilots lose track of which way is up, instructors must design a step-by-step program that gradually introduces cadets to the plane's capabilities.

Built entirely from carbon fiber, the T-19A brings modern composite design to a general aviation field long dominated by traditional steel-tube frames. Even compared to the newer carbon-fiber Extra NG, the Kestrel weighs about 110 pounds (50 kg) less at an empty weight of 1,290 pounds (585 kg). Its wing and fuselage tanks also hold 81 gallons (307 liters) of fuel, giving it a cross-country range of over 1,000 nautical miles (1,852 kilometers).

In the air, the Kestrel reacts far faster than almost all other military trainers. The USAF's primary turboprop trainer, the Beechcraft T-6A Texan II, rolls at 180 degrees per second and handles forces between positive 7.0-G and negative 3.5-G. The Kestrel more than doubles that agility, rolling at 400 degrees per second while withstanding up to positive and negative 10-G.

Unlike older aerobatic planes, the Kestrel avoids the sluggish handling caused by a heavy nose-mounted radial engine. Its horizontally opposed Lycoming responds instantly, without the twisting-torque side effects of a radial engine during aggressive maneuvers. That instant throttle response also translates into blistering acceleration and vertical climb performance. Its power-to-weight ratio allows the Kestrel to take off in just 680 feet (207 meters) and climb at 4,200 feet per minute (1,280 meters per minute).

This sharp handling marks a major departure from the Academy’s existing T-53As, which are militarized Cirrus SR20s built to train for instrument flying rather than agility and dogfighting. These Remote Atolls Are Among the Last Places on Earth Humans Haven’t Touched. Here’s What Scientists Found.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Bikar and Bokak sit so far from people that they may show us what the ocean looked like before humans.

There are few havens on Earth as pristine as the Bikar and Bokak atolls. Formed from sunken volcanoes around which coral reefs grew, these atolls in the Ratak chain of the Marshall Islands teem with life and remain uninhabited by humans. Rainbows of fish swim among corals and sea anemones, while sharks glide silently through the water in pursuit of prey. Until recently, even marine biologists had rarely explored these habitats in depth.

That began to change when Alan Friedlander set out across an impossibly blue swath of ocean and led his team into paradise. Friedlander is a marine ecologist at the University of Hawai’i who served as chief scientist for National Geographic’s Pristine Seas program on the expedition, and he takes a special interest in remote and protected regions of the ocean. The people of the Marshall Islands have known Bikar and Bokak for generations, but the atolls had never been comprehensively surveyed when Friedlander and his team arrived. For the Marshallese, the ocean is home even more than the land is. Their survival depends on the health of the fragile reef ecosystems around the country’s 1,225 islands and islets, only some of which are inhabited.

The opportunity came in 2023, when Pristine Seas was invited to join the Marshall Islands Marine Resources Authority in surveying Bikar and Bokak, which are safeguarded by the Utirik Atoll community. Friedlander and his team received a traditional blessing and farewell on Utirik before sailing north to Bikar, which lies about 72 miles (116 km) from the nearest inhabited atoll. Bokak is even more isolated, about 239 miles (385 km) away. This remoteness is part of the reason the atolls have remained relatively untouched by human influence. Friedlander wanted to carry out an ecological assessment of everything from the land habitats to the reefs and the open sea. That meant studying the structure and biodiversity of these ecosystems, documenting how well they had been preserved and how the two atolls differed, and identifying the conservation measures they would need in the future.

“The remote northern atolls of Bikar and Bokak represent some of the least disturbed coral reef ecosystems remaining in the tropical Pacific,” Friedlander and his co-authors wrote in a study recently published in Frontiers in Marine Science. “Both atolls are uninhabited and geographically isolated from major population centers and industrial development. Previous expeditions documented portions of their terrestrial habitats, seabird colonies, sea turtles, and shallow coral reefs.”

The team’s first stop was Bikar, which curves across the water like a crescent moon. There, the researchers used a variety of sampling methods, including scuba surveys, photography, underwater video, deep-sea cameras, and a submarine, to record which species were present and in what numbers. Polynesian rats, which humans introduced, were sometimes seen scurrying through the island’s old-growth Pisonia forests. Even so, native species still thrive. Seabirds such as masked boobies, great frigatebirds, black noddies, and white terns filled the skies, while shorebirds like the migratory Pacific golden plover pecked at the sand in search of small invertebrates. Bikar’s beaches are also a nesting ground for green sea turtles; the team recorded 132 nest pits there. Its reefs are full of fish, including several species of snapper and grouper. Giant clams, which host photosynthetic algae, filter the water and are vital to the health of the reef.

Farther north and even more isolated, Bokak is an oasis that has seen very little human interference. Its drier, savanna-like landscape is covered in tufts of bunchgrass and tree heliotropes, which bear clusters of small white flowers. Many of the seabirds that soar over Bikar are also found on Bokak. Bokak’s waters are even richer than Bikar’s: its reefs are dominated by coral and hold more fish by weight. Friedlander dove in to investigate. He came across a school of humphead parrotfish, a threatened species once thought to have nearly vanished from the islands. He also saw an unusual reversal of the typical food pyramid. Reef sharks and black jacks outweigh their prey in total biomass. Because these top predators feed so often, they keep energy and nutrients moving quickly through the reef, which helps the ecosystem run efficiently.

After Bokak, Friedlander’s team had one more location to survey, and it offered a sharp contrast. From 1946 to 1958, Bikini Atoll was a center of U.S. nuclear testing, starting with Operation Crossroads. When the islanders were evacuated before the first tests, they took only a few changes of clothes, not knowing that they would never be able to resettle their home for good. The series of detonations devastated the atoll. From the ocean’s glassy surface, Bikini seems to be ringed by cities of coral. But divers who swam closer found that many of these formations, which had taken thousands of years to grow, were little more than skeletons. Still, Bikini Atoll is not without hope. After searching long enough, Friedlander found patches of live coral that had settled on the dead reef and were swarming with fish. It was a sign of how resilient life can be.

Even Bikar and Bokak can’t fully escape what is happening elsewhere. Their remoteness protects them from most direct human pressures, but climate change is slowly reaching them, too. When the water gets too hot, corals expel the symbiotic algae that feed them and give them their bright colors, a process known as coral bleaching. Leftover radiation from the nuclear-testing era is still a source of contamination in the region. Meanwhile, currents carry in trash from other countries, and plastics break down into microplastics. People are responding with conservation measures. In 2025, two years after the expedition, the government of the Marshall Islands designated the waters around Bikar and Bokak, about 18,500 square miles (48,000 square km), as the country’s first national marine sanctuary, as part of the community-led Reimaanlok conservation framework. Despite the threats that come from far away, Friedlander is optimistic.

“As relatively intact tropical marine ecosystems become increasingly rare worldwide,” he and his co-authors concluded, “remote atolls such as Bikar and Bokak serve not only as regional conservation priorities but also as globally important ecological reference systems for understanding the structure and future trajectories of tropical oceanic ecosystems in the Anthropocene.”

IEEE Celebrates 10 Years of Incorporating Ethics Into AI Systems

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Standards and certifications foster socially responsible products

Kathy Pretz is the editor in chief of The Institute, IEEE's member publication

Policymakers, technologists, and members of the public have raised concerns about the harm AI’s rapid advancement might cause to society. Their concerns include bias, data privacy violations, the spread of misinformation, and losing control of AI agents.

Those issues are relatively new to many people, but the IEEE Standards Association has been working for the past decade on ways to prioritize the responsible development of AI systems.

In 2016 IEEE SA launched the IEEE Global Initiative for Ethical Considerations in Autonomous and Intelligent Systems (A/IS) and published the Ethically Aligned Design report. EAD’s mission is to encourage everyone involved in designing autonomous and intelligent systems to prioritize human well-being by applying ethical considerations for the benefit of humanity.

Developed by a multidisciplinary community of experts from around the world, the report outlined eight foundational principles for A/IS. They include human rights, people’s well-being, and transparency. More than 100 leading AI ethics experts provided input. EAD’s Version II report was released in 2017. Both versions solicited public input and received hundreds of pages of feedback, according to John C. Havens, who was the global initiative’s founding executive director, and is now the global director for IEEE Planet Positive 2030. And by 2019, there were more than 400 contributors and the community continues to grow.

“Ethically Aligned Design was the first of its kind in the sense of a report that was more than a list of principles and from an organization with the credibility of IEEE,” Havens says. “At the time it was released, there wasn’t anything like it.”

Since its launch, a series of standards has been developed to provide practical guidelines on designing, building, and deploying technology that prioritizes human values. Several AI ethics certification programs have been introduced.

This year marks the 10th anniversary of EAD’s first draft. To celebrate, IEEE SA recently launched a website, a podcast series, and a blog that reflect on the report’s achievements and impact.

The word ethics is often used interchangeably with morality, but that is not the case in responsible AI systems design, according to EAD’s first version. The report includes insights from classical ethics traditions and value-based design methodologies to offer actionable recommendations for engineers developing AI tools.

The report established eight foundational general principles for designing autonomous systems: respecting human rights, prioritizing increased well-being as success criteria for development, empowering individuals with data agency to have control over their identity, effectiveness, transparency, accountability, awareness of misuse, and competence for the system creators.

The principles shift applied ethics to the design stage of AI systems, compared with being a concern after they’re released, the authors wrote.

The IEEE 7000 series of standards has been developed based on EAD’s framework. Currently the series consists of 29 projects, 11 of which have been published, and more are still being added. They cover topics such as ethical system design model process (IEEE 7000-2021), AI system transparency (IEEE 7001-2021), data privacy (IEEE 7002-2022), algorithmic bias considerations (IEEE 7003-2024), the impact on people’s well-being (IEEE 7010-2020), machine-readable personal privacy requirements (IEEE 7012-2025), and systems that mimic human empathy (IEEE 7014-2024).

IEEE SA offers a suite of ethics in AI programs including IEEE CertifAIEd, which includes two certifications: one for individuals and one for products.

The professional certification provides individuals with skills to assess autonomous and intelligent systems for adherence to IEEE’s methodology and ethics criteria.

“Ethically Aligned Design was the first of its kind in the sense of a report that was more than a list of principles and from an organization with the credibility of IEEE.” —John C. Havens, founding executive director of the global initiative

The product certification program assesses whether an organization’s AI tool or system conforms to the criteria in the IEEE Ontological Specifications. A CertifiAIEd assessor evaluates the product to ensure it meets the criteria. Upon completion of the assessment, the owner submits it to the IEEE Conformity Assessment Program, which can certify it and issue the certification mark.

IEEE SA also recently released its Responsible AI for Children certification program. This program certifies that AI products for children have been independently tested and verified to meet design and ethics requirements.

The IEEE Artificial Intelligence Systems (AIS) for Global Flourishing Collaborative Industry Collections group was formed to provide the resources, knowledge, and tools needed to prioritize people’s well-being to developers of AI systems. It has more than 200 members.

The collaborative describes its work as a practical, systems-level framework for responsible technology design that translates flourishing into applied methods, metrics, and tools that can be used throughout the AIS lifecycle.

The group is establishing guidelines for how to design, evaluate, and govern systems that can measurably improve human well-being and ecological sustainability.

Subcommittees are focused on cognition, education, faith, planetary care, spirituality, and other issues.

“The group is building directly off the well-being chapter of Ethically Aligned Design and the IEEE 7010 standard (Recommended Practice for Assessing the Impact of Autonomous and Intelligent Systems on Human Well-Being),” Havens says. “That’s on purpose because if human and planetary flourishing aren’t addressed first in design, then human well-being and planetary sustainability are afterthoughts.”

To mark the 10-year anniversary, IEEE SA created the five-part “Ethically Aligned Design: 10 Years of Impact” podcast series. It launched on 21 October. New episodes are being released every two weeks.

The episodes center around the vision for EAD, the process for selecting its foundational principles, and how they were translated into the IEEE 7000 standards.

The first podcast, “Origins of Ethically Aligned Design,” explored early efforts to make ethics a practical part of AI and autonomous system design. The experts discussed challenges, debates, global collaboration, and the real-world impact that helped move responsible technology from ethical principles toward actionable standards and practices.

The second podcast, “The Chapters and Committees” focuses on the multidisciplinary groups that worked on turning broad human values into practical guidance for AI and autonomous systems.

The podcast discussions serve as material for a thought leadership program that also includes blog articles about the IEEE 7000 series and educational resources to continue to raise awareness and support of this groundbreaking work.
