The traditional back squat has long been the staple of those who want to crush their quads, but standing under a heavy barbell is not for everyone. Mature gym goers, or anyone with mobility issues, may find that the downward force of carrying a heavy bar across the shoulders leads to stress on the spine. Fortunately, IFBB Pro and physique transformation coach, Tanya Johansen swears by the Spanish squat.

“Try a Spanish squat,” recommends the Bikini Pro competitor. “It fries your quads… when done correctly.”

The Spanish squat is a popular movement utilized for rehabbing the knees, but it also pounds the quadriceps, providing the perfect environment for muscle growth. To try it for yourself, stand in front of a cable station or power rack and anchor a resistance band around the support, looping around the back of your knees. You can stand inside a single loop, or loop the band around each knee, as demonstrated by Johansen.

Next, move back a little to make sure there is tension on the band. Now, you should be able to squat by sitting back with the band keeping you upright. Keep your shins vertical, and place emphasis on the quads as you lift and lower your body.  “Band behind your knees, sit back into it, stay tall, let your knees come forward,” summarized the coach, who specializes in physiques that are over 40.

Because there is no barbell compressing your body, the Spanish squat can be executed without overloading the back and knee joints. The quads can still be worked hard with no additional weight, and for those looking to improve their range of mobility, Spanish squats are awesome for building knee strength because they rehab the patellar and its related tendons.

“Over 40? Even better reason,” advises the coach of the Spanish squat. “You don’t need a heavy bar on your back to grow your legs. Forget heavy. Make the muscle do the work.”  And, for those who use the barbell as a crux for their ego, this IFBB Pro has some real talk. “You’re not in there to impress anyone,” she explained. “You’re in there to make sure your quads earn it.”

To make this move more challenging, you can bring in an isometric hold at the top of the squat, pausing before descending back down. You can also slow down the lowering phase for additional time under tension. Aim for 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps.

“It’s not the weight. It’s the work,” concluded Johansen, who’s own physique is proof positive that training smart can bring serious progress.

With four regular-season home games played and four to go, the Broncos are being proactive — again — about the quality of the playing surface at Mile High Stadium.

The entire field is being ripped up and replaced, despite the absence of any complaints about the quality of the grass there.

For the fifth straight year, the Broncos are spending the money to ensure that the players will be performing on the highest-quality surface, emphasizing that “the Walton Penner Family Ownership Group’s commitment to player safety and a best-in-class playing surface throughout the season.”

At a time when the debate regarding real grass vs. fake grass continues, with the situation boiling down to whether the owners are willing to write the check to ensure the best and safest surfaces for players, the statement made by the Broncos makes their ownership look good and, in comparison, owners who cling to artificial turf look bad.

Beyond caring about player safety, having the best playing surface provides the best protection of the investment the owners have made in their rosters. Which would be a sensible position for any owner — especially the cheap ones.

Now that it has morphed into a matter of collective bargaining, those who choose to be pennywise have even more cover for their pound foolishness. Which makes it even more important that the NFL Players Association apply not general public pressure to the league but specific scrutiny to the owners who won’t ditch artificial turf for grass, or who won’t replace low-quality grass with high-quality grass.

Vin Diesel’s 'Van Helsing' Replacement 'The Last Witch Hunter' Officially Lands on Prime Video

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Although he's best known for his work in the Fast & Furious saga, Vin Diesel has had a long and colorful career that leans into sci-fi, historical drama, superheroes, and action flicks alike. But on just one occasion, the international superstar ventured into fantasy territory with the criminally underrated The Last Witch Hunter, a film that recently left Netflix only to arrive on the free-to-stream platform Tubi. That isn't to say that The Last Witch Hunter is a phenomenal piece of cinema, mind you—but it is an action-based urban fantasy popcorn flick that absolutely delivers on the spectacle, and for that reason, it's worth a watch.

The Last Witch Hunter exists in the same vein as films like Van Helsing or Underworld. It's a fantasy that anchors itself in an "old world" but updates the material to the present day. It could also be compared to I, Frankenstein (which is maybe not the best selling point), but Diesel's action-packed low-budget fantasy is better on just about every front. The 2015 feature follows a lone protagonist, a widower named Kaulder, who has been cursed with immortality by the Witch Queen (Julie Engelbrecht), who he kills after she has killed his family. With time on his side, Kaulder chooses to use his new curse to spend his days hunting down the sorcerers who have made the world a living hell. To help in his quest, Kaulder has allied himself with an organization called the "Axe and Cross," which assigns him a new "Dolan" every lifetime, from Michael Caine at the beginning to newcomer Elijah Wood. Of course, that begins to change when Chloe (Rose Leslie) enters the picture.

For what it is, The Last Witch Hunter is actually a lot of fun. Not only is it a unique take on the witch-hunter concept, but the whole thing was grounded in Disel's own love for the fantasy genre. When speaking to HitFlix prior to the release of the film, the star revealed that his own experience growing up with Dungeons & Dragons was what inspired the titular character in the first place. Long before Dungeons & Dragons: Honor Among Thieves made waves in 2023, Diesel met with screenwriter Cory Goodman (who had previously written The Searchers-inspired vampire thriller Priest), and the pair bonded over their shared love of D&D. The result was a movie inspired by Diesel’s own character Melkor the Witch Hunter, "a name that obviously I stole from The Silmarillion," Diesel added — and who hasn't stolen from Tolkien for D&D? (This author once named his dragon hunter "Eru Ilúvatar," if that tells you anything.) With plenty of rich fantasy lore at its center, this fast-paced, visually stunning, and creature-heavy action flick is 106 minutes you won't mind spending with Vin Diesel.

The Last Witch Hunter may not have been well-received by critics or audiences, but the action is great and the special effects are nothing to write off, either. Even if Diesel's performance leaves a bit to be desired, it's hard to ignore the care and personal connection he put into bringing Kaulder's story to life. Perhaps what comes as the biggest surprise, however, is that The Last Witch Hunter is returning for more. Vin Diesel is working on a sequel, one that is set to bring even Michael Caine out of retirement to reprise his role as Dolan 36. Will it be able to live up to the wackiness of the original? Who knows, but considering many of us wish movies like Van Helsing had gotten their own sequels back in the day (or at least a television spin-off), those who enjoyed this supernatural thriller will certainly be happy.

So, if you're looking for some mindless entertainment to enjoy on an otherwise free evening, give The Last Witch Hunter a go. It's an oddball take on the genre, but one with a rich mythology and some impressive visuals that make it worth the watch. Although Halloween is over, it's still fall, meaning that now is just as good a time to return to this strange supernatural world than now — especially now that you can stream it for free.

The Last Witch Hunter is available for streaming on Tubi.

Awesome repositories are no longer awesome

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On GitHub, you will find a lot of repositories with the prefix 'awesome'. They usually contain curated knowledge and resources related to a certain domain, such as awesome-python (see, e.g., this repository for a list of awesome repositories). I used to see these awesome repositories as potential goldmines of good content. However, nowadays I see most of them as irrelevant and useless. There are two reasons for this, and they are both related to AI.

First, as many of these awesome lists get older, they contain a lot of outdated material if they are not actively maintained. You are in most cases better off simply asking a decent AI model for inspiration or the material you need. That is, I find it difficult to believe that most of these awesome lists are of interest to people anymore. If they remain useful, it will mostly be for agents looking to find and check relevant resources.

I had a quick look at the awesome-R repository. The repository was last updated more than a year ago, and most of the resources included in the overview are either outdated or will not be of general interest to people using R. One of the first links in the repository is an overview of the top 50 downloaded packages - as of 2016! This is simply not a good resource if you want to explore R in any shape or form.

Second, a lot of these awesome repositories are being flooded with AI slop. Go to any random awesome repository these days and you will most likely find a lot of pull requests suggesting the most generic tools and resources. For example, take a look at this awesome-free-online-tools repository. It is a small, unknown repository with 13 stars but 91 open pull requests at the time of writing. I did not look at all of these pull requests, but the ones I looked at all suggested generic vibe-coded tools.

I found this resource because of a pull request made to one of my repositories, a simple project where I store material I like related to statistics. The suggestion was to add StatOrigin ("a free, open database of industry statistics"), and once I had a quick look at the resource and the other pull requests made by the user, I could easily close the PR. This user alone has created at least six vibe-coded websites (StatOrigin, ScanChess, Sticker Printer Hub, QuizQuip, LiquidityPools, DefiLPKit) and submitted more than 100 pull requests to various awesome lists to add these websites.

It did not take me long to find examples where these tools were merged into the relevant repositories (see, e.g., here, here, and here). However, I also found examples where people did the basic work of checking out the material, such as here, where the PR was closed with the following explanation: "There is not yet sufficient evidence of qualified educator review, independent validation of the educational content, or demonstrated learning outcomes." Good stuff!

My point is not that these awesome repositories are now all bad. They can still be awesome. But as a general approach to finding good resources, awesome lists are less awesome than they used to be. As AI gets better at finding and creating resources (and creating pull requests for said resources), these awesome repositories will become less relevant and less useful.

This is the question I’ve been asking myself since it inaugurated its Apple Music Hall, nestled underneath its U.K. headquarters at London’s Battersea Power Station, in late September. Not just any performance space, this intimate 600-person, 48-speaker hall features Apple’s Spatial Audio technology, which uses Dolby Atmos and dynamic head tracking to turn flat stereo sound into a three-dimensional, 360-degree experience.

To my eye, the Music Hall is not just another listening room. It’s the start of an elaborate world-building exercise for Apple Music, designed to engage a community of hardcore fans. In doing so, Apple is looking to increase streaming subscriptions, convert artists to recording on its technology, and eventually challenge everyone from Google Home to Sonos for ownership of our in-home sound delivery systems.

An uphill battle

In just eight years since entering the TV market, Apple has shifted from startup streamer to a television mecca, delivering hit after hit including Severance, Slow Horses, Silo, Pluribus, Ted Lasso, and now Widow’s Bay. Astounding.

Meanwhile, in music it’s played second fiddle to Spotify, which not only boasts three times as many subscribers, but is first with innovative ideas like its Year End Review and the exclusive content serious listeners crave most. The Music Hall is Apple turning up the volume.

The risks here are real. While global spend on IRL brand experiences grew 8.3% to $138.9 billion in 2025 and is projected to rise a further 10.3% in 2026, the highways to hell have far outstripped the stairways to heaven. Samsung 837, the company’s former experiential space in New York City’s Meatpacking District, succeeded in attracting mothers with young children looking for rainy day distractions but few potential customers. Many car companies—Lexus, Genesis, Cadillac—have tried their hand at luxury lounges, only to be reminded that consumers are looking to them for, well, cars.


Those that are true to their roots, however, are hitting all the right notes. A24, the film and TV production company, experienced a huge brand win with its acquisition last year of the historic Cherry Lane Theater in NYC’s West Village. This has been a launchpad for new and experimental content that in turn has led to greater visibility for the studio, deeper fan engagement, and a two-year brand partnership with Dior. Porsche’s Experience Centers delight driving enthusiasts and attract new customers with authentic racing outings.

In seemingly sparing no expense to create a world-class performance space, Apple is signaling to audiophiles everywhere that it is serious about building the world of Apple Music.

Building a successful brand world

Three essential elements go into building a successful brand world. The first is the Functional Offer, quite simply what the brand makes. For example, Volvo builds cars. The second is the Emotional Promise, which is how a brand is experienced emotionally. Through good Scandinavian design Volvo makes people feel great driving them. The third is the Cultural Quest. This is the way the brand engages with the world or a larger topic it constantly explores. For Volvo it is to have no one die in its vehicles. Ever.

All brands achieve the first; many achieve the second; very few achieve the third. The Music Hall gives Apple a route to achieving all three: not only producing and distributing music (offer), or making people feel good while listening (promise), but in our increasingly isolated, AI-fueled world, bringing music fans together around the highest quality, most authentic audio experiences, be they physical or digital (quest).

The key is cultivating a community engaging in meaningful participation around a brand world that is authentic to music.

The key question is, therefore, “How might Apple cultivate this passionate community of music fans?”

Lean into the community

Apple Music will need to do more than deliver music. It will need to become synonymous with the production and presentation of the world’s finest content. Opening with Elton John was a signal of intent.

Baby Keem, Skye Newman, Troye Sivan, Arlo Parks, Burna Boy, Chase & Status, Charli XCX—a real lineup to be sure. Now imagine that with its war chest, Apple starts attracting more prominent acts, and that music (sounding better recorded on Spatial Audio) is available only through a subscription on Apple Music.

The athletic wear brands like On and Bandit have been very successful with their run clubs. Might Apple establish its version? In partnership with Apple Pay and a major credit card company, early access to events is not hard to imagine. Nor are listening parties at the coolest venues for those who can’t attend events in person.

What’s at stake?

In its services division, Apple has shown with Pay and Health that it likes opportunities with huge addressable markets that plug in nicely to its hardware ecosystem. The global home audio hardware market, whose current players have set a fairly low bar, is valued at approximately $43.2 billion in 2026. If Apple can capture a good share of smart home audio and combine that with a greater share of the $54.54 billion global audio streaming market, the dollars become very real.

By striking the right chord between premium audio hardware and subscription streaming, Apple is perfectly positioned to turn this massive market overlap into music to its investors’ ears.

Fantasy football Start ‘Em, Sit ‘Em: 30 players to start or sit in Week 9

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Week 9 of the 2026 NFL season has already brought with it plenty of chaos, as several teams are making notable moves at the quarterback position.

A microcosm of the league's greater movement can be seen with the Baltimore Ravens. The Ravens lost Lamar Jackson and Tyler Huntley to long-term injuries and will start undrafted rookie Joe Fagnano on "Thursday Night Football." However, they are also planning to trade for Arizona Cardinals quarterback Jacoby Brissett over the weekend, so their quarterback room is in flux.

The same is true with the Seattle Seahawks, where Drew Lock will replace an injured Sam Darnold, and with the Cleveland Browns, where Shedeur Sanders may start with Deshaun Watson in concussion protocol. And that's not to mention the Philadelphia Eagles' decision to bench Tanner McKee to see what they have in the potential-packed Joe Milton.

That swath of quarterback changes will leave many fantasy managers with difficult start 'em, sit 'em decisions. Those quandaries will also exist at other positions like running back, where fantasy footballers will have to decide whether to trust Ashton Jeanty or Tyler Allgeier, among others.

Who can you start and sit in fantasy football for Week 9 of the 2026 NFL season? USA TODAY Sports outlines 30 players to start or sit.

Malik Willis, Miami Dolphins (vs. Detroit Lions)

Willis is starting to round into form for the Dolphins and has scored multiple touchdowns in three consecutive games. During that span, he has averaged 37.7 rushing yards per game, which will raise his floor in most matchups. The Lions have allowed the most fantasy points per game (FPPG) to quarterbacks this season, so Willis should be positioned to have a strong showing in what figures to be a favorable, high-scoring matchup.

Drew Lock, Seattle Seahawks (vs. Arizona Cardinals)

The Cardinals are one of just three NFL teams to allow 18 passing touchdowns to quarterbacks this season, along with the Lions and the Las Vegas Raiders. Unsurprisingly, Arizona has allowed a league-high 29.8 fantasy points per game to the position because of that, so Lock should be positioned for a nice outing in which he could toss multiple touchdowns.

Joe Milton, Philadelphia Eagles (vs. New York Giants)

If you're looking for a fun dart throw, Milton could be worth considering. He's set to start in place of Tanner McKee and has the cannon arm and elite athleticism needed to blossom into a fantasy asset. The Giants have allowed the seventh-most FPPG to quarterbacks, so Milton has a chance to emerge as a decent streamer.

Quinshon Judkins, Cleveland Browns (at New Orleans Saints)

Judkins has been boom-or-bust as a touchdown-dependent fantasy option, but he should be poised for a good outing against the Saints. New Orleans has allowed the most FPPG to running backs this season, including a league-high 11 rushing touchdowns to the position. With that in mind, Judkins should be able to find his way into the end zone.

Jonah Coleman, Denver Broncos (at Carolina Panthers)

Coleman took on a lion's share of Denver's backfield touches in Week 9, posting 104 total yards and a touchdown on 15 touches (11 carries, four receptions). The rookie should continue in that role and has a great matchup against a Panthers defense that has allowed the second-most FPPG to running backs this season.

Tyler Allgeier, Arizona Cardinals (at Seattle Seahawks)

Seattle is a tough matchup for any running back, but Allgeier figures to get great volume with Jeremiyah Love (ankle) on IR and Bam Knight dealing with a concussion. That could be enough to make him a touchdown-dependent flex play after he popped off for 191 total yards and two touchdowns against the Cowboys.

Jadarian Price, Seattle Seahawks (vs. Arizona Cardinals)

Price and Zach Charbonnet split carries for the Seahawks against the Bears. Neither was particularly effective but each gets a much easier matchup in Week 9 against a Cardinals defense that has been middle-of-the-pack against running backs this season. Price has some explosiveness, so don't be surprised if this is his breakout game.

Caleb Douglas, Miami Dolphins (vs. Detroit Lions)

As mentioned, the Lions have been weak against the pass this year. If we're recommending to start Willis, then Douglas should get a nod too, as the rookie has notched six receptions for 113 yards and two touchdowns in two games since returning from an early-season ankle injury.

Ladd McConkey, Los Angeles Chargers (vs. Houston Texans)

Trusting McConkey may seem risky, given that Justin Herbert has struggled under pressure this season. That said, the Texans are allowing the second-most FPPG to receivers this season and McConkey is fresh off a four-catch, 86-yard outing that was one of his best of the season. He should be in line for a solid showing against the Texans.

Josh Downs, Indianapolis Colts (vs. Dallas Cowboys)

Could this be a showcase game for Downs ahead of the 2026 NFL trade deadline? It sure feels like it. Dallas has allowed the fifth-most FPPG to receivers this season, and the Colts may be eager to boost Downs' trade value by targeting him early and often in this spot.

Brian Thomas Jr., Jacksonville Jaguars (at Baltimore Ravens)

The same is true of Thomas, who could be traded as he sees his role shrink in Jacksonville. He caught a touchdown last week and could be in line for ample playing time if the Jaguars can build a big lead against a Joe Fagnano-led Ravens team on "Thursday Night Football."

Mike Gesicki, Cincinnati Bengals (at Atlanta Falcons)

Gesicki has scored in five consecutive games and six of the seven he has played this season. Joe Burrow has thrown 13 touchdown passes over his last three games, so Gesicki should continue to have high touchdown upside regardless of his opponent.

Greg Dulcich, Miami Dolphins (vs. Detroit Lions)

Dulcich has posted at least four catches and 37 yards in four of his last five games. Now, he gets to face a Lions defense that is allowing the most receptions (61), receiving yards (684) and receiving touchdowns (11) to tight ends this season. He could emerge as a surprise top-10 tight end this week.

Jacksonville Jaguars (at Baltimore Ravens)

The Jaguars are playing the Ravens on a short week. Baltimore is expected to start third-string quarterback Joe Fagnano, an undrafted rookie, after injuries to Lamar Jackson and Tyler Huntley. Fagnano completed 10 of 21 passes for 91 yards and an interception while being sacked three times against the Bills. The Jaguars should pressure him into numerous mistakes while their top-ranked run defense should be able to bottle up Derrick Henry.

The Lions will likely give up some yardage and points to the Dolphins, but they should also generate plenty of sacks. Detroit has 24 sacks this season, third-most in the NFL, while Malik Willis has been sacked 21 times through seven starts.

Bryce Young, Carolina Panthers (vs. Denver Broncos)

Young has regressed over the last two weeks after a torrid start to the season, completing just 54.4% of his passes for 372 yards, two touchdowns and three interceptions during Carolina's two-game losing skid. The main issue? Young has been pressured a lot in those contests, and he has just a 64 passer rating when pressured compared to 106.3 when kept clean, per Pro Football Focus. The Broncos rank fourth in pressure rate league-wide, per the NFL's Next Gen Stats, so they should be able to limit Young in this favorable spot.

Bo Nix, Denver Broncos (at Carolina Panthers)

The Panthers have a banged-up secondary, but it still won't be easy to justify starting Nix. The Broncos quarterback has looked better recently, throwing for exactly 222 yards in back-to-back games and scoring twice in each. Still, the Panthers are typically more susceptible against the run, so Nix has a lower ceiling than most streaming options this week.

Daniel Jones, Indianapolis Colts (vs. Dallas Cowboys)

Normally, the quarterback playing the Cowboys is a solid streaming option. However, with Jones facing down the potential for a benching and having failed to throw multiple touchdown passes in a game this season, it's best to steer clear of him.

Ashton Jeanty, Las Vegas Raiders (at San Francisco 49ers)

Can you really trust Jeanty after the Raiders gave him just two carries against the Jets while giving Mike Washington Jr. a whopping 25? Jeanty will remain productive as a receiver, but his uncertain between-the-tackles workload makes it hard to trust the former first-round pick, especially against a 49ers defense allowing just 3.6 yards per carry to running backs this season. It's better to wait and see whether Jeanty's decrease in carries was punishment for trash-talking the Jets before Week 8 or a sign of what's to come in Klint Kubiak's running back rotation.

Breece Hall, New York Jets (at Kansas City Chiefs)

Hall seems likely to return from a nagging quad injury this week, but he gets a tough matchup against a Chiefs defense that has largely bottled up opposing running backs this season. Hall may also be splitting carries with Braelon Allen, who has thrived in his stead, so this doesn't seem like an ideal spot to trust him.

Aaron Jones and Jordan Mason, Minnesota Vikings (at Buffalo Bills)

We'll do two for the price of one here. The Vikings have a bottom-six rushing offense this season in terms of EPA per play, per the NFL's Next Gen Stats. Jones and Mason are also stuck in a frustrating time share, so neither seems like a particularly viable play against a Bills defense that has allowed just 97.4 rushing yards per game this season.

Michael Wilson, Arizona Cardinals (at Seattle Seahawks)

Wilson finally had a breakout game for the first time since Marvin Harrison Jr. was traded, generating eight catches for 111 yards and a touchdown against the Cowboys. He'll have a much tougher matchup against a Seahawks team that has been excellent against receivers this year, especially after Wilson suffered a couple of broken ribs on his touchdown catch.

Adonai Mitchell, New York Jets (at Kansas City Chiefs)

Mitchell just logged seven catches for 142 yards and a touchdown against the Raiders in Week 8. He gets a much tougher matchup in Week 9 against a Chiefs defense that has allowed the second-fewest FPPG to receivers this season. Add in that Omar Cooper Jr. could also siphon targets from him and Mitchell may be a hit-or-miss play each week even if Garrett Wilson (concussion) is unable to play.

Jalen Coker, Carolina Panthers (vs. Denver Broncos)

The Broncos get a lot of pressure, so Young won't have much time to throw in that matchup. That could limit Coker's deep-ball upside, and while he could rack up yards after the catch, it's hard to trust him to do so against a quality defense.

Khalil Shakir, Buffalo Bills (at Minnesota Vikings)

Shakir started well in the Bills' win over the Ravens but did little outside the first drive. DJ Moore seems likely to continue getting most of the attention from Josh Allen, so don't be surprised if Shakir struggles against a Vikings defense that has allowed the third-fewest FPPG to receivers this season.

Dalton Kincaid, Buffalo Bills (at Minnesota Vikings)

Kincaid scored a touchdown last week for the first time since Week 2, but he will be hard-pressed to repeat that feat against the Vikings. Minnesota has yet to allow a receiving touchdown to tight ends this season, so Kincaid figures to be limited.

T.J. Hockenson, Minnesota Vikings (vs. Buffalo Bills)

The Bills have only allowed one touchdown to tight ends this season. It also isn't clear whether Kyler Murray (hamstring) will play in this contest, so Hockenson might have a lower ceiling in this matchup than most.

No NFL team has allowed fewer points per game than the Vikings (13.3). No NFL team has scored more points per game than the Bills (33.3). Something will give in this matchup, but if there's one quarterback that's built to beat Brian Flores' pressure-happy scheme, it's Josh Allen.

San Francisco 49ers (vs. Las Vegas Raiders)

The 49ers are favored to beat the Raiders, but this could end up being a shootout given how Kirk Cousins and Co. have been playing. Las Vegas is averaging 27.6 points per game, so even if Nick Bosa returns, this is still a low-floor spot for San Francisco.

Palantir Just Made Company History. Is the Stock a Buy?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Palantir's business is booming, but I'm concerned about the stock.

Palantir (PLTR -2.11%) just did something for the first time during its third-quarter earnings report: Its U.S. commercial revenue surpassed U.S. government revenue.

That's a big deal, because Palantir got its start selling its AI-powered software to the government. As Palantir transitions to this new paradigm, is that a good thing for the company? Or is it a concern?

I think it's a good thing, as the company is spreading its customer base out among different clients that wouldn't be affected by a government spending crackdown. But does that make the stock a buy overall? Let's take a look.

Palantir continues its streak of crushing expectations and outperforming where both management and the analyst community thought results would be. Outperformance is becoming the expectation, as management has consistently underguided and overdelivered every quarter (which isn't a bad thing).

In Q3, Palantir's revenue reached $2.3 billion, up 96% year over year. At the end of Q2, management told investors to expect $2.16 billion in revenue, so this was a sizable beat. Another factor Palantir has going for it is that its net income margin remained strong. It posted a 49% profit margin in Q3, making it one of the more profitable companies on Earth.

If that's the only perspective you have on Palantir's stock, it's easy to conclude that the stock is a strong buy. These results are simply incredible, and doubting Palantir's business isn't a smart move.

However, investing in the stock is a different story.

The price you pay for a stock matters, and even the most impressive company bought at the wrong price tag can lead to underperformance or even negative returns. Palantir's stock (which the company has no control over) is toeing that line.

Palantir's stock trades for a very expensive 110 times this year's earnings estimates and 76 times next year's.

PLTR PE Ratio (Forward 1y) data by YCharts

Somehow, among the broad AI sell-off, Palantir's stock has remained highly valued. Companies on the infrastructure side are trading for less than 20 times next year's earnings, and even the AI hyperscalers are trading in the low 20s. This showcases just how expensive Palantir's stock is, and its day of reckoning could be coming.

I don't think the stock is a buy here, as it's just too expensive compared to some of the other AI investments available. While I'm a huge fan of Palantir, the business, I'm much more concerned about Palantir's stock. As a result, I think it's one to stay avoid.
