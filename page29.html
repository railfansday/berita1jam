iRestore Elite Laser Hair Growth System Review: Husband-Approved

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The Aging Process is hard. It’s jarring, for example, when the luscious, flowing locks of your youth start to disappear before your very eyes. But there's nothing to do about this natural hair loss—or is there? While still the subject of ongoing research, red-light therapy has been used to treat inflammation, pain relief, and wrinkles, and—as it turns out—it may also help with hair regrowth. It’s also a much more accessible option than flying to Istanbul for a full-on hair transplant.

I learned about iRestore’s Elite Laser Growth Hair System after it came across my TikTok FYP. My algorithm likely served it to me because of my own hair woes (scalp irritation and eczema). Upon learning more about the system, I hoped it could help me with cell regeneration and kill off the bacteria causing flares on my scalp.

However, an even better test subject stood before me: my sweet husband, who is dealing with a rapidly receding hairline. So I roped him into testing this laser helmet—for science, of course. And to better understand said science, I spoke with Zakia Rahman, MD, FAAD, a clinical professor of dermatology at Stanford University School of Medicine.

Red-light therapy didn’t start as a treatment for antiaging and recovery. It actually came about as an accidental solution for hair loss in the 1960s, when Hungarian scientist Endre Mester was researching whether exposure to red light could treat cancer in hairless mice. Imagine his surprise when the mice started growing hair.

Red-light therapy has come a long way since then. According to Rahman, red-light wavelengths are thought to activate the mitochondria in skin cells, which may encourage an uptick in the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to promote new growth. Red-light therapy may also improve blood flow to hair follicles.

Importantly, iRestore's devices are FDA-cleared—a safety designation from the US Food and Drug Administration indicating that a product is “substantially equivalent” to another legally marketed device. There are three hair-growth devices in iRestore's lineup: the entry-level Essential ($425), the mid-tier Professional ($799), and the Elite ($1,699). Each contains iRestore’s proprietary Lumitech technology, which combines LED lights and medical-grade lasers. These laser diodes, called Vixo lasers, operate in the range of 655 to 680 nanometers (nm). (Preliminary studies with mice have shown that using red light in the 600- to 660-nm range, and infrared wavelengths in the 800- to 850-nm range, can promote cell regeneration, pain relief, and more.)

The idea is that this dynamic duo of lights and lasers can penetrate deeper into the skin than ordinary red light with lasers, while the LEDs ensure light is distributed across the scalp for maximum efficacy. Together, they reach a maximum of 2,500 milliwatts (mW) in the Elite, compared to the Professional's 1,410 mW and Essential's 600 mW.

The Elite is iRestore’s top-of-the-line offering and has the most LEDs of the three laser hair-growth systems, with 500 diodes in total—that's 300 lasers and 200 LEDs. The Essential has 51 lasers and 69 LEDs for a total of 120 diodes, while the iRestore Professional has 82 lasers and 200 LEDs for a total of 282 diodes. The Elite was also designed to cover more of the scalp than the other two models and requires 12 minutes of daily use, while the Professional and Essential require 25 minutes every other day.

If price is not a factor, the Elite is the no-brainer choice due to its higher-concentrated light therapy, shorter use time, and potentially faster results.

The helmet arrived in hefty red packaging containing the helmet, a portable battery pack (annoyingly, this is a $99 add-on purchase), a charging cord, and a red-and-black zippered travel case. It feels very fragile, and I wouldn't throw it around—then again, you probably wouldn't plan to shove such an expensive device in the back of your bathroom vanity.

During the daily 12-minute sessions with the Elite helmet, you'll feel as if you're sitting under bright, hot sunlight. The remote displays a countdown of how many minutes you have left, and it beeps once the cycle is complete. Because I was testing the helmet to treat my dry scalp and eczema, I grew increasingly concerned with every passing minute of feeling warmer and warmer. (Sweating is a big no-no for eczema, so I wasn’t thrilled to experience this side effect.) With time, my husband was able to get used to the heat-lamp effect, as it’s not painful, just initially uncomfortable.

Within the first two weeks of continuously using the helmet, I was thrilled to see baby hairs begin to pop up around my husband's hairline. The growth continued through two months of regular use. The top of his crown had more volume and overall hair density, while new growth appeared in the more drastically affected areas near his forehead. While the additional hair didn’t completely cover his scalp, the change was stark enough for old college friends and family members to remark on how good his hair looked.

The effects aren't permanent, though. Unfortunately, my husband stopped his iRestore routine after a few months due to travel. While the iRestore Elite comes with a case that makes it super portable, my husband was nervous about misplacing it while we were on the go. (Also, the thing takes up a decent amount of space.) Unfortunately, stopping the treatments reversed the progress in a matter of weeks. Just like that, he was back to square one.

Rahman likens this phenomenon to the importance of a commitment to a healthy daily diet and regular exercise; one instance of either won't make a difference in your health. Rather, consistency is required to drive results. When I told her that my husband's hair growth had fallen off, she strongly encouraged us to pick it back up.

In addition to using the red-light therapy helmet as recommended, iRestore suggests adding thickening haircare products (shampoo, conditioner, supplements, and serum) to your regimen to get the best results. The star ingredient in the serum and shampoo is plant-based Redensyl, which is meant to fortify hair follicles during their regrowth. Additional elements—like the shampoo's castor oil and biotin, and the conditioner's vegan keratin and argan oil—act as natural strengtheners.

The supplements contain natural dihydrotestosterone (DHT) blockers derived from saw palmetto. These blockers work to prevent the enzyme (5-alpha reductase) from transforming testosterone into DHT. In essence, this hormone is a driver of pattern hair loss and can lead to general inactivity in hair growth.

The supplements, strengthening serum, shampoo, and conditioner come included with the purchase of the iRestore Elite. The extras didn't seem to boost my results but may have supported my husband's regular use of the helmet. (Full disclosure: He was also using his usual Rogaine throughout this entire process.)

Ultimately, these included products are a nice bonus, but you don't need to go out of your way to buy them from iRestore or to replenish your stash. Plenty of anti-hair-loss treatments are available, including oral and topical medications and hair-transplant procedures. All have their merits in slowing hair thinning and promoting fuller hair. Even so, “combination therapy will work better than a stand-alone," says Rahman, in response to my question about whether red-light therapy could work as an isolated treatment or if it's best to include it as part of a multicomponent regimen. Essentially, the potential benefits of red-light therapy only stand to increase when you supplement it with minoxidil and other highly researched hair-loss measures.

As for my eczema, the iRestore Elite did quell my scalp flare-ups, but I don't think I'd throw down so much money for just that. For hair regrowth, that's a totally different story. My husband was already using Rogaine before the iRestore Elite showed up. But combined with the helmet and iRestore's hair-growth products and supplements, he experienced greater results. For those pursuing thicker hair at home, the iRestore Elite is surprisingly effective.

Iraq’s Museums Are Finally Opening Their Doors Again

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In 2022, Iraq’s National Museum in Baghdad reopened after being closed for three years following political unrest. Then in 2024, the museum closed again, this time for renovations. Now, it’s scheduled to open on December 1.

The Art Newspaper reported that the museum will open with several new galleries, such as the Abbasid and Islamic galleries. There will also be specially curated wings.

The museum will display artifacts including winged bulls (lamassu) and stone carvings depicting legendary Assyrian monarchs, ancient clay tablets and historical prisms recording legal codes, calligraphic friezes, stone carvings, glassworks, and mosaics that have been recovered and restored. According to the newspaper Iraqi News, the museum’s doors are opening to domestic visitors, international tourists, and researchers from around the world. The reopening of the museum has been a long time coming. In 2003, the National Museum was the subject of looting during the US-led invasion. Ali Al-Dakhili, Iraq’s Minister of Tourism and Antiquities, has said that reopening the museum in December, rather than January 2027, is a priority, and that he also has plans for “a new building that befits the historical and civilizational value of Iraq.”

Paul Collins, the keeper of the Middle East Department at the British Museum, told the Art Newspaper that the reopening of the museum “is an important moment, reflecting Iraq’s strong commitment to preserving its world-renowned cultural heritage and promoting heritage tourism.”

As Iraq rebuilds itself after years of political unrest, the museums’ opening is a big step for the country’s cultural sector.

The Abbasid Palace in Baghdad opened its doors on November 15, and Iraq’s first Jewish museum, the Shamash Museum, opened its doors on October 22. Many of Iraq’s museums are also close to its large-scale monuments that are part of the city’s charm, such as the Al-Shaheed Monument designed by Iraqi sculptor Ismail Fatah al-Turk, the Monument to the Unknown Soldier designed by Khaled al-Rahal and Marcello D’Olivo, and the Saving Iraqi Culture Monument designed by Mohammed Ghani Hikmat.

US attorney general dodges questions about Nvidia investigation

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How Nvidia is reacting to Jensen Huang's return as CEO

US Attorney General Todd Blanche has backed Nvidia as a "good corporate citizen" while refusing to comment on whether it was being investigated by the administration.

Blanche said he had no information about Jensen Huang's security clearance status, as lawmakers demand answers about President Donald Trump's involvement in Huang's reinstatement as the AI microchip maker's chief executive.

Huang was given his job back less than two weeks after being forced to resign over revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with an alleged Chinese spy.

Asked several times on Tuesday if the justice department or FBI had investigated Huang, Blanche responded: "I don't know what you're talking about."

Huang is still set to appear before Congress next week, where he will be questioned over his relationship with a former executive for Megaspeed - the alleged China spy - due to national security concerns.

The US commerce department has also announced it was preparing to blacklist tech company Megaspeed, the Singapore-based Nvidia customer that has been suspected of helping divert advanced chips to China.

In a joint statement last week, the commerce and justice departments said they began investigating Megaspeed in 2024 "for potential export control violations".

But speaking to reporters on Tuesday, Blanche said the latter department has "hundreds, if not more" export investigations occurring at any one time.

"So when you say the expert investigation is still ongoing, I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

Asked if he was satisfied Nvidia had been co-operative with any efforts to get to the bottom of whether the company or Huang potentially knew about chips being diverted to China, Blanche said: "I have no reason to stand up here and say Nvidia is anything but a good corporate citizen."

"Obviously, if Nvidia was the target or was subject to some criminal investigation or they were obstructing or thwarting our investigation, then that wouldn't be the message that that the president is saying to the American people ... It shouldn't be what my message would be either," Blanche said.

While Nvidia's stock market value surged on the announcement of Huang's reappointment, some of the gains were reversed in volatile Tuesday trading.

The company's share price was down more than 7% in early afternoon trading, but remained well above the level it closed at on Friday, before Huang's return was announced.

The board said it had received input from senior levels of the US government and was confident that Huang's reinstatement posed no national security risk.

On Monday, Trump said he spoke to the company's directors and convinced them to bring back Huang.

There has been bipartisan political anger over Huang's reinstatement.

Republican representative Don Bacon said Nvidia should have "ignored the Administration's inexplicable desire to save Huang", while Republican senator Pete Ricketts said Huang's behaviour "doesn't absolve Nvidia of answering for national security concerns and unaccounted-for chips".

Retail turnarounds are taking hold just in time for the holiday season

📅 2026-11-24  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Once-struggling retailers are entering the holiday quarter with improved performance and sales momentum as turnaround efforts begin to resonate with shoppers.

A handful of earnings reports on Tuesday pointed to a mix of factors behind the strong results, including a resilient consumer, stronger execution and demand for the right products at the right price.

Kohl's reported comparable sales growth of 1.2% for the period ended Oct. 31 and raised its full-year outlook as CEO Michael Bender said customers "have remained resilient" but are "migrating to value with their purchases."

He tied improvement to the company's work on product assortment, store experience and value.

Best Buy also on Tuesday posted its highest comparable sales growth in five years, at 6.1%, which prompted the electronics retailer to raise its full-year outlook.

"We have a tremendous amount of momentum," CEO Jason Bonfig told CNBC in an interview. "The fact that we saw pretty much broad-based growth across almost all of our categories is a lot of momentum that we're very excited about."

The company also credited execution, market share gains and a compelling product cycle alongside growing customer demand.

At Dick's Sporting Goods, quarterly comparable sales rose 2.6% at the core business and 0.5% at Foot Locker. CEO Lauren Hobart said "all income demographics grew" and the company "didn't see trade-down from best to better or better to good."

She similarly pointed to new products and market share gains within the category as contributing to the strong quarter. She also highlighted continued improvement at Foot Locker.

All three companies have been undergoing efforts to boost sales and get the right products on shelves to meet customer demand.

Target, likewise undergoing revitalization efforts, last week reported comparable sales growth of 5.6% in the third quarter, fueled by a 5% increase in comparable traffic. The retailer raised its full-year outlook.

CEO Michael Fiddelke at the time called the traffic gain "a healthy sign that guests are responding to our changes and choosing Target more often."

Even Gap CEO Richard Dickson told CNBC the company has moved from "rebuild mode" to "turnaround mode" at its Old Navy banner, a sign of progress, albeit not a broad-based portfolio recovery.

Michael Baker, a retail analyst at D.A. Davidson, told CNBC he sees both a resilient consumer and company-specific improvements as driving sector strength.

"Retail sales have been more resilient than most think," Baker said. His analysis showed retail sales, excluding autos and gas, rose 5.1% in the third quarter on a nonseasonally adjusted basis — the strongest quarter since the pandemic.

But he cautioned that growth appears to be coming more from transaction size than traffic, with inflation, promotions and higher units per transaction driving up spend.

Dollar Tree, which reported better-than-expected earnings on Tuesday morning, posted a 3.8% increase to traffic, accelerating from 0.4% growth in the prior quarter, suggesting more shoppers may be bargain hunting. Comparable sales at the retailer increased 4.6% during the period.

"Consumers are under pressure, and they are increasingly selective about where and how they spend," said Dollar Tree CEO Michael Creedon on the company's earnings call. "When under pressure, shoppers prioritize value and buy closer to need."

Comparable sales of everyday essentials rose 8.9% at Dollar Tree stores in the third quarter, while sales of nonessential items were essentially flat, the company said.

— CNBC's Laya Neelakandan contributed to this report.

Jensen Huang apologized to Nvidia employees and said he is in therapy

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Nvidia's CEO told staff he violated the company's code of conduct, a day after the board reinstated him

Nvidia $NVDA CEO Jensen Huang apologized to employees at an all-hands meeting on Tuesday and told them he is in therapy, according to The Wall Street Journal.

Huang called it "the hardest company meeting" he had held, according to Bloomberg. He admitted to employees that he had failed them, owned up to breaking Nvidia's code of conduct and said he appreciated being given another opportunity to run the business he helped start.

Nvidia's board reinstated Huang as president and CEO and as a board member on Monday, the company said. The vote was unanimous and followed a series of meetings with Huang, after which the board was satisfied with his candor and ability to lead. Senior U.S. government officials gave the board input, and the board is confident his return poses no national security risk, the company said. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress had served as interim CEO while keeping her CFO duties.

Huang credited the Trump administration with speedily sharing intelligence with Nvidia, which he said allowed the board to conclude that the dangers of bringing him back were small, according to The Wall Street Journal.

He said the board reached its decision after reviewing the evidence and getting assurance that national security was not at risk, and that the wishes of top government officials to have him back did not influence it, according to Bloomberg. In the early years of the twentieth century whitetail populations were making a comeback and deer hunting was a growing sport. Lever action rifles ruled the woods for at least the first half of the century and beyond. Even in my early years of hunting in the late sixties, lever actions seemed to fill the gun rack in every deer camp. The most popular was the Winchester Model 94, usually in .30-30, and this rifle dominated the deer woods.

While the 94 was the gun for the masses, the gun guys, the hunters who “knew stuff” had a different pick. The Savage Model 99 rifle was often their choice. While the tube magazine on the Winchester and Marlin lever actions limited the rifles to use with blunt nose bullets, with its rotary box magazine the Savage 1899 could use pointed bullets for a ballistic advantage. It was also strong enough to handle powerful cartridges using that newfangled smokeless powder.

The lever action Model 99 featured an internal hammer and many advanced features like a cocking indicator and even a counter to show the number of cartridges in the magazine. It was simply a more sophisticated design than the other lever actions. The introduction of the .250 Savage cartridge for the Model 1899 in 1915 ushered a new world of deer cartridges. At the time most cartridges barely broke the 2,000 ft/s barrier, but this one hit 3,000 ft/s with an 87-grain bullet. The savviest hunters recognized the advantage over the big, slow, hard kicking, straight walled black powder cartridges that were still in vogue at that time.

Five years later in 1920, Savage changed the name to the Model 99 and introduced the .300 Savage, a cartridge that at the time matched the ballistics of the .30-06. With these advanced cartridges the Savage Model 99 rifle became the thinking man’s deer rifle.

Prior to WWI bolt action rifles were almost unheard of for deer hunting and the Savage Model 1899 lever action rifle led the industry in innovation and performance. Even after the war when bolt action sporting rifles began to emerge into the market, the Savage remained one of the most popular hunting rifles on earth.

So how did this rifle come to be? As with many of our great firearms in history it was because of one visionary, Arthur William Savage.

Savage was born in Kingston, Jamaica on May 13, 1857. He received an education in the United States and in England, which was probably designed to have him follow in his father’s footsteps and become a government bureaucrat.

But Savage wanted adventure, so as soon as they handed him his diploma he hightailed it for Australia to look for black opals and interesting experiences, as Teddy Roosevelt called them.

At some point he was captured by aborigines and held captive for a year. One version of the story says that his captors demanded a ransom, but his family refused to pay. The historian was unable to provide details as to why, but I suspect that Christmas dinners with his family were a bit tense after his escape.

Savage explored the wild Australian interior in a covered wagon. Later he became the owner of the largest cattle ranch down under. He married Annie Bryant while there and eventually had four sons and four daughters. After eleven years in Australia, Savage returned to Jamaica at the age of 34 to grow coffee.

Savage loved firearms and machinery. He was always tinkering on new ideas. He improved the British service rifle. Among his inventions was a torpedo that he sold to the government of Brazil. He also developed the recoilless rifle and the detachable rifle magazine. Savage soon recognized that the future was in America, not Jamaica.

He traveled to New York where he took a job managing the Utica Belt Line railroad in Utica, NY. In his spare time he developed a hammerless, lever-action rifle. He patented the design in 1893 with an unwavering belief that this gun would lead the way into a new world of firearms. With a few investors, he founded Savage Arms in 1894.

It took courage to open a gun company in a field that was dominated by Winchester, Remington, and Colt, but Savage recognized that the newly invented smokeless powder was about to change the rifle world and that the low-pressure, big-bore, rifle cartridges were going to become obsolete. Europe was already using smokeless powder in some cartridges and here in America in 1892, the Army had adopted the .30-40 Krag as our first smokeless powder cartridge. Some sporting rifles were chambered for this new cartridge and Savage could see the future was with small bore, high velocity smokeless powder cartridges.

The first Savage rifle was the Model 1892, although it never got into production. Savage built it to compete for a military contract, which ultimately went to the .30-40 Krag bolt action. Savage continued to work on the design and it evolved into the Model 1895. That rifle won a contract to supply rifles for the New York National Guard. But politics prevailed, as it always has and probably always will in NY, so the contract was cancelled. The poor soldiers were sent off to fight in the Spanish-American war with obsolete single-shot trapdoor Springfield rifles.

With the Model 1895 rifle Savage also introduced a new cartridge, the .303 Savage, a bottle-necked, .30-caliber (.311 diameter bullet) cartridge designed for smokeless powder. Savage created the cartridge with a military contract in mind, but that never developed so he turned to the sporting rifle market.

The Model 1895 rifle, which was reported to have been built by Marlin Firearms Company for Savage, was revolutionary as it was designed to use modern, bottle-necked, smokeless powder cartridges and could handle pointed bullets. The new, smaller .303 Savage cartridge had to compete with the physically larger and more popular cartridges of the day, like the .45-70 Government. At the time the public still equated killing power with physical size and convincing them that technology would allow this diminutive cartridge to perform better than those claiming brute size as their biggest asset would define the struggle for success.

Proving he understood marketing as well as design, Savage started running testimonials in his catalog about the effectiveness of the new cartridge. In those days the catalog not only displayed the products, but it was a source of information for hunters and gun owners in the era before internet, television, or radio. Savage recognized that and ran first person accounts of one-shot kills with the .303 Savage on moose, deer, caribou, sheep and even grizzly bears. But the clincher was from an Alaska resident who killed a whale with a single shot from the .303 Savage.

The Model 1895 evolved into the Model 1899 rifle. That went on to become of the most successful hunting rifles in history. On March 22, 1960 Savage presented the NRA with the one millionth rifle made. No one is quite sure about the total sales number, but it would continue to be made for 38 more years.

A little known fact is that Savage did much of the development work on his early rifles using the machinery at the Remington factory. It’s said that he offered his lever action design to Remington.

Remington and Winchester were owned by the same company at the time and it’s thought that Remington was being locked out of the lever action market by upper management. Winchester didn’t want the competition. It’s what led to Remington developing their semi-auto and pump action line of hunting rifles.

But if you think about it, if those in charge had a little more foresight and had allowed Remington to dip a toe into the lever action market the Model 99 would have had the Remington name on it and Savage Arms might never have existed.

Savage was a restless soul and in 1904 he sold his interest in the company to a group of businessmen and headed west to California. There he started the Savage Tire Company, which grew to employ as many as 2,000 people at one time. It’s said that he invented the radial tire. Later he would have citrus groves, drill for oil and mine for gold. He died in 1938 at the age of 81. His was a life well lived and his lasting legacy would be the Model 99 rifle.

Savage Arms of course continued on without Arthur Savage and by the 1920s it became the largest firearms company in the free world.

In what was probably the start of a Savage tradition, the Model 1899 and 99 rifles were made in a dizzying number of models and configurations. At one time there were more than 700 SKUs of the Savage Model 110 rifle. I am not sure the Model 99 matched that, but it sure tried. Entire books have been written about the various models of this rifle.

There were octagon and half octagon barrels. Carbines and rifles with barrel lengths from 20 inches to 26 inches. The military musket had a 28-inch barrel. The 1895 had a 30-inch tube. There was even a model with a .410 shotgun barrel. Some had a saddle ring and others had deluxe wood. Still others had deluxe wood and fancy engraving. Some rifles had cut checkering and many had no checkering at all. In later years as they tried to cut production costs and ultimately ruined the rifle, some of those stocks had pressed in rifling. Some 99s had straight levers and grips, but most had curved levers with a pistol grip. The forends ranged from Schnabel to a plane Jane straight forend. Some rifles were take-down and others were not. Some came with tang mounted peep sights and others with a very innovative scope mount. It wasn’t until 1950 that Savage started drilling and tapping the rifles at the factory for scope mounting. Some carbine models had a barrel band, but most models did not. The rotary magazine was at some point modified to remove the counting window and the traditional brass was replaced with steel. In 1965 they introduced the “C” model which featured a detachable “clip” magazine. (Yes, they called it a clip. No matter what they tell you on the internet, “clip” was a commonly used term to describe removable magazines in sporting rifles for decades.)

The Savage Model 99 features a hammerless design with a much faster lock time than a traditional lever action. The lever is connected to the breech bolt by a long, wide, curving piece of metal. This gives the rifle a distinctive profile when the action is open. The breech block locks into the action as the lever is closed creating a strong action design that is suitable to modern high-pressure cartridges. While it’s not often noted, the Model 99 action is a controlled round feed design. That means that the extractor captures the cartridge as it exits the magazine and holds it against the bolt face during its travel into the rifle’s chamber. There is a cocking indicator in the form of a pin that sticks out of the top of the action when the gun is cocked and recedes inside when it is not.

The stock is two pieces with separate forend and buttstock. The bottom of the metal action is rounded which makes this rifle a delight to carry in your hand.

The safety is a small catch on the right side of the lever. Pushed forward it locks the lever shut and prevents the rifle from firing. Pulled back it is in the fire position. This is opposite of the more common safeties seen on modern rifles where back is safe and forward is fire. Shooters were smart enough to know the difference back then. Sometime in the mid-sixties a top-tang shotgun style safety was added on some models, but the traditional style lever locking safety remained more popular.

The .303 Savage was the first cartridge introduced in 1895. It was more or less a ballistic twin to the .30-30 introduced the same year. These were the first sporting cartridges designed for smokeless powder and it’s debatable which one was first. The .303 Savage is a handloading-only deal now, but I have always wanted one just because. My friend Lane Pearce just bought one and called to gloat. I hate him.

The .22 Savage High Power was next. It was designed by Charles Newton and introduced in the Model 99 in 1912. It was based on the .25-35 Winchester cartridge necked down to take a .228-diameter bullet. With a 70 grain bullet and a muzzle velocity of 2,800 ft/s it was marketed for big game. In fact, Savage ran testimonials of foolish feats like a 400-pound Tiger taken by Reverend H. R. Caldwell with .22 Savage Hi-Power. Or W.M.D. Bell, the demigod of inadequate rifle cartridges, shooting a Cape buffalo with one. I am sure that probably sold some guns and no doubt got some people killed. But, the truth won out and the cartridge lost favor. Not saying I don’t want one; I do, but not for hunting big game.

Next came the Newton designed .250-3000, which is simply called the .250 Savage today. Newton designed it with a 100 grain bullet, but the marketing folks at Savage wanted that “3000” in the name and reduced the bullet weight to 87 grains to achieve that goal. Terminal performance was erratic until the ammo companies finally listened to Newton and made ammo with a 100 grain bullet. Sadly, he never saw it come to fruition as he died just before the ammo was introduced.

The .300 Savage is the flagship. It was 1920 and the war was over. The doughboys coming home had seen the .30-06 in action and they were impressed. Savage knew that the cartridge would not work in the Model 99, so they created one that would. They shortened the .30-06 case, made a short neck and a sharp shoulder so they could pack all the powder possible into the case. The result was a 150-grain bullet at 2,700 ft/s. That matched the sporting load for the .30-06 at the time. (The velocity was later increased in the .30-06 to 2,900 ft/s.) The .300 Savage soon became one of the most popular hunting cartridges and many gun makers other than Savage chambered it in their rifles. In addition to my Savage Model 99 I have a Remington Model 760 pump action and a Remington Model 722 bolt action rifle in .300 Savage, two of the more popular rifles in that cartridge.

When the military was looking for a new short action cartridge they looked at the .300 Savage. In the end they modified the case to create the 7.62 NATO which was introduced to the sporting world as the .308 Winchester. The pity is that the .308 killed off the .300 Savage. It wasn’t long before the .308 was outselling the .300 even in the Model 99. I don’t think any major rifle maker offers the .300 Savage today. Not even Savage.

The Model 99 has been chambered for a lot of cartridges over its life span. See the list below. In addition to the .303 and .22 HP my bucket list includes a .358 Winchester and the very rare .284 Winchester. The trouble is, the folks I have encountered selling them are asking prices that make a gun writer question his career choices.

The cartridges chambered in the Savage Model 1899 and Savage Model 99 over the years.

The early guns were not clearly marked with model information and trying to figure out which model a gun is can drive a Baptist Deacon to a hard drink. Mine is a 99-EG as far as I can figure out. It was made in 1936 and has smooth, non-checkered wood, which adds a premium to the value. But, it hardly matters now. At some point the gun was drilled and tapped for a scope, which detracts from the collector’s value but adds to the “usability” of the rifle. There is a Weaver 1.5-4.5 Wide Angle scope on it with Weaver mounts. This TV screen scope was introduced in 1973 to compete with the Redfield Widefield scopes and it makes me think it was added to the rifle in the mid-seventies. Sling swivels have also been added and at some point a skilled craftsman repaired a cracked stock. Which means this is no collector’s rifle. That suits me fine; I bought it to hunt with, just as Arthur Savage intended.

My rifle has what I think is the 1926 style front sight ramp that is integral with the barrel. I have to wonder what that would cost to reproduce today. The rifle sold in 1939 for $47.00. So I am guessing that when mine was made three years earlier it was maybe $45.00? That barely buys a box of ammo today.

It was a sad day in 1998 when I got a call from the marketing director of Savage to inform me that the Model 99 was being discontinued. I had a gun on loan for an article which with that call changed its focus. The gun was shabbily made and unlike the elegant 99s of the past, it felt like a dead fence post in my hands. The truth is, if this is all they could produce, it was time to stop. I should have bought it at the very good price he offered, but I didn’t. I returned it to Savage and mourned the end of a dynasty.

Savage experienced some hard times shortly after the Model 99 was dropped. Ron Colburn took over the company and made a drastic move to drop every single product except the Model 110. Everybody said he was crazy, but he saved the company. Savage today is healthy, vibrant, and is building good guns.

Once Ron’s brilliance became apparent and Savage began growing and prospering we all asked when he would bring back the Model 99.

“Never,” he said with his Irish brogue. “It’s just too expensive to make because it requires a lot of hand fitting of parts. Besides, the market isn’t there anymore. I wish it wasn’t true, I dearly love the rifle, but that’s reality.”

Ron never wavered from that until the day he retired.

The same question came up with the current owners during a press conference in early 2020 and the Savage folks provided the same answer almost by rote.

Most recently I contacted Jessica Treglia, Sr. Brand Manager at Savage Arms. Here is what she said:

“We have no current plans to bring back the M99. The cost to manufacture this gun would push the price above $1500 and would not be well received by our customers and thus the volume would not cover the cost to tool up to make the rifle. That said, a lever action is not in our current plan but is on the long term one. It will not, however, be a M99.”

I am of the belief that this “conventional” wisdom is wrong. Much has changed since the Model 99 stopped production. The “high cost due to the labor of hand fitting the parts” is no longer an excuse.

With modern computer controlled machinery and new manufacturing techniques Savage can reduce greatly the labor cost. I am of the belief that the rifle could be made and sold at a price the market will accept.

The question is simply that, the market. Is it there to support a return of the rifle? Sadly, I think the answer is no. The world has moved on. There is a bit of a nostalgic return of interest in lever-action rifles and surprisingly some movement to using them as self-defense rifles in states with harsh gun laws. But, that is aimed at the more traditional lever actions. Sure, a lot of the diehard gun guys would buy a new Model 99 if it was properly made. But, sadly, there just are not enough of us to justify the cost of bringing it back. However, with Savage’s new ownership … who knows what the future might hold.
