The collection of characters from The Little Mermaid is growing, with Ariel’s beloved fish friends Flounder and Sebastian joining the village in Disney Dreamlight Valley. The process for unlocking them looks vastly different from all other villagers, though, and can be tricky to navigate.

If you’re hoping to get every character available to find and meet around the valley, figuring out what needs to be done to recruit these two is essential. Here’s how to unlock Flounder and Sebastian in Disney Dreamlight Valley.

Flounder and Sebastian can be unlocked by purchasing either the Deluxe Edition or the Ultimate Edition of The Keepsake Sea in DDV. These two Adventure Companions are exclusive content locked behind these special variants of the Expansion Pass, which means you must buy one of the two to get them.

With either of these versions purchased, you’ll then be able to unlock Flounder and Sebastian in DDV. These two are likely locked behind a few quests, probably ones that require you to first unlock Ariel and progress through part of The Keepsake Sea’s storyline to learn about Adventure Companions. Most other DLCs have had similar exclusivity systems for special game editions, like Sven as an exclusive mount with the Deluxe Edition of Wishblossom Ranch.

All items included in special editions have never been available in any other way. This system has been in place for years, so it’s unlikely to change in the future. Tesla Just Killed Its Cheapest All-Wheel-Drive Model Y

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

Getting an all-wheel-drive Tesla Model Y just got a lot more expensive after the American company removed the entry-level version of the crossover from its online configurator.

The Model Y All-Wheel Drive, which used to cost roughly $42,000, is nowhere to be found on the automaker’s website, which means the most affordable dual-motor Model Y is now the Premium AWD trim that starts at $51,630.

Introduced in February, the now-defunct Model Y AWD came hot on the heels of the budget-oriented Model 3 Standard and Model Y Standard that debuted in October 2025 as more affordable, rear-wheel-drive-only versions of the company’s best-selling EVs. When the bare-bones Model Y AWD was introduced, Tesla also dropped the “Standard” badge from its lineup, though the other trims are still called “Premium” and “Performance”.

Powered by the same battery as the single-motor Model Y RWD, the discontinued Model Y AWD had the shortest claimed range in the Model Y lineup, with an EPA-estimated 294 miles on a full charge—54 miles less than the most affordable rear-driven crossover in Tesla’s lineup currently.

With two electric motors providing motivation, the dropped Model Y AWD could sprint from zero to 60 miles per hour in 4.6 seconds, 2.2 seconds faster than its single-motor counterpart, despite weighing roughly 200 pounds more.

The cheapest Model Y in Tesla’s lineup remains the basic RWD version, which starts at $41,630. Compared to the better-equipped and more expensive trims, the entry-level crossover comes with redesigned front and rear lights, passive shock absorbers, a lower 225-kilowatt maximum charging power, fewer speakers for the audio system, and seats trimmed in vegan leather and cloth. There’s no FM radio, no rear screen, no ventilated seats, and only the front seats are heated. Furthermore, while the glass roof is still installed, the base trim has a headliner that covers it entirely.

The discontinuation of the Tesla Model Y All-Wheel Drive is just the latest casualty in the company’s quest to slim down its passenger-vehicle lineup. Earlier this year, the Model S and Model X were axed, and their production line has been ripped out of the factory to make room for robots. More recently, Tesla CEO Elon Musk announced that the Cybertruck would be retired next year. Until that happens, the only trim remaining is the top Cyberbeast, which starts at $109,990.

There are no plans to replace the Model S and Model X, and the Cybercab two-seater will take over the Cybertruck’s floor space.

Beond Airlines Suspends Flights Again: Will They Resume In December?

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Back in April 2026, we saw Beond Airlines suspend all scheduled operations for the summer, as the airline claimed it would resume flights as of October. Then in October, it announced it would return in December. Is that actually happening now, or what exactly is going on?

Beond Airlines launched operations in 2023, and it brands itself as the world’s first premium leisure airline. The company is headquartered in Dubai, but primarily operates flights to and from the Maldives. I reviewed the airline in 2024, and the onboard product was super pleasant. The airline currently has a fleet of two aircraft, including one A319 and one A321.

Anyway, in mid-April 2026, the company announced that it would suspend its scheduled operations for the entire summer. The airline promised to resume flights in October, for the winter season.

Then in October, the airline announced that it would suspend operations through November 30, 2026, meaning it will resume flights as of December 1, 2026. Interestingly, the airline has promised to provide a schedule update by November 12, 2026, but that date has now come and gone, and there was no update.

So I guess it remains to be seen if we actually see a resumption of flights as of December, or if the timeline will get pushed back again. The company does have a section on its travel updates page with FAQs, and one of the questions is “will the dates change again?” The answer is “we chose dates we are confident in,” but also notes that “if anything changes, you will hear from us first.”

Beond Airlines does have its “commitment” to customers when plans change, which includes the ability to change destinations or travel dates, receive a travel credit valid for 18 months, or to receive a full refund. A change of destinations or dates, plus a travel credit, isn’t worth a whole lot for an airline that keeps pushing back its relaunch.

Interestingly, but perhaps not surprisingly, earlier this year the airline deleted its customer promise webpage, which used to guarantee rebooking on another airline in business class in the event a flight is canceled, or else the airline would offer a free flight. That page is now gone, and there’s no mention of rebooking on another airline.

So the airline will “support you every step of the way,” unless you actually want to take the vacation you booked, in which case you’re out of luck (which is less than ideal, given that many Maldives properties have very strict cancelation policies).

Saying Overwatch 2 has had a rocky road since its 2019 reveal would be an understatement, and former Overwatch director Jeff Kaplan isn't a fan of how the game was first announced.

Speaking to Gaming World Interviews, Kaplan didn't mince words about the BlizzCon 2019 opening ceremony, where Overwatch 2 was shown for the first time, as Blizzard promised numerous features it either walked back or didn't deliver.

Kaplan said that from 2018 until his departure, he experienced constant friction with Blizzard's executive leadership over game direction, scheduling, and core game mechanics. This led him to confront leadership about the lack of a clear vision or business model for Overwatch 2, and to question the wisdom of announcing Overwatch 2 at the same time as Diablo 4.

"I got in a huge fight with, at the time, it wasn't Mike [Morhaime]. I loved Mike, who was the president of Blizzard, and another president of Blizzard, my executive producer, over announcing Overwatch 2. I went into their office, I’m like, 'What are we doing? According to Kaplan, the new executives "had a lot to prove" when they joined Blizzard. "They wanted to prove that 'Blizzard's back and Blizzard still has it.' And I'm like, 'You guys are f***ing idiots,' you know? I don’t know how else to put it, but after three years of just getting worn down and not agreeing with any of the decisions that were being made on the franchise and not feeling like I had agency anymore--like before it used to be like I'm the game director I get to decide the direction the game goes. And starting in 2018, I lost that, and after three years of fighting for it back, I realized this company belongs to these corporate people now, the Blizzard executive team, the Activision team."

Kaplan left Blizzard in 2021, saying Activision Blizzard’s financial arm told him that if Overwatch didn't meet certain financial goals, 1,000 people at the company would be laid off and the blame would fall on him. Specialized’s Tarmac SL9 S-Level is built to wreck your Zone 2 ride

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Specialized’s second-fastest bike may be the smart buy.

I love a good Zone 2 ride. There’s nothing like getting on the bike, settling into a groove, and letting the miles (and hours) flow by. But sometimes when I hopped on the Specialized Tarmac SL9 S-Level, Zone 2 would turn into a tempo ride. It was like having a little imp—the stereotype dressed in red with the pitchfork—on my left shoulder whispering into my ear: “Go fast. This bike was built to go fast. Speed is good. You can do a Zone 2 ride tomorrow.” Normally, I’d count on the good imp—the dude with the harp and wings—on my right shoulder to restrain these urges. But the Tarmac is so seductive that he would just shrug and say “he’s got a point.” And in a moment of weakness, I would drop the hammer and head into the red, just because the SL9 makes that so rewarding.

The Tarmac SL9 is Specialized’s aero road bike, with the S-Level sitting just below the S-Works in Specialized’s top tier of carbon, aero road bikes, where it replaced the Tarmac SL8 Pro. After it got an update this year, I spent several weeks riding it, putting nearly 1,000 miles on it

Specialized offers the Tarmac SL9 in four models, all available with SRAM and Shimano drivetrains (Ars tested the SRAM version). Notably, you don’t need to buy the top-of-the-line bike to get the top-of-the-line groupset. Here’s the breakdown of the Tarmac SL9 lineup (note that the Shimano versions of the Expert is 20 g lighter and Comp is 70 grams heavier than the SRAM versions):

The $10,500 S-Level offers the top-of-the-line SRAM Red groupset and integrated cockpit, just like the $14,000 S-Works. But the frameset, wheelset, and saddle differ. The major difference is the frame: the S-Level gets the same carbon-fiber FACT 10r frame as the Expert and Comp. At 822 g for a 56 cm frame, the FACT 10r is 135 g heavier than the FACT 12r used on the S-Works. Despite the weight difference, Specialized says the FACT 10r offers the same stiffness and comfort characteristics as its lighter sibling. Along with the frame, different wheelsets and saddles make the S-Level 500 g heavier than the S-Works. With bottle cages, a Garmin mount, and my preferred Shimano XTR XC PD-M9200 MTB pedals, the S-Level I tested weighed in at 7.98 kg. By way of comparison, my own road bike—a Gen 8 Trek Madone—weighs in at 7.71 kg.

I rode the S-Level all around the Chicago suburbs as well as in central Illinois. Unlike the Diverge 4 Pro, I wasn’t able to test its climbing ability in the Rocky Mountains. All we’ve got in most of Illinois are short, punchy climbs … and a lot of long flats. I rode The S-Level on everything from freshly laid asphalt to freshly laid chipseal, including a couple of 60-mile-plus rides on the country roads downstate.

Visually, the S-Level is striking. The blue-on-white with gray striping is one of Specialized’s better-looking colorways. And it looks aerodynamic. Indeed, Specialized says the Tarmac SL9 is the most aerodynamic road bike it has ever made. Notable changes from the previous-generation Tarmac SL8 to SL9 involved tweaking the head tube, rerouting the rear hydraulic brake hose to accommodate the narrower head tube, and moving to a dropped-down tube for improved aerodynamics. But the most visually striking change is the Win Fin. As the seat tube rises from the bottom bracket, it deviates from the vertical to follow the curve of the wheel as it approaches the seat stays. When riding with just a single water bottle on the downtube—common in pro races where a full bottle is just a team car away—Specialized claims Win Fin saves riders half a watt.

The S-Level I tested came with Specialized’s 30 mm Specialized Cotton TLR tires—used by Red Bull-BORA-hansgrohe on the UCI World Tour—in a tubeless setup. According to Bicycle Rolling Resistance, the Cotton TLR outperforms the much-loved Continental GP 5000 S TR tires when it comes to rolling resistance (at 28 mm). Using Silca’s tire pressure calculator, I rode with the tires inflated to 73 psi in the front and 75 psi in the rear, a departure from the 62/64 setting on my 32 mm Contis—and there is a slight, but noticeable difference in ride comfort between the S-Level and my own road bike. Mounted on the Roval Rapide CL III carbon wheels with 21 mm internal width, the Cotton TLR tires measured 30.2 mm wide (my 32 mm Contis are a hair over 33 mm).

On every surface shy of brand-new chipseal, the Tarmac felt comfortable and balanced, with the bike absorbing some of the punishment meted out by uneven pavement. Fresh chipseal is another matter entirely, as it feels more like gravel, which felt jarring on the SL9 S-Level. What was particularly impressive about the SL9 is how great it feels on long rides. Some manufacturers are skilled at building aero bikes that feel great on hours-long rides, and the Tarmac is just as good as the Trek Madone SLR 9 AXS Gen 8 I reviewed in this regard.

When you’re ready to drop the hammer, the S-Level is your willing accomplice. It’s twitchy in all of the good ways, with instantaneous response to the rider’s effort. The SRAM Red AXS groupset makes shifting buttery smooth (and the bonus buttons on the brake hoods allow the rider to change screens on the bike computer without releasing the brake hoods), and the drivetrain (50t/37t chainrings and a 10t-36t cassette) offers gear ratios for every type of terrain.

The first few seconds of a climb on the Tarmac SL9 S-Level feel like they come for free. Whether I stayed seated or cranked out watts in an upright position, there was no lag or flex as the bike snapped forward. Descents were amazing, and cornering on the S-Level is sublime—I felt balanced and in control at all times.

There are a few nits to pick. The reach to the brakes in the drops felt excessive on my first couple of rides, and I had to adjust them slightly to brake easily on descents. Also, I would prefer shorter cranks on the Tarmac. The trend among professional riders has been toward shorter cranks because it makes holding an aero position easier and reduces hip and knee flexion. To its credit, Specialized reduced the crank length from 172.5 mm on the 56 cm SL8 to 170 mm on the same-sized SL9, but I run 165 mm cranks on my road bikes and noticed the difference on the S-Level. One demographic that buys expensive road bikes is middle-aged dudes with empty nests and disposable incomes, and they are the ones most likely to appreciate shorter cranks.

The 3D-printed Power Pro Mirror is a fine saddle, but there’s a reason I ride with a Specialized S-Works Power with Mirror on my Trek—it’s more comfortable and offers noticeably more padding around the cutout. And if I owned the S-Level, I’d move to 32 mm tires as soon as I wore out the 30s for better compliance.

Specialized brags that the Tarmac SL9 S-Works is the fastest bike ever made, and it claims the S-Level is the second-fastest bike ever made. I can’t speak to that claim, but the SL9 S-Level checks all of the boxes for an aerodynamic road bike. It’s lightweight, it’s fast, it’s responsive, and it’s great on rollers and punchy climbs. And it’s nice to get a SRAM Red groupset without springing for the absolute top-of-the-line model. $10,500 is a hefty sum for a road bike, but I’d be a lot more willing to forego the weight savings from the lighter components (frame, wheels, seatpost, bottom bracket, and bearings) of the S-Works—and spend $3,500 less. The real selling point, though, is how often it made me want to go harder just for the fun of it. That little imp has a point: Zone 2 can wait until tomorrow.

Humanoid Robots Are Coming to Factories. Who Is Responsible When They Fail?

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A robot may successfully complete a performance test and yet become a safety hazard if it faces an unexpected obstacle, a change in how it behaves due to a software update, or a real worker. The question is not whether humanoid robots can perform a task anymore, but how they can do it. Whether they can do it safely or not—and who is responsible if something goes wrong.

Agility Robotics and FORT Robotics announced a partnership in October to enhance the safety architecture of Agility’s Digit 5 humanoid robot. They have planned their approach by incorporating onboard safety systems and external interfaces, communications, and manual override. The development marks a significant shift: Robots have to be safe in human workplaces, and that means more than just the robot itself.

However, technology is not enough to answer the question of accountability. When a robot makes a mistake based on a faulty component misidentified by an AI system, the manufacturer, the software developer, the system integrator and the employer who is using the system could be held liable.

Traditional industrial robots are typically found in enclosed work cells, where they are isolated from workers by barriers and safety devices. Humanoid robots pose another challenge: they are supposed to navigate through environments constructed for humans, perform different tasks, and adapt to different environments.

That flexibility creates new risks. If a false detection occurs, it may lead to an unnecessary movement. A software update could alter a robot’s behavior. Changes in the factory layout may create hazards that were not present in previous tests.

Dana R. Wolfe, Vice President and Head of Industry, GFT Americas, told Unite AI to prevent unsafe interventions, multiple layers of protection are necessary, such as deterministic software guardrails, factory-specific confidence thresholds, sensor fusion and staged validation prior to deployment.

There is no way to completely avoid false-positive results, Wolfe said. Rather, manufacturers need to make sure that mistakes are not translated into unsafe physical movements.

Confidence thresholds and human authority should be part of the system’s safety architecture, as should be hardware interlocks, said Wolfe.

Current industrial robotics standards offer a valuable foundation, but are not necessarily the solution to all the problems presented by mobile humanoids.

The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) has published safety requirements for industrial robots, and ISO 10218-2 covers the integration of robots into complete systems.

These standards, however, do not address all applications or hazards of mobile robots working with humans.

Independent testing, scenario-based validation and phased workplace pilots are particularly important as industry practices evolve, said Wolfe.

Safety evaluations must also be repeated if changes in the AI model, software, movement restrictions, tasks or factory environment may render previous safety evaluations invalid.

After a major change, a robot that was safe in one set of conditions may not be safe in the new set of conditions.

It is impractical to monitor every decision an AI system makes, but when people are not involved in critical decisions, another challenge arises: accountability becomes more difficult to prove.

Adrian Figueroa Ramos, co-founder and CEO of Flexio and Routina, said that the focus of oversight should be on decisions that have the most uncertainty or risk.

“In today’s AI world, the volume of outputs is more than a human can process. Reviewing everything makes the human the bottleneck, but human judgment is still necessary for high-risk or low-confidence decisions,” he told Unite AI.

This is particularly true for physical AI. Human oversight should include named individuals with the power to override a system and ensure that decisions are documented and checked and not just click an approval button.

The accountability should be shared based on the control of each party over the system.

For instance, manufacturers are responsible for creating and documenting safety measures. Changes that may impact perception or movement should be tested by software developers.

The whole system needs to be validated in the intended environment by the integrator. Employers are responsible to train workers, ensure safeguards are in place and procedures are established for stopping unsafe operations.

Cybersecurity also matters. Operational technology can be vulnerable to sensor, software and external system issues connected to robots. A security failure that impacts physical behavior is a workplace safety problem, as well as an IT problem.

Humanoid robots are expected to become more flexible and productive in factories in the future. However, adoption will not be based solely on performance measures. There is a need for clear responsibility, independent validation and safeguards that are sustained throughout the working life of a robot.

The question is, can a robot make mistakes? It is, if the individuals who are using it have created a system that will hold them accountable for those errors.

Why Agentic AI Projects Get Canceled, and What an Eight-Week Pilot Gets Right

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A customer asks for a refund, and the agent finds the order in the CRM, checks the returns policy and drafts a reply in under a minute. Then it stalls, because the refund approval sits in a finance system it was never connected to and the customer’s address doesn’t match between two databases. Gaps of this kind rarely surface in a demo, yet they decide how much an agent costs to run and whether anyone can trust it, which puts the bigger risk in agentic AI inside the business the agent is dropped into rather than inside the model.

Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be canceled by the end of 2027, pointing to rising costs, unclear business value and weak risk controls. Read closely, that forecast is about how these projects get approved on the strength of a demo, with little view of what the work costs today or who owns the outcome.

The question I’d put to any leadership team is whether the business can support this agent doing the work every day, at a cost and level of risk it can live with. Answering it means looking hard at what I call the work after automation, meaning everything people still have to do once the agent has finished its part, and an eight-week pilot is a practical way to measure it before committing to a full rollout.

An agent rarely works on its own, since even a simple-looking task touches customer records, a couple of business applications and usually an approval step, and every connection limits what the agent can see and do. Where records are inconsistent or nobody is quite sure who signs off, the agent inherits the confusion along with the task.

Routine cases are seldom the problem; exceptions are, because that’s where operating costs pile up, and they’re precisely the part a demo is built to avoid. Deloitte’s 2026 research found that only 5% of organizations rate their business processes as highly prepared for AI agents, a number more telling than any model benchmark because it suggests the AI is ready well before most processes are.

Timing how fast an agent produces an output is the easiest way to overstate its value, since the figure that matters is how much effort the finished task takes. Say a request takes an employee 20 minutes to process, and the agent gets the first pass down to two, which looks like an 18-minute saving right up until someone spends 15 minutes checking the result and fixing errors. Three minutes saved is a thin return once the cost of running the agent goes on the other side of the ledger.

McKinsey’s analysis of a banking customer-service agent shows the same pattern at scale: tokens made up 20–25% of variable operating costs and human oversight 70–75%, so most of the money sat outside the model. Without a baseline in place before anyone writes code, a pilot can show plenty of technical progress and still leave the investment case untested. At a minimum, the business owner needs a clear answer on each of these points:

Adoption usually gets filed under change management, although in my experience it’s settled much earlier, by where the agent sits in the workflow. A global professional services company had built agents for support and compliance work, and its support agent could pull information from several internal systems, but people had to open a separate application to use it and mostly didn’t bother. Once the same workflow was moved into the existing support platform, it appeared inside the ticket associates were already working on, and a compliance screening agent went the same way, built into Salesforce so that it ran whenever a case was created.

Nothing about the agents themselves changed; their position in the workflow did. Associates already had a reason to open a ticket or create a case, so meeting them at that moment asked nothing extra of them. Placement is therefore an architecture decision as much as an adoption one, because the agent needs context where the work happens, and its output has to feed the next step without someone copying it across by hand.

An agent that can only read information carries one kind of risk, and one that can change records or email customers carries another entirely, so the controls have to follow what it’s actually allowed to do. Excessive permissions and leaking sensitive data are the familiar risks, but prompt injection deserves the closest attention: instructions hidden in a document or an email that the agent picks up while doing a perfectly legitimate job.

Gartner’s May 2026 guidance on agent governance breaks autonomy into four levels, which gives a practical way to decide how tight the controls need to be:

At every level, an agent needs its own identity, permissions limited to its task and a record of each action it takes. Organizations also need rules for how data is handled and retained, clarity on what reaches outside model providers, and someone with the authority to pause the agent and pull its access the moment something looks wrong.

Each of these controls carries a running cost, since wider permissions, extra monitoring and frequent approvals all add up over time. The right level of autonomy is therefore an economic decision as well as a security one, and it sits wherever the agent delivers a worthwhile outcome at a risk the business can accept.

Plenty of teams start with the technology and go looking for a problem when the better starting point is a process whose cost or delays are already obvious to the people doing the work.

A global expert-network company we worked with fit that description: its team was matching survey requests to experts across three systems that didn’t talk to each other, checking profiles, past participation and compliance by hand for every match. We built an agent that combined semantic search with that participation history and the compliance rules, and in production mismatch tickets dropped 80%, mapping accuracy rose 40% and the team got back 22% of its bandwidth.

The project worked because the problem was specific, with scattered information, manual stitching and match quality that could be measured before and after. Specificity also helps answer whether a process needs an agent at all. Work governed by fixed rules is usually cheaper to handle with conventional automation, and work that mostly involves finding and summarizing information is often well served by an AI assistant, while agents earn their extra cost and risk when the job means reading context, choosing between permitted actions and chaining several steps toward an outcome.

The whole pilot hangs on one commercial question: can this agent improve the process enough to justify running it at scale? Eight weeks gives enough room to test the business case, the technology and the organization’s ability to manage what it builds.

Start by documenting the process as it runs today, from volume and completion time to error rates, rework and cost. Note which systems are involved, who owns each stage and which exceptions need a human, then get the business owner and the technical team to agree on the one outcome the agent is supposed to improve. Decision point: keep going once the problem, the baseline, the owner and the success measures are clear.

This is where the agent’s limits get set, covering its identity, permissions, data access, approved tools and degree of autonomy. Test it against prompt injection, unauthorized access, sensitive-data exposure and tool misuse, and put logging and incident response in place along with a way to suspend the agent or revoke its access. Decision point: keep going once the controls and intervention steps have actually been tested and everyone knows what the agent is allowed to do.

Now give it realistic work, including incomplete records, conflicting information, odd requests and the occasional system failure. Track how many tasks it completes, how accurate it is and how much correcting people have to do, then work out what a successfully completed task really costs once model usage, integrations, monitoring and review are all counted. Decision point: move to live testing when the agent hits the agreed thresholds and the team understands both its running cost and the ways it fails.

Give it to a small group of employees and a controlled slice of real work, with the old process still there for anything the agent can’t handle. Watch whether people actually use it, whether it saves them effort and whether they trust what it produces.

At the end, put the results next to the baseline from week one and look at productivity, accuracy, adoption, how often people had to step in, total operating cost and any security risks still open. The call is then to scale, fix specific gaps and retest, or stop, and a pilot that shows the agent is too expensive or too unreliable for the job has still done its work, because it has prevented a much bigger investment built on guesswork.

Counting how many tasks an agent processes says very little, since a system can churn through thousands of requests and still leave employees verifying, correcting or finishing most of them. Cost per successfully completed task, read alongside accuracy and exception rates, gives a far better picture of whether the process has improved end to end.

Those same measures become the case for giving an agent more room. One that reliably handles routine requests inside agreed limits can be trusted to act on them alone, while one that keeps meeting ambiguous cases keeps a human approving those decisions, with permissions reviewed as the evidence builds.

An agent that works well for one team will sooner or later have to deal with other departments, applications and approval chains. Scaling it takes clear ownership, consistent access controls and a way of handling exceptions, along with a budget for keeping the agent current as the processes around it change.

The decision underneath all of this is how much responsibility the organization is ready to hand over, and it has to rest on evidence from real work rather than a good demo. Leadership should come out of the eight weeks knowing what the agent can handle, where people still need to step in and what it will cost to run. The enterprises that pull ahead with agentic AI will be the ones that design for the work after automation from the first week and fund agents on the tasks they actually complete.

Digital Credit: Comparing The Six Bitcoin Preferred Stocks

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A simple four-part investor framework for evaluating Digital Credit.

Digital credit is an emerging asset class that transforms corporate Bitcoin balance sheets into income producing securities. Strategy pioneered the market with five perpetual preferred equity instruments: STRK, STRF, STRD, STRC, and STRE (the first four are listed in the U.S.). Strive subsequently introduced SATA and SATS, bringing the category to six U.S. instruments across two issuers.

These securities offer investors preferred equity (read: credit-like) exposure to companies whose balance sheets are dominated by Bitcoin. Their perpetual structure means investors principally rely on recurring dividends and the continued financial strength of the issuer. Unlike term debt, there is no maturation date at which the full principal is ever paid back.

Evaluating digital credit requires examining four characteristics: creditworthiness, investor protections, liquidity, and dividend profile.

This article explains all four dimensions and then offers a ranking for the six digital credit instruments along these lines. None of this should be taken as investment advice—it is only a systematic framework for analyzing digital credit.

Creditworthiness measures an issuer’s ability to support its financial obligations.

Traditional credit analysis focuses on earnings, free cash flow, and refinancing capacity. Companies with strong cash generation can service interest and repay maturing debt. Digital credit issuers today derive financial strength primarily from their Bitcoin reserves, placing greater emphasis on net asset coverage. In the future we could certainly see digital credit issuers that also have significant cash flows on top of Bitcoin holdings, which would add to creditworthiness.

The central question is how much Bitcoin supports each dollar of credit obligations. One useful measure is the BTC Rating, which compares the dollar value of Bitcoin reserves with the outstanding credit, accounting for the seniority of each credit instrument. Cash reserves can further strengthen creditworthiness by reducing net obligations.

For example, a 5x BTC Rating indicates five dollars of Bitcoin supporting every dollar of notional credit claims. Higher ratings provide greater protection against Bitcoin price declines.

Seniority matters because instruments from the same issuer can have different claims on the same asset base. A senior preferred instrument benefits from greater coverage than a junior preferred instrument: all senior claims are included in the BTC Rating calculation of a junior instrument.

Creditworthiness also depends on corporate governance and capital allocation. Management must preserve sufficient Bitcoin coverage while funding recurring dividends. Issuing additional credit increases dividend obligations, while assets accumulation (Bitcoin or cash) strengthen the asset base. Excessive leverage, poor treasury management, or sustained Bitcoin declines can weaken coverage over time.

Investors must therefore evaluate both their assumptions about Bitcoin’s long term performance and management’s ability to maintain a sustainable capital structure.

Investor protections determine what happens when an issuer fails to pay its promised dividends.

While creditworthiness concerns an issuer’s ability to pay, investor protections govern the consequences of missed payments, and more specifically the compensation investors may receive for delayed income.

The first distinction is cumulative versus noncumulative dividends. Cumulative dividends continue accumulating when payments are skipped, preserving the investor’s claim to unpaid distributions. Noncumulative dividends permit the issuer to permanently forgo a payment.

The second distinction is the treatment of unpaid dividends. Some instruments preserve unpaid amounts without additional compensation. Others compound arrears at the regular dividend rate. The strongest protections impose penalty rates, which cause unpaid dividends to compound at progressively higher rates as deferrals continue.

Penalty rates create financial incentives for management to maintain regular payments. They also compensate investors for the additional risk and delay associated with missed distributions.

Other investor protections may include restrictions on payments to junior shareholders, preferred shareholder voting rights or director elections, and dedicated dividend reserves.

These provisions establish the contractual guardrails surrounding dividend payments. Stronger protections increase the cost of deferral and improve the investor’s position to receive income.

Liquidity measures how easily investors can enter and exit positions without materially affecting market prices. This is especially important for digital credit, which does not mature and pay back principal on a fixed date. A liquid market is how an investor would “get his money back” if he wants to exit his position.

Digital credit has developed substantial secondary market liquidity, particularly in its leading instruments. The six U.S.-listed digital credit tickers are among the most actively traded preferred equity securities in the U.S. markets.

Two metrics help investors evaluate liquidity.

Absolute trading volume measures the total dollar value traded over a given period. Greater volume accommodates larger transactions and makes it easier for institutions to establish or liquidate positions.

Relative liquidity, or turnover, measures trading volume as a percentage of an instrument’s market capitalization. It captures how actively a security trades relative to its outstanding size.

For example, an instrument with $50 million in daily volume and a $1 billion market capitalization has 5% daily turnover. Another instrument trading $100 million daily with a $10 billion market capitalization has only 1% turnover. The second instrument offers greater absolute trading capacity, while the first experiences substantially greater trading activity relative to its size.

Absolute volume facilitates large transactions, while relative turnover reveals the level of trading activity relative to the capital outstanding.

Dividend profile describes the characteristics of the income an investor receives. Three main considerations are the dividend rate policy, payment frequency, and dividend yield.

The first distinction is between fixed and variable dividends. Fixed dividends establish a predetermined annual payment per share. Investors know the nominal income they are entitled to receive, allowing them to plan around a predictable cash flow stream. Variable dividends allow issuers to adjust the payout rate over time. These adjustments can respond to market interest rates, investor demand, or the trading price of the instrument. Fixed dividends prioritize income predictability for investors with reduced flexibility for issuers. Variable dividends provide flexibility for issuers but less predictability for investors.

The second consideration is payment frequency. Traditional preferred securities generally pay quarterly. Digital credit has introduced monthly, twice monthly, and daily dividend schedules. More frequent payments improve cash flow availability, accelerate reinvestment opportunities, and reduce the concentration of distributions around individual payment dates. After January 1 2027, all six instruments will be doing daily dividends.

The third consideration, and perhaps the most important out of anything mentioned in this article, is the raw dividend yield. This is the annualized income received divided by the trading price of the credit. A higher yield is generally preferred, but it also must be contextualized against risks and other considerations.
