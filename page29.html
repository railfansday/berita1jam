10 Thriller Books That Hooked Me From the Opening Chapter

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

At any given moment, Scott Simone has at least three books jockeying for his attention. He’ll never turn down a campus novel with a literary bent, nor will he hesitate to add yet another mystery to his Leaning Tower of Pisa-inspired to-be-read pile — which is built upon the bedrocks of too many short story collections, how-are-these-real true crime books, and classics waiting to be cracked open again. Topics that have him ripping through pages the fastest include closed-circle murders, unhappy professors, life-questioning philosophical deep dives, so long as they feature sharp sentences, a dark sense of humor, and at least a little malice.

He’s spent so much time in book shops over the years he is, to his wife’s chagrin, continuing to build a library that doesn’t quite respect the dimensions of the apartment meant to contain it.

Some thriller writers seem terrified you'll put their book down, so they throw everything at you: a murder on page one and an abduction on page three, all while a massive conspiracy begins to unravel. And that’s all before introducing who’s involved. Eventful, sure, but there's something almost desperate about all that hoopla. It’s as if the writer's afraid one quiet page will send readers straight back to their phones.

The best thriller writers have a little more faith in their stories. They're in no great hurry to show their hand, allowing the reader to become acquainted with a character worth following. Of course, there's nothing wrong with opening on a murder or throwing somebody into immediate danger, but the excitement alone can only take a story so far. What matters is that the writer gives us time to find our footing, to become familiar with the world we're entering and curious about what lies beyond it. By the end of that first chapter, we might have little idea where the story is headed, but we're more than happy to go along for the ride.

A woman burning her father’s corpse in the garden is a shocking way to start a novel, but Liz Nugent somehow makes it relatable in the opening pages of Strange Sally Diamond. Sally's father constantly joked that she should put him out with the trash when he died, so she does. Yet the way she recounts it, with the practicality of someone listing yet another household chore, you understand it. It's an absurd explanation, but Nugent delivers it so matter-of-factly that you begin to appreciate Sally's reasoning.

That's what makes Nugent's introduction so effective: everyone else sees a 42-year-old woman who's done something appalling. The police have questions and turn Sally into a suspect. The neighbors are horrified and turn Sally into a monster. Sally is confused by all the hubbub. The reader is at an advantage here, having spent some time in Sally’s mind and following her peculiar train of thought, understanding her in a way nobody else does. The twists and the shock that follow only deepen that early compassion for a character that, on the surface, could be easily written off as psychotic.

When you’ve got revolvers and shotguns pointed at your head, it usually spells endgame. Lee Child flips that script when introducing Jack Reacher in the opening of Killing Floor. Reacher is simply eating breakfast as four police officers descend on a Georgia diner. But he doesn’t fly off into action, doesn’t protest or attempt to escape: he quietly finishes his eggs and tucks five dollars under his plate. Reacher, in fact, is so composed that the police, all that firepower be damned, begin to look like the nervous ones.

It's a clever way to introduce a man who'll eventually become one of fiction's most formidable tough guys. Child could have staged some spectacular show of force, but instead he lets Reacher's observations do the work: he sizes up the officers and considers their tactics. And just when we're getting comfortable with the idea that Reacher is an innocent man caught in an unfortunate situation, Child drops a revelation: he hasn't killed anybody in this town. He hasn’t killed anyone in some time now. It’s the exclamation point to the chapter, a way of announcing the police may have arrested the wrong man, but he’s not a harmless one.

Sébastien Japrisot begins A Trap for Cinderella in just about the most non-thriller way possible: lulling the reader into a fairy tale. Mi and Do grew up together. The former is the ideal of the pair: beautiful and wealthy, the symbol of adoration for everyone around her. The latter is intelligent but poor, always getting the short end of the stick. But then Japrisot reveals that Do has invented this little fairy tale herself. She resents Mi, so she’s conveniently cast herself as Cinderella. It’s presented in such a way that we’ve no idea how much of what we've read is true.

That quiet beginning is a trap in itself as the novel takes a dark turn, with a fire at a villa on the French Riviera. The blaze leaves one woman dead and another so badly burned that she loses her memory. The survivor doesn't know whether she's Mi or Do, and the fire may have been deliberate, meaning she could be either the victim of an attempted murder or the person who planned it. Japrisot has already shown how easily a story can be twisted by the person telling it, and then allows his protagonist to reconstruct her entire life from what other people tell her.

Jean-Patrick Manchette’s opening to Fatale is a bit of a red herring. Almost the entire first chapter follows a man named Roucart on a hunting trip. We become overly familiar with his protruding belly and his deep regret at giving up smoking. It's the sort of attention you'd expect a writer to give someone we'll be following for the next few hundred pages. So when an attractive young woman appears in the woods, we assume she's wandered into Roucart's story. But she takes a shotgun off her shoulder and shoots him dead.

Ultimately, it’s clever the way Manchette makes us almost as susceptible to this woman as Roucart. He was so enthralled by her appearance that he didn’t for a second recognize the danger. We, too, dismiss the danger, sliding past the fact that she just committed a cold-blooded murder because we're so intrigued by her composure, so curious about who she is. She arrives in a provincial town where the wealthy residents have plenty of dirty little secrets: they're a vain, greedy, thoroughly unpleasant lot, and watching her exploit their weaknesses becomes something of a pleasure. Before long, we're almost rooting for a woman we know is capable of murder.

Many a thriller makes revenge look easy, the only logical next step in the grieving process. It can look a lot different when you’re staring it square in the eye. That's the predicament Kōtarō Isaka gives a grieving father in the opening of Bullet Train. Yuichi Kimura boards a Japanese high-speed train intent on killing the teenager who pushed his six-year-old son off a rooftop. He's got the boy's seat number, a gun fitted with a silencer, and every reason to want him dead, but not the confidence. So when he sees his target, an innocent-looking schoolboy, he hesitates. The boy doesn't.

And that's how Isaka introduces one of the novel's central ideas: Having a perfectly good reason to do something doesn't mean you're remotely able to pull it off. It's bad enough that Kimura's been outsmarted by a teenager, but worse, the boy finds his humiliation entertaining. And Isaka has plenty more fun at the expense of his characters as the train fills with professional killers, all convinced they know what they're doing. They're a dangerous bunch, sure, but for all their cleverness and carefully laid plans, they spend an awful lot of time making fools of themselves.

Jo Nesbø warps a symbol of winter wonderland into one of holiday horror in the opening pages of The Snowman. Sara Kvinesland meets her lover at his house, leaving her young son waiting in the car. She's nervous about someone catching them, so when her lover notices a face staring at them through the bedroom window, they both panic. But it's only a snowman, its pebble eyes fixed on the house. Except, snowmen don't build themselves — somebody went to the trouble of putting it right outside that window.

Nesbø lets that thought linger as Sara returns to her car, where her son is trembling behind locked doors, seeing something that's scared him half to death. Sara's got other things on her mind: she's worried about her affair and keeping up appearances. Her son can barely get her attention, even when he tells her they're going to die. And that's where Nesbø really gets under your skin: the boy knows something's wrong, but Sara, the one person who really should be listening, is too distracted to notice. There's no murder and no grisly discovery; just a snowman standing outside a house, and already we’re hooked.

Thirteen Hours opens at 5:36 a.m. with a woman running for her life. We don’t know why, or who’s chasing her, and we don’t find out. Deon Meyer leaves her, exhausted and terrified, desperate to escape the men who seem to want her dead. Meyer cuts to Detective Benny Griessel, who's being dragged out of bed by a phone call about a body outside a church. And the man’s got problems of his own: an estranged wife, another woman he slept with recently, and a 156-day desperate attempt to stay sober.

It's almost infuriating how casually Meyer abandons the woman, but that frustration only makes us more desperate to get back to her. Griessel, of course, hasn't the faintest idea she exists, and Meyer takes his time introducing the detective, giving us a man whose considerable professional experience is at odds with the mess he's made of his personal life. Each time Meyer takes us away from the woman, we're left worrying about what's happening to her while we're gone. And all the while, the clock keeps ticking, whether we're watching the woman running or not.

An ex-con in a stolen car isn't exactly the person you'd entrust with an 11-year-old girl's safety, but Jordan Harper has us begging her to climb inside in the opening of She Rides Shotgun. You see, a prison gang has ordered the murder of Nate McClusky and his family, and he's racing to get his daughter Polly before the killers do. But Polly doesn't know: all she sees outside her school is a hulking man covered in tattoos and scars, a father she barely remembers and who's supposed to be behind bars.

Harper puts us in an uncomfortable position, knowing that the man Polly fears might be the only person capable of protecting her. And he plays with that contradiction throughout their uneasy reunion. Polly is painfully shy and bullied at school, carrying a teddy bear for comfort. Nate looks like he could frighten just about anybody. But as they're driving away, a police car passes, and Polly catches something on her father's face she'd never expected to see: fear. Now this intimidating man doesn't seem so invincible, and that's what makes the opening so compelling.

It's a familiar enough scene: a mother worried about a son who's out past his curfew. But Gillian McAllister takes that moment of parental anxiety to the extreme in the opening of Wrong Place Wrong Time. Jen is waiting up for her 18-year-old son, Todd, watching from the window of their Liverpool home as he finally comes down the street. She's relieved to see him, until a stranger approaches. Todd pulls a knife and stabs the man three times, and she hasn't the faintest idea why.

It's a horrible thing for a parent to witness, made worse by the realization that the child you've spent 18 years raising might be someone you don't know at all. McAllister gives Jen a chance to go back and find out. We watch as the clocks move backward through the rest of the novel, allowing her to revisit the days leading up to the murder. She knows who wielded the knife but not what drove him to it. This literal trip down memory lane allows her to scrutinize the years she's spent raising him, revisiting conversations and decisions she'd long since forgotten.

It's easy to imagine what you'd do if someone broke into your house: grab the children, call the police, find something to defend yourself with. But Tracy Sierra makes those plans laughably optimistic in the opening of Nightwatching, in which a mother hears footsteps on the stairs. She entertains just about every possibility until a stranger appears at the top of the stairs, so tall he nearly hits his head on the ceiling. Even then, she struggles to accept what's happening.

Sierra makes us endure every second of that hesitation, making the mental cycle of decisions seem visceral. She thinks of screaming, but doesn’t want the intruder to know she’s there. She thinks about moving, but doesn’t want to wake the children. All the while, her mind also keeps latching onto the most random of details, from the man's dry sneakers to the light falling across the landing. There's something convincing about the way she thinks, grasping at anything that might help her understand the situation while struggling to do something about it.

It's Officially The End Of An Era For Marvel Shows

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Marvel’s ventures have been very successful in 2026, but it is now officially the end of an era for the TV side of the franchise. Upcoming Marvel movies will move the MCU back into more mainstream territory, with two Avengers films, an X-Men story, and Black Panther 3 hitting theaters in the next few years. This comes after a period in which Marvel Studios has arguably put more focus into crafting small-screen stories set in the MCU on Disney+.

Despite a somewhat mixed bag of projects releasing between 2021 and 2025, Marvel’s 2026 TV shows have been excellent. Be it Daredevil: Born Again season 2, VisionQuest, and the brilliantly unique Wonder Man in live-action or animated ventures like X-Men ‘97, Marvel Television seems to be hitting its stride of late. However, in a cruel situation involving unfortunate timing, Marvel’s TV era is seemingly at an end.

2026’s VisionQuest was a huge success in many ways; from a self-contained story about humanity and grief to a sequel to WandaVision and a stepping stone to Avengers: Doomsday, VisionQuest worked on all fronts. That said, where the former and latter points are concerned, VisionQuest does seem like the end of Marvel’s TV dominance. The show is the final one before the release of Avengers: Doomsday, and the second-to-last live-action show confirmed to be released before Avengers: Secret Wars in December 2027.

What this means is that, besides Daredevil: Born Again season 3, which is as self-contained as MCU shows can get, VisionQuest ends the Disney+ era of the franchise before upcoming movies change everything. Avengers: Doomsday will begin the end of the Multiverse Saga and, with no projects but Daredevil: Born Again season 3 set for release in the main MCU universe between it and Avengers: Secret Wars, it will likely change the multiverse drastically. Adding in the soft reboot of Avengers: Secret Wars, which will change Marvel TV forever, VisionQuest is truly the end of an era.

VisionQuest ending the current MCU TV era only makes the future of the franchise on the small screen more unclear than ever. As alluded to, Daredevil: Born Again season 3 will release in 2027, though the show has been canceled beyond that, meaning it will likely end that section of the franchise. After 2027, only one MCU TV show remains in development: Zodiac. As of right now, it is unclear what Zodiac is, what it is about, or who will be part of the series. Moreover, it is set for release after Avengers: Secret Wars' reboot of the MCU.

Other than that, Marvel Television has only standalone animated TV projects to produce, from Marvel Zombies to Your Friendly Neighborhood Spider-Man, which exist within their own continuities. All this reinforces how VisionQuest is the true end of an era for Marvel Television, as the future will only house standalone projects and those due to end soon, while the movie side of the franchise resets everything in massive ways.

Although the lack of clarity surrounding Marvel TV's future is worrying, VisionQuest concluded its most recent era in the best way possible. For one, on a broader level, VisionQuest was simply one of the studio's best Disney+ shows. Marvel TV has had as many hits as misses since 2021. The fact that the studio ended on such a high certainly makes things sweeter for Marvel and its audiences alike. Moreover, VisionQuest was a deeper, thematic conclusion for the WandaVision trilogy, which began in 2021 and has permeated Marvel TV since.

Not only was VisionQuest a good ending, quality-wise, but it signified the end of the Marvel TV storyline that started it all on Disney+ in 2021. Finally, VisionQuest's post-credit scene is one of the most exciting the MCU has produced in years, teasing Doctor Doom's upcoming rampage and the resurrection of the Scarlet Witch. This was the best way to signify the MCU's transition into the Avengers-fueled era that is about to ensue over the next year or so, only further solidifying VisionQuest as a fitting, exciting, thematically fruitful end of an era for Marvel Television.

‘9-1-1’ Is Officially Switching Protagonists, but There’s a Big Problem

Editor's Note: The following contains spoilers for 9-1-1 Season 10, Episode 6.9-1-1 is just six episodes into its tenth season, but the show already just aired its mid-season finale. Any network procedural fan knows it's standard for each main character to get a couple solo storylines during a shorter half-season like this, except for the show's leads – which, since the beginning of 9-1-1, were Athena (Angela Bassett) and Bobby (Peter Krause). Since Bobby's death, Athena has been 9-1-1's main lead, while the other characters each get a significant storyline every few episodes. Buck (Oliver Stark), however, has been getting more storylines than everyone else this season.

9-1-1 continues to go in circles with Buck's character in Season 10, making him regress to the point of impulsively giving up the child he chose to foster instead of actually working through his trauma and self-worth issues. Five episodes have been dedicated to the Theo (Lincoln and Theodore Sykes) storyline this season, and every Buck and Eddie (Ryan Guzman) storyline this season has been about Theo. The latter wouldn't necessarily be a problem, except that 9-1-1 is still giving Buck way too much focus without real growth, while unfairly relegating Eddie to the supporting role.

After spending pretty much all of his non-work screen time this season supporting Buck, Eddie got to make a bold move in last week's episode when he chose to foster Theo. Even knowing that Buck would take this as a personal slight, Eddie prioritized the needs of Theo over the second most important relationship in his life – barring that with Christopher (Gavin McHugh), who cares deeply about Theo and even wrote Buck an angry letter for giving him up. 9-1-1 set up something compelling here, where viewers could see how Eddie adjusted to raising a second child, and how he's been feeling about this entire situation. This was the perfect opportunity to get a rare insight into Eddie's emotions, and to see the pain that Buck's cruel remarks caused him.

Instead, Eddie only gets a little bit of focus in the mid-season finale. 9-1-1 finally delivers the long-anticipated first scene between Eddie and Maddie (Jennifer Love Hewitt), but the whole scene sees Maddie treating Eddie coldly and accusing him of fostering Theo to teach Buck a lesson. Maddie hasn't been afraid to call Buck out in the past, like when he hurt Eddie's ankle in "Buck, Bothered and Bewildered." Here, though, she takes his side when Eddie isn't even acting against him, and everyone from the 118 except for Ravi (Anirudh Pisharody) also backs Buck up. Eddie doesn't get to talk about his feelings or his new role as a foster parent at all, and he instead oddly emphasizes the apparent importance of Buck's role as Theo's biological parent.

The Buck and Eddie pairing became so beloved by fans because of their deep friendship and care for each other. They always supported each other through the toughest moments and showed up for each other – until now. Eddie tried to give Buck helpful and honest advice earlier this season, but Buck just lashed out at him. Eddie later stepped up and did something really thoughtful to help both Buck and Theo, and Buck blew up at him again. Instead of having each other's backs like they promised they would before they even became friends, Eddie's been consistently monitoring Buck's back, while Buck can't so much as be bothered to turn around and look at him. Everyone this season is coddling Buck except for Eddie, but Eddie's still been exceptionally gentle with him, refusing to even accept his lackluster apology without also unfairly blaming himself.

Becoming a foster parent has the potential to be an amazing storyline for Eddie, but not in the way 9-1-1 is currently setting it up, where Eddie is just a stand-in parent for Theo until Buck is ready to take him back. 9-1-1 should focus on Eddie's journey of parenting a second child, as well as the memories that this will inevitably bring up for him from Christopher's childhood. Then, either Theo needs to stay with Eddie, or Buck and Eddie need to find a solution that works for them – because it would do Eddie a tremendous disservice to have him simply give Theo back to Buck after choosing to parent him. Considering the show has neglected Eddie's storyline all season, it's about time that he gets something more than just the crumbs of a storyline from Buck.

Homes along Los Angeles' Sunset Boulevard could be seen engulfed in flames during the fires of January 2025

A jury in Los Angeles has found a 30-year-old man guilty of starting one of the most destructive wildfires in California's history.

Jonathan Rinderknecht was convicted of arson after starting the fires that swept through Pacific Palisades and parts of Malibu in January last year, killing 12 people and destroying more than 6,800 buildings in the US state.

The conviction was handed down nearly five months after deadlocked jurors led to the judge declaring a mistrial.

Rinderknecht, who had pleaded not guilty, was convicted of two federal charges. He is due to be sentenced on 17 March when he faces between five and 20 years in prison on each charge.

Jonathan Rinderknecht's arrest in October last year

Rinderknecht was found guilty of lighting a fire on 1 January last year which burned undetected deep in root systems before flaring back up six days later.

One of the charges was for starting the original brush fire, while the other centred on the damage caused when it reignited into a catastrophic blaze.

"We were determined to hold him accountable, and today's guilty verdict delivers a measure of justice for the families who lost loved ones and the thousands whose lives were upended," said Bill Essayli, who leads the US Attorney's Office for the Central District of California.

Rinderknecht is a Los Angeles resident and former ride-share delivery driver.

Defence attorney Steve Haney had argued at trial that prosecutors lacked sufficient physical evidence or records to prove their case.

Lawyers for the defendant said they were "disappointed" by the verdict but respected the jury's decision.

Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass, who was widely criticised for her handling of the fires, said in a statement that "no verdict can bring back the lives lost or erase the pain families have endured".

"So many are still struggling to rebuild," she said. "For those still displaced, the devastation remains a daily reality."

Prosecutors had argued that Rinderknecht was driven by revenge, anger and loneliness because he had nowhere to go for the holiday.

They cited geolocation data from his iPhone, security camera footage that showed his car's location and witness testimony from passengers.

They alleged that Rinderknecht had a "deeply entrenched belief that the wealthy were destroying the world" and in turn purposely started the blaze in the affluent neighbourhood.

The fire was one of several that ignited back-to-back across the LA region in January, which in total left 31 people dead and destroyed 16,000 structures.
