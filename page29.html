I plan to have the robot automatically dispense a treat for my pet bengal, Leona, perhaps by bowing its head to release one from a 3D-printed, head-mounted storage chamber.

I was able to code and train the virtual robot in just a few hours, which is, quite frankly, incredible. Previously, this kind of thing might have taken me weeks to figure out. It was possible because OpenAI’s latest models are remarkably good at understanding and manipulating 3D representations of the physical world and then translating that into useful code, agentic actions, and robot training.

Real roboticists are using the latest models to do some amazing stuff. When OpenAI released Astra this past September, University of California, Berkeley professor Ken Goldberg and his students saw demos that showcased its ability to render 3D images. They wondered if it could help with the simulations required to train different kinds of robots. These simulations need to be incredibly detailed and extremely accurate to ensure that sophisticated behavior—a humanoid that can dance or a robot hand that can grasp—will transfer faithfully to the real world.

One member of Goldberg’s lab gave Astra access to an Nvidia tool called Newton, showed it a video clip of a real robot in action, and asked if it could create a simulation of the scene.

“What blew us all away was that within an hour it had produced a full 3D simulation with all the parameters set—the position of the camera, the lighting, the background, all the materials,” Goldberg tells me.

Astra is also able to automate much of the drudgery involved with training a robot in simulation, Goldberg says, by running experiments and examining the results.

This is precisely what I found while training the virtual Microduck. Along with GPT-6 Sol, I used an agentic harness from Nous Research called Hermes and a Nvidia DGX Spark, a personal computer that contains a powerful AI chip.

I’ve previously written about using agents and coding models to configure, program, and debug physical robots. Normally, this kind of work requires significant expertise; modern coding models make it a cinch. But OpenAI’s newest model went far beyond just writing code.

It used a piece of Nvidia software called Isaac Sim to feed instructions to a model that controlled the virtual robot, watch videos of it in action to assess how well things were working, and suggest tweaks to its training regime.

This process allowed me to train the Microduck to walk around without toppling over. I initially tried getting GPT-6 Sol to teach the robot to moonwalk—I fed it a GIF of Michael Jackson sliding across the stage, which it tried to copy—but after hours of effort, it only resulted in a very jaunty backward jog. (The robots don’t have us totally beat quite yet.)

Jim Fan, a researcher at Nvidia who has collaborated with Goldberg, says the way AI can now control robots mirrors how software agents function. LLMS can use models trained to control physical actions—visual language action (VLA) models—just as they access different software on a computer.

This week, Fan, Goldberg, and others open sourced an agentic framework for robots called Aspire, which allows a robot to discover and use new skills. One example: GPT-6 Astra figured out how to use a pair of robot arms to solve a Rubik’s cube.

“It picks it up, tries to twist it, but has the wrong angle,” Fan says. “Then it reasons for a very, very long time, but it eventually is able to solve it because it learns the skills over time.”

At the Conference on Robot Learning (CoRL), held in Austin, Texas, this week, Fan gave a keynote speech in which he talked about the incredible realization that AI models can automate a lot of the work needed to have a robot do sophisticated things. Fan showed off a demo in which Astra was put in control of a robot arm and told to paint a picture of the Golden Gate Bridge. Using some paint and brushes nearby, it duly completed the task.

Of course, getting robots to work reliably and efficiently is still extremely difficult, requiring significant engineering expertise. The vision system on my robot duck does not work well enough to be deployed in a real product.

Still, the ease and speed of training robots with AI could soon have an enormous impact on manufacturing and e-commerce, and Goldberg says a number of industrial robotics and automation firms have shown an interest in the approach.

Much like vibe coding a few years ago, we may well see breakthroughs in “vibe robotics” as hobbyists and engineers realize how easy it is to program and train robots. And, assuming I can perfect my robot helper, Leona may never have to go without treats again.

CoreWeave turns to customer prepayments, shorter contracts to fund AI growth

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CoreWeave is rewriting how it pays for the AI boom, according to the company’s third-quarter earnings, reported on Tuesday. CoreWeave, a neocloud that rents out the computing power behind AI, said it’s increasingly asking customers to pay up front, borrowing against specific contracts, and even pursuing short-term deals that could lead to higher prices.

The new strategies could help CoreWeave finance its growth more efficiently while squeezing more cash from existing infrastructure. But these bets only hold if today’s demand and pricing power continue.

CoreWeave reported $3.66 billion in revenue, up 168% from the year prior, but it also posted a $709 million net loss and $846 million in quarterly interest expense.

“We will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields, nor do we need to,” CoreWeave CEO Michael Intrator said during Tuesday’s earnings call.

Instead, CoreWeave’s plan is to rely on loans backed by specific customer contracts, drawing on funds as needed to finance most of infrastructure it still has to build to deliver on its significant backlog of $132.5 billion.

CoreWeave also said it expects about $9 billion in customer prepayments and deposits over the next three months, including more than $3 billion tied to signing new customer contracts for capacity it has already built, which requires no additional spending on hardware.

Rivals Nebius and IREN have also relied on hefty Microsoft prepayments to pay for computing capacity, signaling that the industry is moving toward asking Big Tech to pay up for their buildouts.

Upfront payments could alleviate CoreWeave’s immediate borrowing needs, but they also commit the company to providing services at a price that might be less attractive down the road if compute demand continues to surge.

CoreWeave is also looking to capitalize on that growing demand by signing more shorter-term contracts that can command much higher prices.

The company raised prices across its offerings by about 25% in July and by another 10% later in the quarter. Prices are still climbing this quarter, in a way that “more than offset[s] cost increases,” said Intrator.

“We are also allocating more capacity to short-term opportunities, where the pricing justifies it, selling capacity at more than $40 million per megawatt annually, while pricing for new infrastructure on four and five-year contracts is moving to $20 million to $25 million per megawatt,” the chief executive said.

CoreWeave’s financing strategy now revolves around those opportunities. In August, the company closed a $2.6 billion loan, its first designed specifically for shorter-term customer contracts. Known as DDTL 5.5, it lets CoreWeave borrow in installments as it needs the money.

While short-term deals let CoreWeave charge higher prices and change them more frequently, it’s a tradeoff. Lenders give more weight to longer-term contracts, even if they’re priced lower, because they value predictable cash flow and consistency to repay the loan above all.

Even though Wall Street reacted positively to CoreWeave’s earnings, bond investors are much less sanguine. Last month, yields on some of the company’s bonds hit above 15%, highlighting that investors are concerned about the debt needed to fund data center buildouts before those investments see a return.

In short, CoreWeave is finding new ways to expand without relying on unsecured debt. The strategy it has chosen could boost profits, but it also carries greater risk around contract renewals and pricing — and it just goes to show how dependent the AI infrastructure boom is on sustained, and as yet unproven, demand.

"I usually spend the release days scouring social media looking for all the good news. It drives up my morale ... I'm always excited to see people receiving our stuff well and knowing I was a small part of it, so that was gone."

Amid a period of historic tumult in the gaming industry, it's easy to become desensitized by news of another mass layoff, but hearing from the workers themselves in this way really underscores the immense human toll of corporate downsizing.

Former Id Software AI engineering lead Daniel Young says the hardest part about being laid off was losing friends he'd worked with for years. "It hit me pretty hard. I think that was the biggest thing was the people that I was gonna be leaving behind. I hate to say it's like a death in the family, but it's almost like that's what it felt like," says Young.

"Personally the biggest thing was that I'm trying to help my kids through college," says Todd Boyce, former Id Software lead VFX artist and Hidden Horses technical artist. "I have two kids in college right now, and that is really expensive. That's probably the thing that hurts me the most is the possibility of them being financially stressed because of something that wasn't even in their control, and for a layoff that honestly didn't need to happen."

Meanwhile, Hidden Horses producer Andrew Willis says players can expect "absolutely stellar out-of-this-world fun gameplay, amazing and super cohesive and stylistic visuals, and really optimized gameplay on the technical side."

Technical designer Tim Locke says one key difference between working at a corporate-owned studio like Id and now Hidden Horses is creative freedom. "Being able to do what we want and talk through, like, what are our ideas, like, which ones resonate with the most people and how do we move forward with that. That's really the difference, and it has been different so far. It's been great."

Echoing that sentiment, Hidden Horses lighting artist Tyler DeLisle says "the people that were let go at Id ... we were all, none of us were slackers. We were all best of the best, top of our game, so now that we're all given this opportunity to come together and keep doing what we love to do, but kind of untethered and more collaborative, I think we can really put out something special."
