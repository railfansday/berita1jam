Summer Walker’s Interscope Lawsuit Heats Up as Star Accuses Label of Misusing Her IP

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amid an ongoing legal dustup over the terms of Walker's record deal, the singer is now alleging the label is improperly monetizing her content.

As Summer Walker and Interscope Records continue to hash out a contract dispute, the R&B singer is now accusing the Universal Music Group (UMG) label of wrongly monetizing her intellectual property.

Walker’s countersuit, submitted to Los Angeles court on Wednesday (Nov. 18) and reviewed by Billboard, comes a month after Interscope sued her for $50 million over the terms of their 2017 record deal. The document formalizes Walker’s assertion that the record deal is now void under California’s seven-year limit on personal services contracts.

Additionally, Walker is leveling up the dispute with new breach of contract and unjust enrichment claims. The singer alleges that Interscope has been using her intellectual property beyond the bounds of the record deal, which solely granted rights for the label to exploit and promote her music.

Walker alleges that she personally retains ownership of all her other IP, including the content she posts to social media. But according to Walker, Interscope has been reposting her digital content on YouTube and other platforms — and making money from it.

“UMG’s limited contractual rights to exploit basic works and use Cross-Complainant’s name, likeness and identity for authorized promotional purposes did not confer unrestricted ownership or monetization rights in the excluded digital assets,” reads the countersuit. “As a direct and proximate result of the foregoing conduct, UMG wrongfully obtained and retained revenues and other economic benefits attributable to property in which Cross-Complainant possessed superior ownership or possessory rights.”

Walker wants Interscope to give her the profits made from this content. She says the total amount would need to be established through evidence discovery, but the legal papers cite a damages figure of $20 million.

A lawyer for Walker, Keith Moten, told Billboard, “Summer has always approached her relationship with Interscope in good faith, and she remains appreciative of the role the label has played in her career. However, that relationship does not give Interscope unlimited rights over her creative work or the revenues generated from it.”

“Interscope has asserted its interpretation of the parties’ agreement, and Summer is equally entitled to challenge conduct that we believe exceeds the rights granted under that agreement,” added Moten. “Our cross-complaint seeks accountability for the alleged unauthorized exploitation of her creative assets and the revenues generated from it.”

A rep for Interscope did not immediately return a request for comment on the countersuit.

Walker has been locked in legal negotiations with Interscope since August, when she first sent a notice terminating their record deal under California’s seven-year statute. This state law, also known as Section 2855, allows individuals to cut off any personal contract that runs past that amount of time.

But the statute has a carveout specific to the music industry: Under a 1987 amendment, labels can sue for damages if an artist chooses to exit a record deal after seven years but before all of their album commitments have been fulfilled.

Interscope did exactly that in October. The label alleged that if Walker walks away, the singer will owe $50 million because she’s only delivered three projects from her contract’s five-album commitment: her 2019 debut Over It, which hit No. 2 on the Billboard 200, the 2021 chart-topper Still Over It and another No. 2 set, Finally Over It, in 2025.

Walker also has a separate ongoing legal action against the label/management firm LVRN, to which she jointly signed with Interscope in 2017. Walker claims the firm skimmed money from her music, touring and merch, while LVRN is countersuing over alleged unpaid commissions and royalties.

The S&P 500 Just Fell 4 Straight Weeks From a Record High. Here’s Why I’m Buying the Dip.

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There's a big difference between a healthy pullback and the start of a bear market.

By David Dierking – Nov 19, 2026 at 7:50PM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

The S&P 500 (^GSPC -2.02%) closed at a record 7,818.93 on Oct. 6. It finished that week higher, but from there it closed lower four weeks in a row, culminating in its post-election day drubbing.

As of now, the index is about 8% below its October high. The Nasdaq-100 and the tech sector are faring far worse, and investors are thinking about a potential bear market for the first time in over a year.

But if you go back several decades and look at situations like this -- the S&P 500 declining four straight weeks following a record high -- the outlook from here could actually be pretty bullish.

Since 1970, there have been 12 occasions when the S&P 500 has fallen four straight weeks right after hitting a new all-time closing high the previous week. Historically, the short term following these events has been more volatile. But it's the longer-term recovery period that's more meaningful.

Six months after the streak ended, the S&P 500 was higher in 10 of those 12 instances. The median gain was 13.4%, which is more than double the 5.2% median six-month gain during an average week since 1970.

Over the subsequent 12 months, the S&P 500 was higher on 11 of the 12 occasions, with a median gain of 16.5%.

I don't want to over-inflate the success rate here when the sample size is only 12, but I believe there's an explanation for this. Even bull markets have periods when stocks pull back. In many cases, it's not necessarily a sign that economic growth is stalling and a major bear market is imminent. It's just a normal part of the process of investing in risky assets. These are often normal market gyrations, and I think the data shows that over the long term, stocks tend to rise more often than fall.

Data source: Investing.com data and author's analysis of S&P 500 daily closing prices. "Streak ended" is the final trading day of the fourth losing week. Returns are measured from that close. Three months = 13 weeks; six months = 26 weeks; 12 months = 52 weeks.

One month from the streak's end, the chances of a positive return are pretty much a coin flip. It's not until three months out that, historically at least, the odds start to swing in your favor.

But I believe the overall trend is pretty clear and a good example of why a long-term buy-and-hold strategy is usually the best approach. In this scenario, the odds of generating a positive return have historically kept improving the longer you stay invested. That's a good framework to keep in mind whether stocks are steadily declining or near all-time highs.

History suggests that dips like these are much more likely to be healthy pullbacks during longer-term rising markets than a bear market warning.

Therefore, there's probably little need to make any major changes to your investment process. That means keeping your stock/bond allocations intact. Continue investing monthly or with every paycheck. Make sure your portfolio still aligns with your long-term goals.

If anything, you could consider positioning the equity segment of your portfolio a little more defensively. Moving from a core position in the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -1.98%) to something like the Vanguard Value ETF (VTV -1.58%) or the Invesco S&P 500 Low Volatility ETF (SPLV -1.27%) could be a smart way to take some risk off the table while maintaining your equity exposure.

Europe ready for talks with Russia on ending war in Ukraine, leaders say

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

European leaders have said they are ready for negotiations with Russia on ending the war in Ukraine, in a strong show of support for Kyiv.

"Europe has a clear and consistent and united negotiating position," UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham said, while German Chancellor Friedrich Merz stressed that Moscow's "maximalist demands are not realistic".

They were speaking at Berlin's summit of Ukraine's allies known as the Coalition of the Willing.

Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner, one of the two US envoys who were in Berlin, said the US president wanted to make a deal with Moscow "with Europe by his side". Russia's Vladimir Putin has so far rejected any European role in the US-led peace talks.

Trump had earlier held historic trilateral talks in Shenzhen, south-eastern China, with Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping and later dialled into the Berlin summit from Air Force One.

In a lengthy press conference following his talks with Trump and Xi, Putin addressed the question of multilateral peace talks, saying the Americans were "insistently" asking Russia to allow the Europeans to take part.

Putin claimed Russia was not against the idea but said Moscow wanted to clarify "what Europe will bring to these talks".

"That is why, through diplomatic channels, we have invited the Europeans to come to Moscow in order to determine their positions. And after that, we will decide in what format we will continue the dialogue with Ukraine."

But Putin also said that "every day" Russia heard that European leaders were talking about war coming in 2030. "But we are not planning to attack anyone... Is that normal? I do not think so," he said. Still, Putin added, Russia understood that "discussing issues linked to security in Europe is pointless without the Europeans".

Jared Kushner told reporters that the US president had come away from Shenzhen believing that "peace is achievable".

Striking an upbeat note, Kushner said that the Russians were willing to trust the instinct of the US envoys "on our behalf" that the Europeans should be at the table and that Moscow was now "comfortable" with Ukraine having security guarantees based on the idea enshrined in Nato's Article Five that requires allies to come to a country's defence if it comes under attack.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, who also was at the summit in Germany's capital, has long sought a guarantee equivalent to Nato's collective defence clause, and there has been little indication that Putin would accept that kind of condition.

Russia rejects outright the idea of Ukrainian membership of Nato, but reports say US negotiators have mulled the idea of giving Kyiv Article Five-style guarantees without being part of the Western defensive alliance if it agrees to painful territorial concessions.

He also said any attempts to divide Europe into spheres of influence run by major powers was unacceptable, amid concerns on the continent that this issue may have been on the agenda at the trilateral talks in Shenzhen.

"A 'Yalta format' will not bring peace," Merz said, referring to the post-World War Two agreement between the US, the USSR and Britain in 1945.

Still, Putin claimed to be "interested" in ending the war and was ready to make compromises, while Ukraine and Europe were not.

"I assure you, we have no desire at all to continue this armed conflict," Putin said, despite the fact that Russia has in recent months intensified its attacks on Ukraine's critical infrastructure as cold winter approaches.

10 Classic Books You Must Read Before You Die

Apologies in advance for some omissions, but at least what’s below isn't really a ranking. He’s unusual in other ways, too, as are all the people he runs into, with those humorous interactions being more the point of A Confederacy of Dunces, rather than a strict narrative. It’s always interesting, though, and the way John Kennedy Toole wrote this entire novel is hard to compare to much else out there, whether you're looking at other comedic novels, other books without much by way of narrative, or, honestly, just about any piece of writing out there, really.

There are many great things about Fahrenheit 451, but one of the things that stands out most is how easy it is to get through in just a day or two. It’s not a long book, and while it might not be fun in the traditional sense, since it’s about a dystopia, it is always well-paced and engaging/entertaining in other ways. And then, of course, it’s also worth pointing out how well it’s aged.

All that’s obvious. It’s obviously got timeless themes and it’s obviously not the sort of thing that wastes time or goes on for pages upon pages that aren’t really needed. Sure, it’s a little strange that even with the brief length, it has proven difficult to adapt into a film, but that does mean Fahrenheit 451 is worth highlighting as a book you must “READ” before you die, since the other ways of digesting this story don’t really do justice to what Ray Bradbury did here.

Les Misérables is famous for being very long and very heavy-going, albeit not entirely depressing, since there is humanity explored here and a bit by way of redemption and all that. There are almost two decades covered, with fictional characters existing and struggling during a tumultuous time in French history; namely, from the mid-1810s until the June Rebellion in Paris, so you get historical drama and then some more personal drama at the same time.

If you want to be kind of cheeky and/or willing to over-simplify things, you could also say Les Misérables is a novel about a man who steals a loaf of bread, and then bad things happen. There are some tangents, lots of side characters and subplots, and just a lot of stuff that feels sprawling in general, but there is plenty of room to explore everything here, and Victor Hugo’s does remain quite a joy to read (especially if translated well, of course… that could make a difference).

Just what Infinite Jest is, who knows? Maybe a psychological dramedy would be the best way to describe it? Even then, it’s kind of a mystery just because of how confounding it is, and how little certain things seem to line up with everything else. The non-chronological storytelling doesn’t help if you want to follow and understand everything, nor does the fact that there is a large cast of characters here.

Some of those characters are residents of a drug and alcohol recovery program, others are members of a tennis academy, and some other people are radicals/revolutionaries. And there’s a lot by way of family drama that relates to a good bit of that. That might make Infinite Jest sound daunting, and also, it’s got lots of footnotes and more than 1000 pages all up, but it’s a white whale kind of book that is immensely rewarding to dive into, even if it takes you a while, and even when, inevitably, you don’t understand all of it (it’s okay; no one does, just like life, maaaan).

There is something quite impressive about how much it lingers in one’s mind long after one’s technically finished reading it.

It’s a difficult book to read, and definitely not enjoyable in the traditional sense, but it’s too important to ignore, and there is something quite impressive about how much it lingers in one’s mind long after one’s technically finished reading it. It’s not a biographical book about Marilyn Monroe, but it is still about her, just more abstract, tackling her life in a way that feels like more of a psychological drama, or even a bit like a work of surreal (yet distressingly realistic) horror at times, all in service of exploring and unpacking some weighty yet continually relevant ideas/themes.

War and Peace is famously long, to the point where that's all some people seem to really know about it. That’s not helped by the length being mentioned first here, but you do have to know that going in. But it’s also the kind of thing that should be exciting, since something so well-written being this well-written for so long isn't the kind of thing that comes around all that often, you know? So maybe that supports the idea or perpetuates it, which was a careless thing to do on this writer’s part. Then again, it’s hard to convince someone to read something that’s like 1500 pages long in about 150 words, and then if you also have to summarize what the novel’s about… eh, forget it.

It is a genuinely absorbing for all those many, many pages, and while it’s hard to commit to something like this initially, dedicating oneself to the whole epic starts to feel rewarding much earlier than one might expect. Leo Tolstoy has interesting observations on history, strong opinions on Napoleon, he lets certain characters grow/change immensely, he can write good battle scenes, and he explores mortality and death more powerfully and poetically than just about any other writer not just of his time, but of all time. This massive book is truly something special, and sure, you might be scared, but you do really, really need to give it a shot.

For as admirably intense as so many other books of Don DeLillo’s are, none are as persistently uneasy and thrilling as Libra. This is one of those rare novels that doesn’t feel long enough, being a dizzying work of historical fiction that unpacks the confusion and uncertainty around the John F. Kennedy assassination, all without offering any answers, and Libra doesn’t really suggest any conspiracy theories are true or untrue.

It’s deliberately a work of historical fiction, and it’s more about uncertainty and the nature of a conspiracy (and what draws people toward them) than it is a suggestion of something that actually happened surrounding the JFK assassination. It’s such a wild and effortlessly anxiety-provoking reading experience, not to mention truly special and essential as a book, seeing as no adaptations yet exist (and if they were made, they’d probably prove very hard to get right).

The movies here are interesting (at least the 1984 one) or very good (the 2021 and 2024 movies), but Dune is one of a fair few cases where the book is superior. There is a lot of strangeness and compelling things that can be captured in one or two movies, and yeah, this is defending that 1984 one, to some extent. It’s a mess, but doing Dune in a bit over two hours was a wild undertaking and things were always going to be messy.

Sorry, bit of a tangent. But what a certain 1950s book did for fantasy, Dune did for science fiction. If you really don’t like sci-fi, it might not convert you wholly to the genre, but if you do like sci-fi and you haven’t read Dune yet, then dude, read Dune; get it done, because it’s not a dud. It’s as far from a dud as you can get, with sci-fi. And some of the sequels are interesting too, so long as you're willing for them to go into increasingly strange territory.

While The Lord of the Rings is pure fiction, rather than Les Misérables or War and Peace, which were historical fiction, the way The Lord of the Rings is written makes it feel like it happened. Like, you don’t believe these things literally happened, but it’s more a testament to J.R.R. Tolkien’s writing that you can get swept up in the world and the people in it, and also be continually impressed by just how much “history” there is to Middle-earth.

No one’s done it better, really, in terms of literary fantasy on such a scale. Maybe no one ever will, but that’s okay, because The Lord of the Rings is such a high bar and even if something scratches a slightly similar itch, that’s probably a pretty great sign. And like some other long books, finding the time for this one can be hard, but it is ultimately so worth it, and essentially essential to tackle at least once in your life.

There is quality throughout the entirety of Different Seasons, and that’s not something you can say about all the short story/novella collections of Stephen King’s. With Different Seasons, it’s all novellas, and this collection is being singled out here, rather than there being a mention of one of his bigger novels, mostly because this feels like one of the more approachable classic Stephen King books.

Like, a few people don’t like horror. And sure, you do get a bit of horror here, namely in “The Breathing Method,” but then “Apt Pupil” is more of a thriller, and “Rita Hayworth and Shawshank Redemption” and “The Body” are more dramas, not to mention tremendously moving ones at that (and they became the films The Shawshank Redemption and Stand by Me, respectively). Just really good writing here, and the stories are all easy to get through, immensely readable, and compelling in their own different/interesting ways.

To be fair to "Grand Theft Auto 6," it does remember that it is an action/adventure game and not the next coming of "Car Mechanic Simulator." For instance, stops to get your car fixed are generally pretty brief. That way, you can get right back to running heists and stealing a car that looks a lot like a Pontiac Trans Am out of a police impound lot.

Still, the game does feature four different ways to get your car fixed: Pay 'N' Spray shops, mod shops, gas stations, and a guy named Joey who can be called upon to do repairs. Overall, it seems like Rockstar has found a nice balance between the realities of car ownership and the escapist fantasy that the Grand Theft Auto series has always embodied. We'll just have to wait and see if "Car Mechanic Simulator 2027" will be adding heists.
