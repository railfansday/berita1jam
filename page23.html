Colette Kress Has Been Named Interim Chief of Nvidia. But There’s Another Executive Favored to Replace Jensen Huang

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Kress is the logical pick for now, as the company's longtime CFO. But other executives, both inside and outside the company, are being discussed.

Although Jensen Huang has officially resigned as CEO of Nvidia (NVDA -4.34%), a new story is quickly emerging: who will replace the man many call the Godfather of artificial intelligence.

AI is a generational, game-changing new technology, and Huang founded Nvidia over three decades ago, so whoever replaces Huang has enormous shoes to fill.

Nvidia's CFO Colette Kress has been named interim CEO. It's a logical choice. Kress has been CFO of Nvidia for 13 years, is always on conference calls, and knows Nvidia's numbers likely better than anyone.

However, another executive is favored to replace Huang in the top job as of this writing.

As of 5:53 p.m. ET on Nov. 12, Kalshi assigned a 42% chance that a Nvidia executive named Ian Buck would become Nvidia’s next CEO. Kress has the next-highest chance at 13%, and there are others in the running as well.

Buck, according to his LinkedIn profile, has been a vice president and general manager of Nvidia for nearly 14 years and has worked at Nvidia for over 22 years. Buck completed his undergraduate studies at Princeton University and then earned a Ph.D. in computer science from Stanford.

According to various biographies, Buck was the development lead for Brook, the programming language that enabled developers to leverage the parallel processing capabilities of graphics processing units (GPUs) for general-purpose computing. Buck is also credited with creating CUDA, which stands for Compute Unified Device Architecture.

CUDA came out in 2006 and has served as a key moat for Nvidia and its GPUs. CUDA is a parallel computing platform and programming model that helps developers build applications with Nvidia's GPUs. CUDA has become the industry standard for deploying new AI applications, providing Nvidia with a strong moat.

Investors can think of CUDA as an operating system that runs only on Nvidia chips. So even though there are competitors that can build chips just as powerful as Nvidia, developers will be less prone to switch because they would have to use a different computing platform that they may not know as well.

The Huang scandal and quick departure stunned much of Wall Street, which is why the stock has tanked over the past week. Many analysts and pundits also speculated that there was no succession plan in place prior to the scandal.

Buck could make sense for several reasons. Even though Huang is tough to replace, Nvidia still has a great product in high demand. One question, though, is what does innovation look like after Huang.

After all, Nvidia frequently releases new chip models. Buck provides investors with reassurance that the product is unlikely to go stale. His hiring would resemble Apple's recent decision to name the longtime Apple executive and former senior vice president of hardware engineering, John Ternus, as its new CEO to replace Tim Cook.

"He (Buck) could protect Nvidia's technological edge, but his ability to manage the wider commercial relationships and geopolitical challenges would face scrutiny," Charu Chanana, an investment strategist at the investment bank Saxo, said in a research note, according to the BBC.

With Huang gone, there are still many concerns about Nvidia, particularly regarding the company's relationship with China and its sales to foreign companies that may have illegally re-exported the chips to China.

Chanana raises an interesting point, as one of Huang's big tasks over the past year-and-a-half has been convincing the Trump administration to allow Nvidia to sell chips in China. Buck may not have experience in this realm, which will likely face even more scrutiny moving forward.

While Kalshi offers some insight, the prediction markets platform has certainly been wrong many times before, and we don't know how long it will take Nvidia to name a new CEO -- it could be tomorrow or months from now.

There are also other candidates who have a chance of becoming Nvidia's CEO, according to Kalshi, including former Nvidia executive Rene Haas, who is now the CEO of Arm Holdings, and Apple's Chief Hardware Officer Johny Srouji.

But right now, Buck is the clear favorite.

VCLT vs IGLB: Which Long-Term Corporate Bond ETF Offers Investors Better Long-Term Prospects?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Vanguard's fund offers a lower expense ratio and higher yield, but both ETFs share similar risk profiles and diversification.

The iShares 10+ Year Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (IGLB -1.28%) and Vanguard Long-Term Corporate Bond ETF (VCLT -1.29%) provide efficient exposure to long-dated corporate debt, differing primarily in their specific maturity targets and cost structures.

Both funds target the long end of the corporate credit curve, focusing on high-quality debt with maturities of 10 years or more. These instruments are particularly sensitive to interest rate fluctuations, making them primary tools for investors seeking to lock in yields or hedge against falling rates. While they share similar risk profiles, their underlying portfolios and expense structures provide slight points of differentiation for income seekers looking to optimize their fixed-income allocations in a volatile rate environment.

Beta measures price volatility relative to the S&P 500; beta is calculated from monthly returns over the available fund history (up to five years). The 1-yr return represents total return over the trailing 12 months. Dividend yield is the trailing-12-month distribution yield as of the close of trading on Nov. 10, 2026.

Vanguard is slightly more affordable with a 0.03% expense ratio compared to 0.04% for iShares. Additionally, the Vanguard fund currently offers a higher payout, with a yield gap of 0.24 percentage points over its iShares competitor.

The iShares 10+ Year Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF mirrors high-quality, U.S. dollar-denominated corporate debt with remaining maturities exceeding 10 years. As a fixed income fund, it has no equity sector breakdown. Its portfolio is highly diversified across 3,811 holdings, and its largest positions include diversified corporate issuers where no single bond issue exceeds 0.29% of its assets under management (AUM). It launched in 2009. iShares 10+ Year Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF has paid $2.64 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$45.77 share price works out to a 5.8% yield.

Vanguard Long-Term Corporate Bond ETF provides a consistent flow of income through investment-grade corporate bonds with weighted average maturities typically between 10 and 25 years. This fixed income fund offers no equity sector breakdown. It holds 2,864 positions, and its largest positions include high-quality corporate bonds where no single position exceeds 0.36% of its assets under management (AUM). While holding fewer individual issues than its peer, it remains robustly protected against individual issuer defaults through broad market representation. It launched in 2009. Vanguard Long-Term Corporate Bond ETF has paid $4.13 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$68.69 share price works out to a 6% yield.

Bonds are having a rough go of it in the market this year, but over time, they have proven to be a crucial part of a diversified portfolio. Each of these funds offers the safety of bonds with the income generation that debt paper is known for, but they have crucial differences to examine before investing.

IGLB, the iShares bond ETF, has an 11.36-year average duration in its portfolio, primarily from bonds from U.S. issuers, accounting for 82% of the portfolio, followed by the U.K. at 3.6%, and Canada at 2.2%.

Just 2.2% of the portfolio is dedicated to the top 10 holdings, which greatly reduces the concentration risk of any one bond in the portfolio. However, that figure doesn't account for the fact that positions can include multiple bonds from the same corporate issuer.

Over the past three years, IGLB has returned an annualized 6.8%, but a negative 3.2% over the five-year look-back. Over the prior decade, the ETF has returned 1.7%.

The Vanguard corporate bond fund examined here, VCLT, has an 11.62-year average duration, with 89% of the portfolio in U.S. issuers. Bonds from issuers in the U.K. (3.,2%), Canada (2.2%), and Belgium (1.1%) are also noteworthy. About 2.8% of the portfolio sits in the top 10 holdings, which means there's not much risk to the portfolio from any single bond. However, there can be multiple bonds from the same issuer in the portfolio, which can carry a hard-to-spot issuer risk.

Performance-wise, VCLT has returned investors an annualized 6.7% over the past three years, a negative 3.4% over the past five years, and a positive 1.7% over the past decade.

Based on performance, these funds are very close. We give the nod to Vanguard's VCLT for its very slight expense ratio advantage over IGLB. Over the long term, lower expenses should equate to better returns.

A coffee shop latte costs about $5, and buying one most workdays adds up to more than $1,000 a year. At home, the same drink costs about 70 cents. Make most cups yourself with a setup that pays for itself within two months, and keep a café visit or two a week as the treat.

That $5 is the typical price of a 12-ounce latte at independent coffee shops, according to the fall 2026 Coffee Price Index by Fresh Cup. Buy one five workdays a week for 50 weeks and that’s $1,250 a year, before tax and tip.

Making the latte yourself costs a fraction of that. Ground coffee averaged $9.25 a pound in October 2026, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. At the weakest strength the National Coffee Association suggests for a French press, a 12-ounce mug takes about 22 grams of coffee, roughly 45 cents’ worth. Brew those 22 grams with less water for a strong base, add 8 ounces of whole milk at the bureau’s average of $4.10 a gallon, and a latte costs about 70 cents.

Pricier beans don’t close the gap. At $15 a pound, that latte costs about $1, still about $4 less than the café’s $5. Plain drip has a gap too, $3.25 for 12 ounces in those shops against about 45 cents at home.

The switch lasts only if you like the home cup, so spend on beans you enjoy before you spend on gear. A drip coffee maker or French press handles plain coffee. For lattes, pair a handheld milk frother with a moka pot, a stovetop brewer that makes strong, espresso-style coffee. Add a travel mug for the commute, plus a grinder if you buy whole beans, and keep the total near $100. At about $4.30 saved per homemade latte, that setup pays for itself in about 23 cups, six to eight weeks at three or four homemade lattes a week.

Judge any upgrade the same way. A $500 espresso machine takes about 117 homemade lattes to break even.

Keep the café for the visits you’d miss most. Brew at home three or four days a week, buy a latte on the others, and the year’s coffee costs about $390 to $610 instead of $1,250.

TL;DR: Bottomline has completed live USDC transfers inside its enterprise payments platform. The initial transfer was between company entities.

Bottomline disclosed the cross-border transactions in a November 10 announcement, using its Global Payments Hub and Cash Management platform.

The practical point is where the payment fits. A finance team may be more interested in approving, tracking and reconciling a transfer within its existing systems than in opening a separate crypto application to make it happen.

Bottomline describes the milestone as an intra-company transfer. That establishes that the company used the integration for a live movement of funds; it does not establish that unrelated businesses are already using it at scale.

The release says the aim is to let treasury teams handle stablecoin payments alongside traditional methods, retaining familiar approvals, controls and audit processes. It presents near-real-time settlement for selected scenarios, especially cross-border payments, as part of the attraction.

The company has not supplied a transaction-by-transaction performance dataset in this announcement. Readers therefore cannot use it to calculate an average fee saving or a reliable improvement in end-to-end delivery time.

Bottomline’s cash-management product description emphasises connecting treasury, accounting, compliance, banking and enterprise resource planning data. It describes tools for cash visibility, reconciliation and reporting, helping place the USDC announcement within the wider product.

Consider a group with businesses in two countries. Moving value between them is only one part of the job. Staff may also need to identify the correct entities, record the purpose of the transfer, obtain approval and match the completed payment to the accounts.

If a stablecoin route requires those steps to be reconstructed manually outside the normal workflow, a fast blockchain transfer can still leave an expensive administrative task. Integration is useful when it keeps the financial record attached to the movement of money.

NewsBTC explored a related implementation issue in Circle and ACI’s Latin American settlement collaboration, which examined how a digital-dollar option fits within existing payment operations.

Circle describes USDC as a dollar-redeemable token backed by liquid reserves. That explains the asset being transferred, but a corporate payment can involve more than the onchain movement itself.

A business funding a transfer from a bank account needs an entry route. A recipient that pays suppliers in local currency may need conversion and a bank payout. Each stage can affect the amount received and the time before it can be spent.

For that reason, the useful comparison is between two complete payment routes serving the same need. A low network charge does not settle the comparison if conversion costs are higher, or if an exception requires substantial staff time to resolve.

The next useful disclosures would identify supported use cases, the businesses choosing them and performance over repeated transfers. Clear handling of failures, rejected payments and reconciliation exceptions would be particularly informative.

There is also a difference between a first transaction and a mature operating metric, as NewsBTC’s analysis of Bitso Business payment volumes explains. Neither measure should be mistaken for fee revenue or universal customer availability.

Bottomline has demonstrated a concrete integration step. Its commercial value will come from making a recurring treasury task easier to complete and account for, with enough transparency for finance teams to compare the new route with the options they already trust.
