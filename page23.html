Raheem Sterling's chaotic descent into insanity is a product of the cocooned football world where stars can't handle rejection, writes IAN HERBERT - now he must listen to those around him if he wants to salvage his career

📅 2026-11-25  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Published: 08:21 EST, 25 November 2026 | Updated: 08:23 EST, 25 November 2026

Raheem Sterling wore black to his sentencing hearing today, just as he had when he arrived at Basingstoke Magistrates Court two months ago and admitted to 90 minutes of terrifying rush hour driving, with canisters of nitrous oxide on the passenger seat beside him.

He was visibly more subdued this time, wearing the look of a man who’d spent two months contemplating the prospect of a jail stretch, whilst the contemporaries he once stood with in the England squad, Harry Kane, Jordan Henderson and Jordan Pickford, were continuing to hit the heights.

Imprisonment was only ever a narrow possibility under the sentencing guidelines for dangerous driving, given the miracle that Sterling did not cause serious injury or worse as he zig-zagged between lanes on the M3 and M4 that morning last May. Critically, he was not found to be under the influence of nitrous oxide. A motorist who drove in equally erratic fashion and initially ignored police on Cheshire’s M56, in a BMW which had only three tyres, was handed a suspended sentence earlier this year.

But while the mitigation preceding the magistrate’s decision that Sterling would walk free - his 12-month prison sentence is suspended for 18 months - garlanded him for his charity work and promotion of racial equality, don’t let it be said that his conduct that day was nothing less than a disgrace. And that the tetchy hyper sensitivity Sterling displayed with the police officers who arrested him revealed a shocking lack of contrition and shame.

Sterling has had his challenges, of course. The early death of his father and a nomadic London childhood spent helping his mum on cleaning jobs. But then he disappeared into the cocooned existence of the football academy world, full of the little princes who have people fawning over them, telling them how good they are and throwing obscene amounts of money at them.

Raheem Sterling's conduct the day of his car crash was nothing short of a disgrace

Sterling's meteoric rise has meant he does not have the tools to deal with rejection

Kane has flourished in a way that Sterling hasn’t because he was forced to learn the hard way, deal with rejection and develop a sense of perspective. The princes are at the mercy of the hangers on and leeches, around them for a cut of their next big pay cheque. It was Sterling’s misfortune to select Aidy Ward as his agent – the man who conducted ugly, public contract negotiations with Liverpool which included declaring that Sterling wouldn’t be staying at Anfield even if he were offered nearly £1m a week. The reputational damage that caused Sterling should have seen him sack Ward on the spot.

The problem for those whose adolescence takes place in football’s gilded cage is that they combust and can’t hack it when the going gets remotely tough. For Sterling, read Dele Alli, Kalvin Phillips and Aaron Ramsdale.

But what makes Sterling’s personal disintegration so needless is that he really has had positive influences around him in the background. Friends who were there when he wanted to develop a voice to speak against racial discrimination in football. There when he wanted to establish a Foundation to help the kind of London teenagers that he used to be.

He lost those influences during the chaotic descent into the insanity of that spring morning when he went berserk in his car. Some of them have described to me how he flatly refused to respond to calls and went into ‘oblivion mode’ in the hours after his arrest. It seemed like he was seeking escape from any vestige of the game which is all he has known.

England captain and Sterling's former team-mate Harry Kane has thrived due to the early setbacks in his career

The tragedy is Sterling has a strong support network who want the best for him

The bleak reality for Sterling is that his career in football is surely over now. Yes, he could pick up the thread in some place below the Premier League, though his unhappy experience at Feyenoord – where there was a prurient fascination with his struggles – suggests that he will face the same relentless scrutiny wherever he goes. In the modern world, more gratuitous pleasure than ever is taken in the fall of those who were once great. It will be a circus if he pitches up somewhere new.

But the evidence of that work with boys in London suggests that he has far more of a hinterland to turn to than Alli and his ilk, if only he can re-establish those positive influences and seek help.

A moment in time, precisely two years ago, proves what his future might look like. At a meeting room in King's Cross, Sterling sat with a group of seven young men, all of whom were deemed at risk of drifting into knife crime and who had been sent along to his foundation by Brent's youth offending service.

After an initial 15 or 20 minutes when the boys seemed blinded by the superstar in their midst, they became more comfortable, seeming to feel he understood the likes of them. There were smiles as he described the various school social groups he knew. 'The cool group, the middle of the road group and the geeks,' as he called them. The 'geeks' always seemed to be a bigger part of his world when exams approached, he said with a grin. The room was wreathed with laughter, then.

The group session evolved into several one-to-one conversations between him and the boys who'd been more reticent. It was then that he pointed out the value of allowing outside mentors to help you, if your home life is challenging. 'Having someone else involved changed the course of my life,' Sterling told them.

Now is the moment when Sterling needs others to help change the course of his own life and if he accepts that, he could play a part in public life again. First comes the challenge of rousing himself from any lingering pity, emerging from the celebrity bubble and appreciating that he is supremely lucky to remain a free man after those abominable, mindless few hours in his souped-up car.

BitMEX co-founder warned the AI sector will implode, pushing talent back to crypto

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Maelstrom Fund's Arthur Hayes said bitcoin could test $50,000 if the Nasdaq keeps falling after seven straight weeks of declines

Arthur Hayes, co-founder of crypto exchange BitMEX, expects startup talent concentrated in artificial intelligence to return to crypto "when AI implodes," he told CNBC in an online interview on Tuesday.

Hayes, chief investment officer at Maelstrom Fund, said bitcoin could retest $50,000 should the Nasdaq $NDAQ extend its slide.

"A lot of talent has gone into the AI space, so I definitely think there'll be a resurgence in good founder talent coming back to crypto," Hayes said, according to CNBC. He also suggested that certain founders might head back to crypto after they "recognize" that Anthropic or OpenAI could "usurp" what they have built.

Hayes said the biggest frontier AI labs are so large and capable that it is hard to "build a real AI business." In his view, "most" founders "raise a bunch of money and then light it on fire."

He expects the AI trade to run into "serious headwinds" during the latter half of 2027, as leading labs start to owe payments on leases for computing capacity and similar commitments that cannot be canceled. Anthropic projects around $451.6 billion in obligations for compute and infrastructure through 2030, and about $70.7 billion of that falls due by the close of September 2027, according to its prospectus.

"Do they have the money? We're going to find out," Hayes said.

Bitcoin slid to a low of $55,142.16 last Saturday and has barely bounced back since. It has dropped for seven weeks in a row, a run that has happened only twice before in its history, according to CNBC. The cryptocurrency peaked at a record $126,279.63 in October 2025.

Hayes called the current market moves a "correlation-led liquidation." Bitcoin and gold have both stayed tied to the Nasdaq so far, he said. "Stuff goes down, you sell crypto first because it's easy, and then you sell everything else," he said, according to CNBC.

Hayes said his preferred scenario is a bitcoin bounce from roughly $55,000 even as the Nasdaq continues to slide. That split would indicate markets are drifting away from a general sell-off and toward pricing in "reflation," as the Federal Reserve supplies additional liquidity, he said.

Once that pattern shifts, Hayes said, Zcash would draw his interest, a token he characterized as a "high beta bitcoin" that places more emphasis on privacy. He named ENA, the token of Ethereum-based platform Ethena, as the other holding he is watching. A bitcoin rally could strengthen returns from funding rates and the futures basis, which would swell Ethena's income and give its USDe synthetic dollar broader appeal, he said.

Hayes had predicted in October that the AI data center buildout would overshoot and crash, with bitcoin rising afterward. He said then that data center providers would seek payment when new capacity is delivered, a point he placed in late 2027 or 2028.

Ezra Frech Reacts to Prosthetic Falling Off Mid-Dance During DWTS

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Before Ezra Frech and Daniella Karagach were crowned the Dancing With the Stars season 35 winners, they seamlessly navigated Frech’s prosthetic leg falling off mid-dance.

There was a moment Ezra Frech didn’t have a leg to stand on—but he had a ballroom supporting him.

After the Paralympian’s prosthetic leg came off during his freestyle dance alongside partner Daniella Karagach on the Dancing With the Stars Nov. 24 finale, he revealed what went through his head before seamlessly reattaching the limb with the help of his partner as well as pro Alan Bersten.

“The leg just wasn't on correctly, and I felt it,” he told Variety following the episode, which saw him and Karagach crowned as the season’s winners. “Sometimes it'll readjust and I'll be okay. And so, as I was running up the steps, I'm like, ‘There's a 50 percent chance my leg will resuction and I'll be okay.’ And that wasn't the case.”

Nevertheless, he was ready to do what had to be done to finish the dance. After all, it was a moment he and Karagach had long been preparing for.

“I was just like, ‘Okay, I'm gonna do the rest of the dance on one leg. Like I'll do the entire thing on one leg,’” the 21-year-old continued. “She's drilled in me since the beginning that no matter what happens, we keep pushing through. You never give up, and you fight for it. And in this case, regardless of the circumstance, I was gonna do whatever I can to fight for it.”

Thanks to the quick thinking of his pro, however, Frech was able to get a leg back up the competition—literally. During a brief pause in the dance, Karagach appeared to yell something to her partner as she handed him his prosthetic, allowing Frech a few beats to reattach the limb.

“I heard her say, ‘Put your leg on,’” he recalled, “and I was like, ‘Yes, okay, yeah, good idea. I'll put it on right now.’”

While Karagach had the utmost faith in her partner’s ability to persevere, she knew  taking the time to reattach the limb was worth it.

“I was just like, there is no other place in the routine that he can do this right now, and he cannot do the whole routine on one leg,” she explained. “So, I was like, ‘Just put your leg on.’ And it's not like—we had one little trick in there that he did on one, like a 540. It was really impressive. It was really great. But nothing's better than him just clicking on his leg properly and continuing on with the dance. I was like, ‘This is our only shot to do that.’”

The moment was so adrenaline inducing, however, that Frech admitted he didn’t quite remember how his prosthetic made it back onto his body.

“Did somebody give me the leg? Because I blacked out,” he asked aloud, to which Karagach confirmed she did. “You gave me the leg? Really?”

Thankfully, those few seconds were the only thing Frech needed.

“I clicked it on,” he said, “and when I put it on the second time. I was like, ‘Ooh it's on now, we're good.’”

And good he was, with he and Karagach crowned the season 35 winners by the end of the episode—an accomplishment as good as gold for Frech.

"This is the most special moment of my life," he told the audience. "Thank you, everybody, for voting. Thank you for believing in us. Oh my God."

Why Indians are still ending up in Russia's war

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Whether through deception or desperation, Indians continue to be drawn into Russia's war in Ukraine. What can New Delhi do to protect its citizens from ending up fighting in Ukraine?

Gurpreet Singh Randhawa, a 40-year-old from Jalandhar in India's northern Punjab state, said he traveled to Russia for work after responding to a Facebook advertisement for a driving job at a garment factory.

But instead of finding work as a driver, he was sent to fight in Ukraine for the Russian armed forces.

After escaping from his unit, he surrendered to Ukrainian forces in November. He is now being held as a prisoner of war.

In an interview with Ukrainian YouTuber Volodymyr Zolkin, Randhawa described how, after arriving in Russia, he was taken to a military facility, made to sign a contract and sent for training. He said they confiscated his passport and phone. His account has not been independently verified.

Randhawa's story is a familiar one in India.

He is the second Indian national known to have escaped the Russian military. Sahil Majothi, a 23-year-old from Gujarat, surrendered to Ukrainian forces in October 2025. He had joined the Russian military while serving a prison sentence in Russia, reportedly believing that military service would help him secure his release.

The cases underline how little has changed since Prime Minister Narendra Modi raised the issue with Russian President Vladimir Putin during his visit to Moscow in July 2024.

At the time, Putin assured him that Indians who were misled into joining the Russian army would be discharged. But the recruitment of Indians has continued.

In an October filing before the Supreme Court, India's Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) said approximately 267 Indians have joined the Russian armed forces since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

Of these, 139 were discharged and 51 were killed. The whereabouts of the 77 others were unknown. Russian authorities have confirmed that seven of them are missing.

The problem extends beyond Indians who travel to Russia seeking civilian employment, only to find themselves exposed to recruitment networks and the dangers of a war zone.

The MEA has also highlighted cases of Indians knowingly enlisting in the Russian military for the supposed financial benefits on offer, believing they could later secure release through government intervention.

Some had also approached Indian authorities to renounce their citizenship and obtain Russian passports. "This is a dangerous path and is strongly discouraged by the government," the ministry said.

Former diplomat Anil Trigunayat said the growing movement of Indian workers to Russia presents both economic opportunities and new challenges for their protection.

"Inadequate oversight of recruitment networks could leave vulnerable workers exposed to exploitation," Trigunayat told DW.

He said Indian workers were often burdened by substantial recruitment fees and continued deductions from their salaries by intermediaries.

"Some of them had paid between 300,000 to 500,000 Indian rupees [$5,400 /€4,900] to Indian recruitment agents, and even while working in Russia, more than 30% of their agreed monthly salary was taken away by local and foreign agents," Trigunayat said.

The former diplomat added that some Indians who had overstayed their visas in Russia were particularly vulnerable. Military service was allegedly offered to earn substantial wages and regularize their immigration status.

Mohammad Sufiyan, from Hyderabad in the southern state of Telangana, said he was lured to Russia under false pretences and forced into active combat in Ukraine.

He had traveled abroad in search of employment and a better livelihood, but ended up in a war zone. He has since returned home to India.

"Many people are duped. Some decide it is better to seek citizenship than come back to India. It's a tough life, and people will go to any lengths to get some sort of employment," Sufiyan told DW.

Sufiyan's experience points to the economic desperation that makes such offers attractive, even when they carry the risk of being caught up in a war.

Rajan Kumar, a professor at the Centre for Russian and Central Asian Studies at Jawaharlal Nehru University's School of International Studies, said some Indians who traveled to Russia for work could find themselves caught up in the fighting without having joined the military.

"Some of them, they don't join the army, but they end up in the army region. They're caught in the crossfire," Kumar told DW.

He said India was negotiating with Moscow to secure the return of its citizens caught up in such situations. "Russia is very cooperative so far," he said.

India has been pressing Russia to take action, but the issue persists.

The recruitment efforts reflect Russia's need for more manpower for its nearly five-year war in Ukraine , and the limited influence New Delhi can exert over Moscow, despite the close political relationship between the two countries.

Mohsin Raza Khan, a professor at the Jindal School of International Affairs, told DW that Putin's need to sustain the war effort was taking precedence over the concerns of a strategic partner.

He points out that the Russian president was reluctant to impose another mobilization on his own population for fear of domestic unrest.

"In these circumstances, sustaining the war effort appears to take precedence over concerns raised by India. Neither Modi nor New Delhi has sufficient leverage to change that," said Khan.

Khan also said the willingness of some Indians to enlist, and even consider giving up their citizenship, is down to the lack of decent employment opportunities at home.

"For young, educated men without contacts or connections, the chances of respectable employment are so low that the risks of a high casualty war are preferable," he said.
