Many leafy plants we now grow as houseplants evolved in hot and humid rainforests which is why, now fall’s here and the heat’s turned on, the dry air in our homes is leading to less-than-lovely foliage. So, if you're wondering how to keep houseplants healthy in winter so you can enjoy lush, glossy leaves, you're not alone. Thankfully, a humidity tray is the quick and easy DIY solution that will speed your houseplants back to happy in time for the holidays, and it won't cost you a dime.

It’s a cruel fact that it’s the plants with the biggest or boldest leaves that struggle most in the dry air of our winter-heated homes: adapting to life in a rainforest is exactly why the foliage is so fabulous in the first place. Beneath a dense forest canopy, sunlight is a precious commodity so some plants like Elephant Ear (Alocasia) grow huge leaves to catch as much light as possible. Others such as peace lilies has foliage densely packed with chlorophyll to absorb as much of the scarce light as possible, and that's what creates their intense, deep green hue. Some like Calathea have purple or red undersides to their leaves precisely so that precious light gets bounced back into the leaf.

So, if you want to enjoy these dramatic leaves at their best, you have to provide these houseplants with the humidity level they need to flourish. Any plant likely suffering from a lack of humidity at this time of the year is easy to spot, starting with any houseplants with brown leaf tips or edges, smaller or discolored leaves, or slow growth. Now, to restore these ailing houseplants to lush, glossy growth, you could treat them to a small humidifier like the Homedics Humidifier from Amazon. Or you could simply DIY a humidity tray with stuff you’ve probably already got in your backyard.

A humidity tray, also called a pebble tray, is a shallow container that’s wider than the plant pot it serves, filled with small pebbles and water. The plant pot sits on the pebbles above the water level so instead of water being pulled up into the potting soil, it evaporates into the air. The higher the heat in your home, and so the drier the air, the more water evaporates to create a humid microclimate around your houseplant.

An old plant saucer is perfect, or if you’d like something more decorative for your houseplant display, look in your kitchen cupboards for a deep plate or shallow dish. Fill this with fine gravel or small pebbles – pea gravel is perfect and you can buy bagged pea gravel pebbles from Home Depot very cheaply if you don’t have a gravel path or driveway to raid.

Then simply stand the plant pot on top of your DIY humidity tray and keep the water topped up, making sure the water level stays below the base of the pot.

Grouping your houseplants together will also help to raise the humidity. Clustering plants together creates an indoor microclimate because the water vapor that’s lost through the leaves diffuses more slowly – and this effect is amplified if even one or two of the grouped houseplants have humidity trays.

Houseplants that have evolved to live in the damp environments of rainforests and woodland are most sensitive to humidity. These common houseplants all thrive in 55-80% humidity and will benefit from a DIY pebble tray:

If you grow any of these houseplants, then a $5 room thermometer like this from Amazon that measures humidity levels as well as temperature, and records the minimum and maximum levels in the past 24 hours, is a really useful tool. You’ll be able to see exactly what the humidity levels are and, if they’re too low, take action before the plant tells you it’s an issue with brown leaf tips. Already made the plant a pebble tray? To raise the humidity further, simply DIY a wider one to increase the surface area of water available to evaporate.

Many more adaptable houseplants won’t be as dramatic about a less-than-ideal humidity level, but add a little more moisture to the air and they’ll grow faster and produce bigger leaves with a better color and shine. Making a DIY pebble tray will help all these plants to flourish:

These plants all thrive in humidity levels of 40-60%. While that's likely within the average humidity of a home in summer, it's a different story in winter. Because cold air can't hold as much water, and home heating dries it further, home moisture levels typically drop to 20-40%. While that lack of humidity isn't enough to kill these adaptable plants, it will certainly prevent them from looking their best.

Leaves with brown tips and outer edges are the most common sign that a houseplant is struggling in low humidity levels and is losing moisture from its foliage faster than the roots can replace it. All plants release water vapor from tiny pores on their leaves and, in dry air, that moisture is sucked out at a faster rate. The plant parts furthest away from the roots supplying the water are the first to run out of moisture, which is why leaf tips are the first to dry out to a crispy brown. At this time of the year, it's very common to see houseplants with long, narrow leaves like spider plants with brown leaf tips. Low humidity is also an easily-avoided reason why peace lily flowers turn brown.

There are other symptoms to look out for too, such as slow growth and smaller new leaves. While many plants naturally produce aerial roots, if you spot a sudden escalation in their number, the houseplant is likely seeking more humidity. And if you find that the potting soil dries out super-quickly after watering, too-dry air may be the cause.

Misting houseplants provide a quick fix but the spike in humidity only lasts for a very short time, whereas a humidity tray delivers constant moisture to create a more permanent microclimate. To match the humidity levels provided by a pebble tray, you’d need to mist houseplants every hour, every day, every night. Misting can also create a breeding ground for fungal and bacterial disease by leaving water sitting on foliage.

In dry or arid zones like Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico or Colorado, your houseplants may need humidity trays topped up year-round. You’ll likely see even plants that don’t require very high air moisture levels developing brown tips, which is a sure sign that they’ll benefit from a humidity tray.

At the other end of the scale, if you live in a high-humidity climate like Louisiana, Mississippi or Florida, only the houseplants that are highly dependent on humidity need a pebble tray – unless your home is equipped with an efficient air-conditioning system that dehumidifies, and dependant on your winter home-heat level, of course. Unnecessary standing water in a warm, damp home puts plants at a higher risk of pests and fungal disease.

Gifting many of your houseplants a DIY humidity tray will help them to flourish in our heated homes through fall and winter, and happy plants grow leaves that are larger and lusher, greener and glossier. Along with a cheap thermometer that displays air moisture percentages, your DIY pebble tray makes it very easy to adjust humidity levels, too. Keep the trays topped up with water for maximum humidity, and gradually reduce if needs be once your home's heat is turned off. You can even leave the trays water-free over summer if natural humidity is high enough in your climate, and let your DIY pebble saucers serve as stylish houseplant decor for a personal touch, until the heat's turned back on.

Manchester United have appointed Mauricio Pochettino as their new manager.

The club confirmed their decision to sack Michael Carrick after just 10 games of the season on Thursday, with the club dwindling in 18th heading into the international break.

The Independent reported that contact was made with United States national team (USMNT) manager Pochettino over succeeding Carrick on Monday and the Argentinian, who previously managed Tottenham, Chelsea and Southampton, has now sealed his return to the Premier League.

United have paid US Soccer compensation worth an initial $7m (£5.3m) to get Pochettino out of his existing contract, which ran until the 2030 World Cup. Additional compensation will be required for Pochettino’s trusted coaching staff, including long-time assistant Jesus Perez.

"The opportunity to represent the USA crest was one of the greatest honors of my career,” Pochettino said after his departure and move to Man United was confirmed.

“We are so grateful to the players for their commitment, to the fans for their belief, and to the Federation for always supporting our staff and creating the opportunities to impact the culture of soccer in this great country,” Pochettino said. “We want to thank Cindy Parlow Cone, JT Batson and Dan Helfrich for their partnership and incredible working relationships throughout our time here.

“There is tremendous talent in the U.S. player pool from the youth teams through to the senior team, and it’s one of the reasons we have so much belief for the future of U.S. Soccer. You will always have a special place in our hearts, and we will be supporting the teams whenever they step on the field."

He will remain in charge for the United States’ two Concacaf Nations League fixtures against Haiti during the international break, with an away match on 14 November and a home match in Cincinnati on 18 November.

Pochettino will then report to Carrington shortly after, with his official start date being on 18 November, ahead their next Premier League match against Liverpool at Anfield on 22 November.

While losing Pochettino is blow for the USA, The Independent understands that US Soccer has been prepared for the prospect of his exit at some stage before the 2030 World Cup, with a succession plan already in place.

Pochettino arrives at Old Trafford with the club in crisis yet again, having won just once in the Premier League this season, against Ipswich Town, and gained only eight points from the first 10 matches.

Carrick still held goodwill with the fanbase before his sacking despite the dire run of form, with frustration targeted at the ownership and the board. United bosses nevertheless called time on his reign after the latest round of crisis talks in Monaco this week.

Pochettino last managed in the English top-flight during the 2023/24 season, when he guided Chelsea to a sixth-place finish despite spending a lot of the campaign struggling in mid-table. He left by mutual consent at the end of the season.

Between his five-year spell at Tottenham - where he led Spurs to the Champions League final in 2019 - and his single term at Stamford Bridge, Pochettino spent 18 months at Paris Saint-Germain, winning the Ligue 1 title in 2021/22.

He delivered a run to the last-16 for the USA at this summer’s home World Cup, though his side did not escape controversy after striker Folarin Balogun’s red card was controversially overturned following a call from Donald Trump to Fifa president Gianni Infantino.

10 Great HBO Shows With Endings That Change Everything

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The following article contains spoilers.No matter how many seasons it lasts, what themes it focuses on, or even which genre it exists within, every television series must eventually reckon with how to execute a meaningful finale. Many series opt for a cathartic wind-down, a safe and comfortable ending that offers closure to the audience. However, some series do something a little different, opting to strive for more ambitious conclusions that have a more dramatic effect on viewers.

Many of the series included ended with bold and brash big swings that completely reframed how viewers regarded the show. Others misfired as underwhelming resolutions to iconic titles, the backlash of which completely changed how television as a medium regards the art of delivering a climactic episode and season. All of these finales have made an undeniable impact on the format and its fans, and all stand as noteworthy chapters in HBO’s impressive and extended stay as the flagship brand of television excellence in the 21st century.

It is a conclusive shift that isn’t necessarily restricted to the series finale. After all, a tone of realism and uncertainty permeates the entire five-season run, but The Wire’s conclusion does reframe the series quite substantially. Revolving around the complex criminal ecosystem of Baltimore, spanning from the street-level drug dealers to the politics of City Hall, The Wire delivers an all-encompassing, novelistic epic of modern American crime, one laced with dozens of focal characters on both sides of the law.

Towards the end of the final season, it begins to become clear that creator David Simon has no intention of wrapping character arcs up in a neat little bow that will give viewers a sense of closure. Instead, The Wire ends as a grim meditation on the cyclical nature of organized crime, institutional corruption, and bureaucratic inefficiency. In the context of the series itself, it’s a thought-provoking conclusion about the nature of society and the way things both change and stay the same over time. Pertaining more to television at large, however, The Wire’s resolution proved that major series don’t need to end with clarity or cliffhangers to be compelling.

The air of infamy that surrounded Game of Thrones thanks to its final season has begun to dissipate — the success of House of the Dragon and A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms has been influential in this — but the impact of what the epic fantasy series actually changed is still being felt. This isn’t a reference to the plotting of the story, but rather to the way television as a medium changed in the wake of Game of Thrones’ finale and the outpouring of audience disgust.

It shifted the paradigm of television storytelling. Suddenly, it wasn’t enough to run with an enticing premise and figure it out on the go. Showrunners and writers had to approach major, ongoing series with a mind to how the story would end, or risk facing strong backlash from the fanbase. Granted, some major series haven’t learned the lesson in the ensuing years, and the nature of television storytelling was already evolving due to the rampant rise in streaming services, but Game of Thrones’ conclusion has proven to be a cautionary tale of negligence and subversion, with most shows since opting to conclude in a manner that honors central themes and character dynamics of their respective stories.

Barry is one of those series that has come out since the fallout of Game of Thrones, one where the lessons HBO learned from its failure have been most evident. After four seasons following the violent hitman struggling to transition from a life of crime to a career on the stage, Barry ends with a poignant sense of inevitable tragedy that dismantles the antihero redemption trope, then closes with a 10-year time jump that sees Barry’s (Bill Hader) life being recontextualized and completely misinterpreted in an adoring Hollywood biopic.

The thematic notes that Barry flaunted throughout the four seasons are beautifully handled in “wow,” which also adds a healthy dose of social commentary at the expense of glamorizing entertainment and the audiences that embrace it. The series as a whole is redefined in its final minutes, pivoting from a black comedy crime thriller to a scorching indictment of Hollywood sanitization. With every major character also facing appropriate ends to their arcs, Barry’s finale is a masterclass in how to end a story with thematic punch and emotional deftness.

There have been plenty of series that have completely changed their own nature in their finale; some have even altered the fabric of television with their resolutions, but few have done what Curb Your Enthusiasm did with its swan song outing. The hit comedy series follows the sensationalized life of Larry David, the producer of Seinfeld, as he drifts through Hollywood aimlessly. After 12 seasons and 120 episodes, it ends with Larry on trial in Atlanta, with “No Lessons Learned” delivering a meta recreation of Seinfeld’s infamous and divisive finale.

It’s clever, endearing, and cathartic for all who felt undermined by Seinfeld’s finale, especially as it ends on a twist that sees Larry walk free, leading him and Jerry Seinfeld to declare that would have been a better conclusion for their hit ‘90s sitcom. It’s an admission that Seinfeld was flawed that also serves as a perfect finale for Curb Your Enthusiasm in its own right, making it possibly the only TV show ever made with a finale that has directly changed the perception of a separate series.

Criminally underrated, Show Me a Hero is a breathtaking political drama that uses the tragic true story of the youngest mayor of Yonkers, New York, to explore the complexities of desegregation, the cost of political conviction, and how grassroots humanity clashes violently with institutional power. Oscar Isaac is brilliant as Nick Wasicsko, with much of the miniseries revolving around his desire to embrace a low-income housing project that many of his constituents are viciously opposed to.

The miniseries adamantly rejects the comfort of white savior heroics, delving into the grim reality of the true story that sees Wasicsko grow paranoid after losing an election and eventually commit suicide. It shatters the tone of an idealistic, progressive politician navigating the red tape of city hall, highlighting Wasicsko’s egotistical knack for defining his self-worth through his career as a damning fault, while also illustrating the callous, arduous nature of true systemic change.

The Leftovers is one of those series that feels like everything changes every episode. While it is a slow and meditative show, the depth it invests in its characters constantly brings new revelations to the fold, not only in their own lives as they navigate heartache, frustration, and rage, but in accordance with overarching story themes of grief, trauma, and the human condition as well. Overall, The Leftovers unfolds in a world reeling from the “Sudden Departure”, an inexplicable event in which two percent of the world’s population vanished in an instant.

The series finale, “The Book of Nora,” transpires years after the rest of the series, exploring an uneasy reunion between Nora (Carrie Coon) and Kevin (Justin Theroux). It revels in the surrealist intensity and compassionate dramatic might that defined the series at its best, ending on an unproven explanation as to what caused the Sudden Departure, then leans on empathy and choice in its final moments, rather than a traditional, plot-centered resolution. It’s a bold decision, but it perfectly suits the series’ overarching tone and brings touching closure to Kevin and Nora’s relationship.

Blending corporate satire with engrossing, high-stakes drama, Succession fast became one of HBO’s greatest ever series in its exploration of the vying for power that erupts between three siblings when their father, the aging founder and head of a multi-billion-dollar multimedia company, signals his intent to retire. It is a manic fight for power and position with no blow being deemed below the belt, and yet the fourth and final season still ends in a manner that leaves viewers numb and shocked.

The kinship the Roy siblings display as they stand against Lukas Matsson’s (Alexander Skarsgård) efforts to buy Waystar RoyCo gives way to total desperation and betrayal. It proves that the inherited wealth and influence the Roys flaunted made them incapable of ever following in their father’s footsteps, and their festering animosity with one another, as their shared childhood trauma went unresolved, was instrumental in them fragmenting and losing everything they wanted. It is quite brilliant how a series that features such unlikable and conniving characters leaves the audience feeling hollow, if not completely rattled, at their failure.

Based on Gillian Flynn’s novel of the same name, Sharp Objects is a methodical, meandering crime thriller that anchors its drama in Camille Preaker’s (Amy Adams) investigation of the murder of two young girls and the inner turmoil she experiences as she returns to her hometown of Wind Gap and is confronted with the ghosts of her traumatic childhood. It holds a captivating Southern Gothic atmosphere, a litany of exceptional performances, and a macabre balance of character drama and murder mystery, but its greatest asset is the festering sense of dreadful unpredictability it exudes throughout the entirety of its eight-episode run.

Even with viewers accustomed to its volatile plot twists, Sharp Objects completely changes the nature of its story in its final episode. In fact, it does so many times. The most striking of which comes in the final minute of the series, revealing a horrifying truth that shatters Camille’s sense of self-growth and safety. Accompanied by a credits sequence intercut with shots that force the audience to bask in the terrifying truth, Sharp Objects delivers an unforgettable ending that elevates it from a gripping psychological thriller to a masterclass in nightmarish suspense.

Six Feet Under is essentially a tale of mortality and the human condition wrapped up in a dark comedy about the dysfunctional Fisher family and their management of a funeral home in the aftermath of their patriarch’s death. It’s funny, often poignant and sad, always thought-provoking and compelling, and it ends with what is arguably the greatest series finale in the history of television, a feat of storytelling steeped in catharsis, closure, and beautiful thematic touch.

Within the context of the series itself, it recalibrated all the death and drama as being a simple fact within the wonder of life, transforming the show from being an acidic satirical dramedy to a powerful ode to family and existence itself. Its flash-forward montage and evocative use of Sia’s “Breath Me” only add to the emotional resolution. Through this, Six Feet Under even redefined the commercial possibilities for television, with the episode marking a major breakthrough for Sia that proved a perfect needle-drop moment could define a series’ legacy for decades to come.

It should come as no surprise that The Sopranos’ finale altered the course of television history. After all, the series itself did so from its very first episode. Whether it was through presenting such a complex and challenging antihero in Tony Soprano (James Gandolfini), pioneering long-form serialized drama on the small screen, or simply through its stronger appetite for graphic material, The Sopranos revolutionized the modern age of television drama, and its finale marks another major evolutionary step for storytelling in the medium.

It is iconic in its own right. The diner, the uneasy tension, Journey’s “Don’t Stop Believin’”, and, of course, the cut to black. It was divisive at the time, but it has come to be heralded as a stroke of genius. Almost 20 years on, people still discuss what they think happened to Tony, but from the perspective of television drama, it proved that series didn’t necessarily need to end with neat, conclusive closure to be rewarding. It proved ambiguity had a huge part to play in 21st-century television; it redefined how audiences engaged with television (especially as it coincided with the rising popularity of the internet and social media), and it remains one of the best and boldest TV finales of all time.
