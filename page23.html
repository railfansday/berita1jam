Hong Kong: Fire Anniversary Underscores Growing Repression

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A memorial near the site of the deadly fire at the residential complex at Wang Fuk Court in Tai Po, Hong Kong, December 2, 2025. © 2025 Ng Han Guan/AP Photo

(London) – The Hong Kong government should ensure meaningful accountability for the devastating fire that killed 168 people one year ago and end its suppression of peaceful criticism, Human Rights Watch said today.

On November 26, 2025, Hong Kong’s deadliest fire in decades engulfed seven residential high-rise buildings at Wang Fuk Court, Tai Po. The authorities initiated an independent investigation and arrested three dozen people implicated in crimes. The Housing Minister resigned. But the authorities have also threatened and arbitrarily arrested those engaging in criticism and activism concerning the fire.

“The Hong Kong government responded to the horrific Wang Fuk Court fire by using Beijing’s tactics of shutting out the voices of the victims and promising an elusive accountability,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “The authorities need to recognize that the only way to prevent similar tragedies in the future is to allow people to speak out safely and hold those responsible fully accountable.”

The authorities have engaged in apparent acts of repression of free expression in the weeks ahead of the anniversary:

The media reported in November that the police made inquiries with Catholic churches planning masses to commemorate victims of the fire.A church in Tai Po subsequently removed references to the fire from its announcement and instead described the service as a mass for “the brothers and sisters who died in the past year;” hundreds nevertheless attended.A priest at another Catholic church posted on Facebook that three police officers had inquired about a planned memorial mass marking the anniversary, even though the church had not publicly announced the service.On November 22, a group working with migrant workers who survived the fire cancelled a book launch and drama event scheduled ahead of the anniversary, citing “unforeseen circumstances.” On November 23, the online news platform Boom News said it would remove some videos featuring interviews with survivors, quoting the whistleblower Edward Snowden on “censorship and fear.” The Boom News founder, Tang Ho-wing, had been arrested in September for alleged “sedition” for reporting on another politically sensitive anniversary.

Police Commissioner Joe Chow said police routinely asked organizers about upcoming public events, while decisions on whether to proceed “rested with the organizers themselves.”

Amid continued restrictions on freedom of expression and peaceful assembly in Hong Kong, Hong Kong communities around the world have planned commemorations of the fire.

Deep concerns surrounding the 2026 anniversary follow a year-long government crackdown on criticism, reporting, and civil society-organizing related to the fire. Immediately following the fire, hundreds of people gathered near Wang Fuk Court to donate and distribute supplies to victims, but as the volunteer effort grew, police told them to leave and government workers took over the site.

Authorities targeted people calling for accountability. Just four days after the fire, national security police reportedly arrested a university student, Miles Kwan, for “seditious intent” after he initiated an online petition calling for an independent investigation into the fire, and two others on suspicion of “inciting hatred against the government.”

Hong Kong and Beijing authorities subsequently accused “foreign forces” and “anti-China destabilizing forces” of using the fire to “incite hatred against the government.” Within hours of these announcements, two prominent online commentators, Ellie Yuen and Hailey Cheng, who had been frequently quoted in foreign media, said they would stop reporting on the fire.

National security police also arrested the online commentator Wong On-yin in December after he planned to hold a news conference about the Wang Fuk Court fire and discussed on YouTube his subsequent questioning by police. The authorities prosecuted him for allegedly obstructing a national security investigation and publishing “seditious material.” He remains in custody awaiting a verdict in January 2027.

Between April and May 2026, the authorities barred Wang Fuk Court residents from displaying banners from their homes or being accompanied by journalists as they were briefly permitted to return to their apartments to retrieve belongings.

Also in May, the authorities arrested the former Wang Fuk Court owners’ committee member Kong Cheung-fat and his wife on allegations of fraud and money laundering. Kong had joined other owners in seeking a meeting to discuss insurance and other post-fire issues. While out on bail, he said he would no longer give interviews or comment publicly. Some residents said his arrest made them more cautious about speaking publicly.

An independent committee, set up by Hong Kong Chief Executive John Lee, published a report on October 29 that found wide-ranging regulatory failures across four government bodies, including inadequate oversight of renovation work and fire safety, weaknesses in building maintenance regulation, and supervisory failures by contractors and other professionals. That day, Lee and senior officials formally apologized to the victims of the fire and their families while promising to implement “systemic reforms.” Housing Secretary Winnie Ho later stepped down from office while the authorities arrested 35 people in connection with the fire and possible corruption in the renovation project.

“An apology and promises of reform are no substitute for ensuring that people can freely question and criticize the authorities,” Pearson said. “The Hong Kong government should act on the independent committee’s recommendations and provide adequate compensation and other support for victims and their families.”

From Files to Cited Answers in One API Call: Managed Ingestion and SearchDocuments in Agent Retrieval

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A guide to the new managed ingestion and SearchDocuments features in Agent Retrieval, for searching PDFs, Office files, and videos without writing an ingestion pipeline.

Before an agent can answer a question about your PDFs, someone has to build the pipeline: parse every file, split it into chunks, embed each chunk, write the chunks to a vector database, and then learn that database's schema to query it. For video it gets harder: transcode the files, cut them into clips, embed the frames, and keep track of which second of which video each clip came from.

Most of that code has nothing to do with your application. Two features recently added to Agent Retrieval (formerly Vector Search 2.0), now in Public Preview, take that work off your hands. Managed ingestion builds the pipeline for you: point a data store at files in Cloud Storage, and the service parses, segments, and embeds them with Gemini Embedding 2. SearchDocuments, the second new feature, then searches the data store with one API call and returns the matching passages with their page numbers or timestamps. In our tests, 18 Alphabet investor PDFs went from a public Cloud Storage bucket to searchable, page cited results in about 9 minutes, and 19 minutes of video became searchable by both visuals and spoken audio in 0.3 to 0.5 seconds per query, with no parsing, chunking, transcription, or embedding code.

This guide covers how to create a data store, import documents and videos, and search them with filters, reranking, and audio-aware video search.

Pairing managed ingestion with SearchDocuments gives you several immediate benefits:

Agent Retrieval Collections already give you semantic, text, and hybrid search over your own data objects. But you have to produce those data objects yourself, and to query them you need to know how they are stored. Here is what changes when you let a data store do the work:

The backing Collection is still a regular Agent Retrieval Collection, so you can query it directly whenever you need full control.

A data store holds your files and the configuration that controls how they are parsed and segmented. The data store and SearchDocuments APIs are currently served over REST at v1beta, so the examples use an authorized session from google-auth:

Two segmentation modes are available. Chunk segmentation (the default) splits text into chunks of 100 to 500 tokens and works with layout parsing, which also detects tables and headings. Page segmentation produces one segment per page. For paginated documents such as PDFs, both modes record page numbers (startPage and endPage) so you can cite pages or filter by page range: page segmentation returns a single page per segment, while chunk segmentation returns the page range each chunk spans.

Point an import at your files with URI patterns such as gs://my-bucket/reports/*.pdf, or with a JSONL manifest when you want to set your own document IDs and attach metadata:

The import runs as a long-running operation. Re-importing the same files updates existing documents instead of duplicating them, and files whose content didn't change are skipped.

SearchDocuments needs only the data store and a text query:

Each result is a segment of a document, with the text, the page, and the source file:

Pass filter to search only the segments that match, using the same paths you read back in results:

You can also pass document_ids to search only inside specific files, and output_fields.metadata to return each document's metadata with its results. In our test, searching "What did the CEO say about AI?" with the page-1 filter returned the CEO quotes from the headline page of five different earnings releases.

For "How many employees does Alphabet have?", plain hybrid search returned two balance sheet pages among the top five, which don't mention employees at all. With reranking on, every top result reported the headcount: the annual report's "Alphabet had 190,234 employees" and the "Number of employees" row of the quarterly supplemental tables.

Video works the same way: create a data store with document_type: VIDEO, import the files, and call SearchDocuments. The service transcodes each video, splits it into fixed windows, and embeds each window:

Each result carries the moment it matched:

We imported five Google sample videos (about 19 minutes in total) and searched them with scene descriptions. "Cucumbers being sorted by a machine" returned 03:20-03:40 of the machine learning video, where a farm's sorting machine grades cucumbers, without any tags or captions. Searches returned in 0.3 to 0.5 seconds.

By default (audio_mode: DISABLED), only the frames are embedded, so a query matches what is shown. With ENABLED, the first audio track is embedded together with the frames. We imported the same videos into two data stores and asked both about statements from a keynote, spoken while the picture shows the speaker:

Only the audio-enabled data store found the right moments.

The rule of thumb: If your data is files, let a data store ingest them and search with SearchDocuments. Drop down to the Collection only when you need control the data store doesn't give you.

Managed ingestion doesn't change what good retrieval needs: well-segmented content, good embeddings, and the right filters. What changes is who builds it. Instead of maintaining parsers, chunkers, embedding jobs, and a schema, you point a data store at a bucket and get back segments that are ready to cite.

For agents, that means a retrieval tool that returns where each answer came from, a page in a PDF or a moment in a video, with one API call.

Try it yourself: the document notebook ingests 18 Alphabet investor PDFs and walks through filters and reranking, and the video notebook searches five sample videos with an inline player and compares audio modes.

Building Self-Checkout at Keells: Challenges Beyond the Checkout Screen

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

During my time at Keells, one of Sri Lanka’s leading supermarket chains, I had the opportunity to work on the company’s early self-checkout implementation.

Having worked extensively with our existing Point-of-Sale (POS) system, I was familiar with many of the requirements of supermarket operations. However, moving from a cashier-operated checkout to a self-service environment brought a different set of challenges.

We already had an established POS system that supported a wide range of business functions. Introducing self-checkout meant finding ways to retain those functions while making the shopping experience simpler for customers.

I wanted to share a few of the challenges we faced during this project, particularly those that required us to look beyond the technical implementation.

One of the more interesting problems I worked on was product pricing.

In Sri Lanka, it is possible to have two batches of the same product on a supermarket shelf with different marked prices, even though both have the same barcode.

At a traditional checkout, the cashier can check the price marked on the product and handle the situation accordingly. With self-checkout, the system cannot determine which marked price is printed on the individual item simply by scanning its barcode.

We needed a way to handle this without making the checkout process difficult for customers.

Introducing another screen or asking customers to request assistance was an option, but it would have defeated part of the purpose of introducing self-checkout in the first place.

I looked at the problem from a business perspective as well as a technical one. Building a complicated solution would involve additional development, testing and ongoing maintenance. I wanted to understand whether that level of complexity was actually necessary.

The approach I proposed was to automate the handling of these pricing situations and avoid unnecessary customer intervention.

There were commercial considerations behind that decision, but the main objective was to keep the checkout process straightforward.

It was a good example of a problem where understanding retail operations helped me make a better engineering decision.

Promotions were another area that required considerable attention.

Our existing POS system supported various promotional campaigns. Some required customers to provide their mobile numbers for raffle entries, while others involved free items or additional interactions during a transaction.

These were already familiar processes for our cashiers, but supporting them through self-checkout was not as straightforward.

We were working with an externally managed checkout interface, which meant our development team had limited control over certain customer-facing interactions.

We couldn’t simply reproduce everything from our existing POS application.

One of our internal developers came up with a clever solution using the integration capabilities available to us. This allowed us to support certain promotional requirements without directly modifying the externally managed interface.

My involvement was mainly in understanding the existing POS behaviour, identifying what needed to be supported, discussing the integration options and coordinating with the relevant technical teams.

Knowing our existing POS system was particularly useful here. I could explain not only what a particular function did, but also why the business needed it.

Keeping the checkout running during network interruptions

One of my responsibilities across both POS and self-checkout was to help keep supermarket checkout operations running smoothly.

Network interruptions were something we had to consider. We couldn’t assume that connectivity would always be available during trading hours.

As part of the implementation, we supported offline operation so that temporary connectivity issues would not necessarily bring checkout operations to a complete stop.

However, I always preferred the systems to operate online whenever possible, particularly because of price changes.

Retail prices can change, and a system operating offline may not have the most recent information. The longer it remains offline, the greater the possibility of working with outdated data.

The purpose of offline operation was to help the store continue operating while the connectivity issue was being resolved. It was never intended to replace normal online operation.

From my perspective, the priority was to minimise disruption to the business without overlooking the importance of accurate retail information.

The project also involved working with people outside the IT department.

In particular, I worked with the Finance team because they needed sales information from the self-checkout system for their reporting requirements.

Introducing another checkout channel meant considering what happened to the transaction information after the customer had completed their purchase.

We had to understand the reports and sales information required by Finance and take those requirements into account during the integration.

This was an important part of the project, even though it was not something customers would ever see.

A customer might have a perfectly good experience using a self-checkout machine, but if the business cannot properly account for the sale afterwards, there is still a problem that needs to be addressed.

My previous experience working with POS and business reporting helped me understand these requirements and communicate them to the technical teams involved.

What I enjoyed about this project was that it involved much more than application development.

There were discussions with internal developers, external technology partners, Finance and other business stakeholders. Each team had different requirements and priorities.

Having worked on the existing POS system gave me a useful understanding of how these requirements were connected.

It also helped me identify areas where we could reuse existing functionality instead of developing everything again for self-checkout.

Not every solution came from me. Some came from our developers, while others came from discussions with the people responsible for different areas of the business.

My role was to contribute my knowledge of the existing systems, work through the technical challenges and help bring those requirements together.

By the time I left Keells in 2022, self-checkout had already been introduced across several supermarket locations.

Looking back, the pricing challenge remains one of my favourite examples from the project. It showed me how a relatively simple engineering decision could address a problem that might otherwise have required a much more complicated implementation.

The project also changed the way I looked at enterprise software development. A decision made within an application can affect store employees, customers, Finance and several other areas of the business.

Understanding those dependencies is something I continue to value in my work as a software engineer.

There were several other technical challenges during the implementation, including an interesting issue involving a third-party payment integration. I hope to share that experience in a separate article.

Plants can grow elaborate leaves, so why are most of them so simple?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A study suggests that the way leaves grow helps explain why most flowering plants have simple leaves, even though complex shapes have evolved many times.

The leaves of a maple tree, a rose bush and a magnolia bear very little resemblance to one another. Deep cuts are seen in some of them. Others are made up of several small leaflets or one unbroken blade.

Complex leaf shapes have arisen many times during the evolution of flowering plants. Simple leaves have nevertheless remained by far the most common form.

A new study suggests that the way leaves grow helps explain why leaves with a single blade and no deep cuts are so common.

Random changes were introduced by the researchers into a computer model that simulates a growing leaf.

No particular shape was favored in those runs but simple leaves were still produced far more often than other kinds.

The research was led by James Malone, a graduate student in the Department of Physics at the University of Oxford.

He was joined by Ard Louis, a professor of theoretical physics at Oxford. Researchers in Barcelona and at Stony Brook University in New York also took part.

“What we found is that you can largely explain which leaf shapes dominate in evolution simply by looking at how leaves develop and how mutations affect that process, without needing to invoke natural selection,” said Malone.

“The bias towards simplicity is built into the developmental system itself.”

For the purposes of the study, every leaf was assigned to one of four broad groups based on its outline.

An unlobed leaf consists of a single blade with no deep cuts along its edge. In a lobed leaf the blade is divided by cuts into distinct sections.

Those cuts extend much further toward the center in a dissected leaf. Separate leaflets attached to one shared stalk are what make up a compound leaf.

Large differences in leaf form can be found even among closely related plants. All four leaf types are represented among species of the geranium group Pelargonium.

Both lobed and unlobed leaves may even be produced on a single mulberry tree, as the authors pointed out.

Different leaf shapes do not necessarily require different genes. Much of the variety depends on the timing and location of gene activity inside a developing leaf.

Comparable genetic processes determine whether single leaves or leaves divided into three parts are grown by strawberry plants.

For centuries, botanists have collected, pressed and dried plants for long-term storage. Such specimens are held in collections known as herbariums that now serve as a record of plant diversity.

The team looked at 8,911 digital images of specimens from the Naturalis Biodiversity Center in Leiden, the Netherlands. The shape of each leaf was identified by hand.

The identified shapes were then placed onto five large family trees of flowering plants. One of these trees included 9,506 species and was based on information from 353 genes.

In 2012 an earlier study of 561 plants had reported that about 76% of them carried unlobed leaves. That share rose to between 86% and nearly 89% in the much larger trees used for the new study.

The frequency of leaf shape changes during evolution was then estimated from those labeled trees. Complex leaves were found to give way to unlobed ones far more readily than unlobed leaves became complex.

All five family trees showed this pattern. No disagreement was seen among the trees on the direction of any clearly established shift.

It seems natural to assume that simple leaves give plants some kind of edge over complex ones.

The shape of a leaf is known to influence the way heat and water are managed by the plant. Sunlight capture and protection from plant-eating animals may also be affected by it.

In one experiment with paper leaves, symmetrical unlobed shapes sank the fastest. Such behavior could allow the nutrients in fallen leaves to stay closer to the parent tree.

When earlier research was reviewed by the authors, the evidence for selection proved much stronger for leaf size. Far weaker support was found for selection on these four broad shapes.

In many of those studies it was shown that shape changes how a leaf works. Far fewer showed that a given shape actually helps a plant survive and reproduce. Many also looked only at certain groups of plants.

“For many biological traits, such as the camera eye with its lens and retina, complexity clearly accumulates over time through natural selection,” Louis said.

“But leaf shape shows the opposite pattern. Complexity evolves – and then evolution frequently abandons it.”

A different explanation was therefore explored using a model built by Adam Runions and colleagues in 2017.

Leaf growth in this model is driven by the veins and edge along with chemical signals acting on that edge.

Before the main experiment began the model was compared with results that had been obtained in real plants.

Earlier work on thale cress and hairy bittercress had shown that compound leaves need two genes active together.

Lobes alone were formed in thale cress when only one of those two genes was switched on.

The model behaved in the same way once its two most comparable settings had been adjusted.

Twenty of the 25 tested combinations of those settings went on to produce simple unlobed leaves.

The main experiment was started from 48 virtual leaves that represented all four shapes between them.

Each setting in turn received a small random change and this went on for 320 steps. Five runs were made from every starting leaf and 76,800 virtual leaves came out of the experiment.

Rejection was limited to physically unrealistic leaves such as those in which veins filled too little of the blade.

No leaf was rewarded for its shape and yet about three-quarters of the virtual leaves were found to be unlobed.

Within the margins of error the model’s rates of change toward simple shapes matched those of the family trees.

The model’s settings can be imagined as a long list of instructions for growing one leaf.

Random edits to that list would mostly still end in a leaf with a simple outline.

A far more specific combination of settings is needed to produce a more elaborate leaf, as the researchers found.

The settings for unlobed leaves were grouped into only three separate regions within the model.

Lobed leaves by contrast were spread over 131 regions while dissected leaves occupied 96 and compound leaves 235.

Many of the small regions that gave complex leaves were located inside the much larger region that gave simple ones.

The arrangement can be compared to a mostly green map on which hundreds of tiny blue patches have been scattered.

A few random steps taken from a blue patch would often lead a walker onto green ground.

Someone who started out in the green area might travel a long way before reaching any blue at all. Leaf development appears to work in a comparable way according to the study’s results.

Complex leaves are easily turned into simple ones by random genetic change while the opposite outcome is less likely.

Switches from one complex shape to another such as from lobed to compound were handled less well by the model.

The real family trees were also more likely to disagree with one another about these particular changes.

The authors suggested that natural selection may have had a larger influence on those shifts. It is also possible that some of the finer details of complex leaf growth are not captured by the model.

Closed net-like vein patterns cannot be recreated by it and the parallel veins common in grasses are also absent.

Much of what is known about leaf growth has been learned from a handful of laboratory plants.

Every group of flowering plants may therefore not be represented equally well by a single model.

The small teeth found along the edges of some leaves were not included in the analysis. Hand classification of thousands of herbarium images also put a limit on the amount of usable data.

Whether similar patterns exist elsewhere in nature is the question the researchers hope to answer next.

Tree and root shapes will be examined next along with tooth complexity and the fins of African cichlid fish.

“By continuing this approach – combining large-scale analysis of evolutionary trees with computer simulations – we will gain a much greater understanding of how the complexity of a trait evolves over time across different kingdoms of life,” said Louis.

Folded RNA molecules and grouped proteins had already shown a similar preference for simple forms in earlier work.

Evolution can certainly produce complicated structures but greater complexity is not always the direction it takes.

One reason simple leaves may be so common is that plants have more ways to grow them.

The full study was published in the journal eLife.

Photographers Protest Australia’s Plans to Weaken Copyright Laws for AI

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Photographers Protest Australia’s Plans to Weaken Copyright Laws for AI

Photographers have signed a statement protesting the Australian government’s plans to weaken copyright law to benefit AI companies.

According to multiple reports, the Australian government is considering weakening copyright law in order to attract investment from overseas AI companies. Under the proposals, AI companies would no longer be required to get permission from rights holders before training commercial AI models on their work.

Most of the world’s leading AI companies are known to build their models by using copyrighted work without permission or payment. The changes proposed by the Australian government would legalize this unlicensed training on copyrighted work, as long as it happened on Australian soil.

Now more than 7,000 photographers, writers, and musicians have come together to publish a statement opposing proposed changes to Australian copyright law that would favor AI companies and weaken copyright law.

“The Australian government must not legalise the theft of the work of the world’s creators on their soil,” the statement reads. “Our work must only be used to train AI models in Australia with our explicit permission.”

Isabelle Doran, CEO of the Association of Photographers and Vice Chair of Creators’ Rights Alliance, argues that photographers are already at a significant disadvantage when dealing with technology companies, and an opt-out system would place further responsibility on those whose work has been used without permission.

“Creators, such as photographers and image-makers, have little bargaining power against the might of big tech,” Doran says in a statement. “We have been absolutely clear, creators’ work has been exploited without consent, and any Opt-Outs proposal, which has proven to be burdensome, should not be forced onto creators.

She warns that Australia’s ambitions to become more competitive in the AI sector should not come at the expense of creators’ rights.

“It’s imperative that the Australian Government doesn’t backtrack on its initial commitment to protect creators, both in Australia and across the globe,” Doran explains. “Copyright is a human right and critically the lifeblood of creators; this basic human right cannot and should not be used as an economic bargaining chip for national AI investment.”

The widespread use of copyrighted material by leading AI companies without permission or compensation has prompted around 150 major lawsuits in the U.S., with rights holders alleging unauthorized use of their work.

What Running a Free HTML5 Games Site Taught Me About SEO

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 25 • Originally published at tongport.com

I did not start Tongport to learn about search engines. I started it because I wanted a simple place where anyone could open a browser tab and play a game, with no download, no install, and no account standing in the way. The site is Tongport, at https://tongport.com/ and it is a collection of free HTML5 games that run right in the browser. Search came later, and it taught me more than I expected.

The first lesson was that speed is part of the content. A games site lives or dies on how fast a game starts. If a visitor clicks a game and waits, they leave, and they do not come back to try another one. That is true on a desktop, and it is even more true on a phone, where most of my visitors arrive. So I learned to care about loading, about keeping pages light, and about making sure a game is playable on a small screen with touch controls, not just on a keyboard.

The second lesson was about categories. Tongport groups games into .io games, 2 player games, action, racing, shooting, puzzle, arcade, adventure, sports, and cooking. At first I thought of categories as a menu, a way to tidy things up. In practice they do much more. They tell a visitor what kind of fun is on offer before they click anything, and they tell a search engine what each page is about. A clear category page, with games that really belong in it, is easier to find and easier to use than a long mixed list. When a category is vague, both people and search engines get confused, so I try to keep each one honest.

The third lesson was that every game page has to stand on its own. A title and a short, plain description matter more than a clever name. If I cannot say in one or two sentences what a game is and how it plays, the page is not ready. I also learned to avoid copying the same description across many pages. It is tempting when you have a lot of games, but identical text helps nobody, and it makes each page harder to tell apart.

The fourth lesson came from writing guides. On the Tongport blog I write short guides about playing games in the browser, like soccer games, two player games on one phone, and how to get better at racing games. Those guides do something a game list cannot do. They answer the question a person actually typed into a search box. Someone looking for how to play a game with a friend on one screen does not want a wall of thumbnails. They want a clear answer, and a few games that fit it. Writing those guides taught me to think in questions, not just in keywords.

The fifth lesson is the simplest one, and the one I keep relearning. Keep the site easy to use. No forced sign up, no maze of popups, and a game that starts when you click it. People remember how a site made them feel. If it was quick and friendly, they return. If it was slow and pushy, they do not, and no amount of clever search work will fix that.

Running Tongport is still a work in progress for me. I am learning as I go, one category and one guide at a time. But if I had to sum up what a free games site taught me about search, it would be this. Make pages fast, make them clear, group them honestly, and answer real questions. Search engines notice the same things visitors notice, just a little later.
