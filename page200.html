Our lives are defined not by what we have but what we give — on what and whom we spend our energy and time, how we share our material possessions and our mental powers, what we do with our gifts. “Anything you do not give freely and abundantly becomes lost to you,” Annie Dillard wrote in contemplating the nature of creativity. “You open your safe and find ashes.”

Paradoxically, the ability to give hinges on the ability to receive: You must be rested in order to labor, taking the repose it takes to recharge your vital energies; you must be interested in order to be interesting, taking the world in with a greedy curiosity that absorbs its beauty and strangeness in order to metabolize it into meaning.

C.S. Lewis (November 29, 1898–November 22, 1963) offers a wonderfully counterintuitive perspective on our notions of generosity and our ideas about what it means to be interesting in his memoir Surprised by Joy (public library).

With an eye to the ideal structure of the creative life, he writes:

It is a life almost entirely selfish. Selfish, not self-centred: for in such a life my mind would be directed towards a thousand things, not one of which is myself. The distinction is not unimportant. One of the happiest men and most pleasing companions I have ever known was intensely selfish. On the other hand I have known people capable of real sacrifice whose lives were nevertheless a misery to themselves and to others, because self-concern and self-pity filled all their thoughts. Either condition will destroy the soul in the end. But till the end, give me the man who takes the best of everything (even at my expense) and then talks of other things, rather than the man who serves me and talks of himself, and whose very kindnesses are a continual reproach, a continual demand for pity, gratitude, and admiration.

This is also why, Lewis intimates, the “thrill” of our greatest creative breakthroughs — those revelations that give us back to ourselves, out of which we give our best work to the world — is rooted in “self-forgetfulness”:

Only when your whole attention and desire are fixed on something else — whether a distant mountain, or the past, or the gods of Asgard — does the “thrill” arise.

Shut your mouth; open your eyes and ears. Take in what is there.

Couple with John Steinbeck on the art of receiving, then revisit C.S. Lewis on what we long for in our existential longing.

Comparing Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo’s final international stats

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

A major chapter in the Lionel Messi vs Cristiano Ronaldo rivalry is drawing to a close, with Messi having bade Argentina farewell and Ronaldo having now announced his retirement from international football with Portugal.

With both players’ international careers now over, their total tallies for Portugal and Argentina are finalised. How do they compare on the international stage?

After a legendary career with Argentina, in which he’s comfortably their all-time top goalscorer and appearance-maker, the 39-year-old captain waved goodbye to the fans with a goal and two assists against Benin.

Ronaldo has now confirmed that his time with Portugal is also over, bringing the curtain down on an extraordinary international career. Whether he’ll be given the opportunity to enjoy a farewell appearance in front of his home supporters remains to be seen.

The 41-year-old leaves international football as the most prolific goalscorer in men’s international history, having represented Portugal at five World Cups and six European Championships.

Few players will ever get close to the international numbers Messi and Ronaldo have left behind. Ronaldo finishes with 234 Portugal appearances and 146 goals, both higher than Messi’s final totals of 207 caps and 125 goals for Argentina.

Ronaldo was also the more prolific goalscorer – both in terms of his overall tally and averages. His 146 strikes arrived at one every 127 minutes, compared with Messi’s rate of one every 136. Taking penalties out of the equation leaves Ronaldo ahead again, averaging a goal every 151 minutes to Messi’s 172.

Messi’s advantage comes in the creative department. He provided 67 assists for Argentina, comfortably clear of Ronaldo’s 37, and that swings the overall goal contribution numbers his way: 193 to 183.

There is very little separating them elsewhere. Messi scored 12 direct free-kicks to Ronaldo’s 11 and registered 11 hat-tricks to Ronaldo’s 10. He was also, surprisingly, more reliable from the spot, converting 78% of his penalties compared with Ronaldo’s 69%.

Add everything together, and Messi averaged a goal or assist every 89 minutes of his international career. Ronaldo’s corresponding figure is every 101 minutes, despite his superior individual scoring rate.

Messi also finishes one ahead in the trophy count. Argentina won four major honours during his career, including the World Cup and two Copa Americas, while Ronaldo helped Portugal claim Euro 2016 and two Nations League titles.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/regulatory/cms-final-2027-outpatient-pay-rule-brings-hospitals-little-relief-340b-site-neutral" hreflang="en">CMS’ final 2027 outpatient pay rule brings hospitals little relief on 340B, site-neutral changes</a>

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
CMS’ final 2027 outpatient pay rule brings hospitals little relief on 340B, site-neutral changes" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services on Tuesday finalized its annual outpatient hospital payments rule with a lower-than-proposed rate bump and its major, industry-opposed policy shifts moderated, but largely intact.

The 2027 Hospital Outpatient Prospective Payment (OPPS) and Ambulatory Surgical Center (ASC) Payment Systems rule outlines a 2.3% reimbursement increase for those meeting applicable quality reporting requirements. That’s below the proposed 2.4% increase, and reflects a 3.2% projected hospital market basket increase and a 0.9 percentage point productivity adjustment.

The agency is also moving forward with a substantial and controversial cut in 340B drug reimbursements, though not quite to the extent that it initially proposed.

The final rule adjusts these drug payments down from the current bar of average sales price (ASP) plus 6% to ASP minus 28%, as opposed to the proposed 33.4% below ASP. To make the reimbursement change budget neutral as required by statute, OPPS payments for non-drug services will be boosted by 7.5%.

CMS said it tweaked the proposed 340B reimbursement cut based on 340B ceiling price data from the Health Resources and Services Administration, with the baseline reductions informed by a drug acquisition cost survey it conducted earlier this year. Those changes will reduce Original Medicare drug payments by $4.35 billion and the drug payments from beneficiaries by $1.1 billion (or $900 on average per beneficiary who receives an affected drug from a hospital outpatient department) in the policy’s first year, CMS said.

The rate adjustment did little to calm hospital industry groups who’d railed against the 340B reimbursement changes as a threat to safety-net care and a gift to for-profit hospitals that do not participate in the subsidy program but would benefit from the budget-neutral offset. The American Hospital Association, the Association of American Medical Colleges and America’s Essential Hospitals each condemned the finalized policy in reaction statements, with the latter two issuing more forceful rebukes.

“CMS has based its reimbursement to 340B hospitals on the results of a hasty and faulty survey, failing to follow the clear requirements of the Medicare statute,” AAMC Chief Health Care Officer Jonathan Jaffery, M.D., said. “The drug payment cuts disproportionately affect and target non-profit and public hospitals serving low-income patients, while redistributing billions of dollars to hospitals that do not share the same safety-net and academic missions. These cuts threaten access to critical services made possible by the 340B program and will lead to worse health outcomes for patients.”

“Rather than strengthening and preserving safety net funding, CMS is instead prioritizing hospitals that do not serve the safety net and nodding to the pharmaceutical industry's interests,” AEH’s statement reads. “These cuts are landing at a time when essential hospitals and their mission are more important than ever, as millions of people risk losing their Medicaid coverage in the coming months.”

The Federation of American Hospitals, which represents for-profit hospitals that do not participate in 340B, struck a different tone, calling the rulemaking “a win for for patients, taxpayers, and the hospitals that communities depend on.”

“By bringing Medicare drug payments more in line with actual costs, this reform will lower seniors’ out-of-pocket expenses and redirect billions in Medicare dollars where they’re needed most—supporting access to care for patients served by small and rural hospitals across the country,” Charlene MacDonald, the group’s president and CEO, said in a statement. “We applaud the Administration for advancing a smarter, fairer approach to Medicare payment.”

Additionally, CMS finalized an increase in an annual OPPS conversion factor to non-drug items and services, speeding up the agency’s effort to offset a prior $7.8 billion recoupment to 340B hospitals it was forced to make after previously reducing 340B rates contrary to law. But while this accelerated payback—often described by non-340B hospitals as a clawback—was proposed as an increase from 0.5% to 3%, the final rule brings the conversion factor to 2%.

AHA Senior Vice President of Public Policy Analysis and Development Ashley Thompson described both 340B policy changes together as a “continued assault” on the subsidy program that “will further stress hospitals’ ability to care for their patients.” AAMC’s Jaffery said the combined policies’ impact “will be devastating to patients,” and that his group “remains committed to pursuing all available avenues to reverse these payment cuts.”

Further rankling the hospital industry was the final rule’s continued push toward site-neutral payment policy and a phase-out of the inpatient-only list.

For the former, the agency will apply Physician Fee Schedule payment rates for some imaging services to off-campus provider-based departments. In line with the proposed rule, CMS said it expects the plan to trigger first-year reductions in Medicare Part B expenditures and beneficiary cost-sharing obligations of $260 million and $70 million, while also reducing a market incentive for provider consolidation.

Meanwhile, CMS finalized its proposal to remove 647 services from the inpatient-only list, allowing them to be delivered and paid for in the lower-cost outpatient setting.

“Both policies are misguided,” AHA’s Thompson said, “and overlook the unique role hospital outpatient departments play in delivering care to patients in communities nationwide. Hospital outpatient departments treat sicker and more complex groups of patients than independent physician practices, including those with higher rates of disability and who are more likely to come from low-income and rural communities.”

CMS’ fact sheet on the 1,270-page annual rule also noted various changes to quality reporting programs for hospital outpatient departments and ASCs, a proposal to require prior authorization for eight botulinum toxin injection codes CMS said it has seen a jump in claim volume, and new permission for accreditation organizations to assess hospitals’ compliance with EMTALA (the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act of 1986).

“CMS is committed to spending taxpayer funds wisely and efficiently, so America’s seniors can continue getting the care they need,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in a release. “By right-sizing payments and rooting out waste, today’s reforms will drive affordability in Medicare for current beneficiaries while ensuring it will still be there to serve their children and grandchildren.”
