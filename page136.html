Here's Why You Shouldn't Use AI to Vote

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As Americans go to the polls today, many will have had their decision about who to vote for informed by an artificial intelligence-powered chatbot.

Around 50 percent of recently surveyed US adults have at one time used an AI chatbot, and 8 percent say they have used these tools ahead of the midterms to find information on candidates and policies, according to Pew Research.

But a lot of information from these chatbots is out of date and has been hallucinated or sourced from partisan websites.

Even supposedly non-partisan chatbots like OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Anthropic’s Claude, and Google’s Gemini have been found wanting when it comes to questions about the midterms.

Just last week, Google’s AI Mode refused to provide the results of the 2020 elections and the company’s Gemini chatbot said the deadline to request a mail-in ballot in Pennsylvania was November 3, rather than the real deadline of October 27, according to research by Forum AI.

According to the Forum AI research, almost 40 percent of election answers given by all the mainstream chatbots contained at least one factual error. In August, when Forum AI’s researchers asked why someone should vote for North Carolina congressman Don Davis, Claude gave a response that confused the lawmaker with a “former school superintendent” of the same name.

Google and Anthropic did not respond to requests for comment. Open AI directed WIRED to a blog outlining the company’s approach to elections in 2026.

Part of the problem is that chatbots are reliant on an increasingly problematic pool of online information. Research by NewsGuard found that mainstream chatbots cite so-called “pink slime” websites nearly 50 percent of the time when asked about candidates and policies related to the midterms. “Pink slime” sites present as mainstream news websites, but are in fact funded by political or ideological groups and individuals.

Ahead of the midterms, many more of these sites have sprung up, and chatbots are paying attention. The worst offender, according to NewsGuard, was OpenAI’s ChatGPT, which cited the “pink slime” sites in more than 70 percent of searches, while xAI’s Grok chatbot performed best, citing “pink slime” sites in 30 percent of cases.

“I don't think that most Americans are prepared to navigate an election in the age of consumer AI,” Nina Jankowicz, executive director of the American Sunlight Project and a former disinformation czar during the Biden administration, tells WIRED. “One of the problems is that executives and companies have really overpromised on what AI is capable of. It has to ingest this information from somewhere, and often those sources are not very good. And it's predictive, so if it doesn't know the answer, it does frequently make things up.”

Why Amazon Stock Can Keep Rising Through 2035

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amazon's cloud business gets the spotlight, but improving e-commerce margins is an overlooked opportunity.

Most of the attention on Amazon (AMZN +2.20%) goes to its cloud business, Amazon Web Services (AWS). But the company's online stores are quietly becoming more profitable, thanks to automation and robotics. That's a big reason the stock can keep rising through 2035.

On the third-quarter earnings call, CEO Andy Jassy said, "There has never been a business with a trillion dollars of annual revenue. Amazon is on a path to have two of them in AWS and stores." He added that some folks are "sleeping a bit" on how much progress the retail business has made.

That progress shows up in profit margins. Jassy said Amazon's North America and International segments have each expanded operating margins by nearly 10 percentage points over the past few years. The company is also on track to lower its cost to serve each unit, or deliver each item, for a fourth straight year.

Robotics is a big driver. Amazon has more than 1 million robots in its fulfillment network, and its newest facilities, including same-day delivery sites, are "very heavy on robotics." In the third quarter, shipping costs, excluding fuel and long-haul inflation, grew about four percentage points slower than units shipped. Jassy said the economic impact on its business so far is "small compared to what it is going to be in the next five years."

Lower costs on a sales base this large, with Amazon's online store generating $286 billion in annual revenue, can free up a lot more cash. Over the past 12 months, operating cash flow, the cash the business brings in before investments, grew 29% year over year to $168 billion. It has compounded about 25% a year over the past five years, and with Amazon's automation push still early, that cash flow has room to keep growing for years. And over time, Amazon's stock price tends to follow cash flow.

Yet the stock trades at about 16 times operating cash flow, based on the Oct. 29 close. That's near the lowest multiple Amazon stock has traded since the 2008 bear market -- a discount for a business whose cash flow is still growing rapidly and has room to expand.

The risk is heavy spending. Amazon expects capital expenditures of about $220 billion in 2026, mostly for Amazon Web Services, and management expects to spend "meaningfully more" in 2027. High capital spending has pushed free cash flow (operating cash flow minus capital expenditures) negative over the past year.

If those investments don't earn strong returns, free cash flow could stay low and weigh on the stock. Still, with retail becoming a second profit engine and the stock trading at a discount, Amazon shares could deliver excellent returns over the next 10 years.

What's the meaning of the "C" on your USB-C ports?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For a cable that is considered a "universal connector," the trusty USB comes in a staggering array of different shapes and colors. You may find yourself in the aisle in the electronics store or scrolling Amazon, staring at the different options and wondering what makes each of them different from the other and which one is the correct one for your needs.

In the past few years, we have seen more and more devices finally offer just one clear choice: USB Type-C. It comes in one shape, has no color coding and promises to work with any USB Type-C device. But what does the "C" actually stand for? Does it have a secret meaning? Well, the answer is a lot more straightforward than one might think.

If you go searching online for the answer to that question, you might come across several theories that the internet loves to repeat over and over. Those are all myths and are easily explained away.

Explainer sites keep running into the same three guesses. Some people assume the C stands for "connectivity," since the new connector works with such a wide range of devices. Others figure it means "charging," because that's what most of us use it for. And a third camp thinks it refers to the rounded shape of the plug. None of them are right. The "C" only names the connector, and it does not stand for any word beginning with the letter C.

The myths don't end with the name. There are even more about what a USB-C port can do. Plenty of people assume it means fast, or that it's the same thing as Thunderbolt, or that every USB-C port will fast charge a laptop and drive a monitor. But they don't all have the same capabilities. The connector is just the shape, and what comes through it depends on the device and the cable, which is why a USB-C port isn't a guarantee of high speeds.

The real answer is a lot less exciting. USB Type-C is just the connector that comes after Type-A and Type-B.

USB 1.0 arrived in January 1996 with only those two. They weren't interchangeable on purpose. Type-A, the flat rectangle, went into the host, meaning the computer or a power source. Type-B, the chunky square one, went into the device, meaning the printer or scanner on the other end. USB was built around that one-way idea, with the host supplying power and running the show and the peripheral going along with it. Different shapes meant you couldn't plug a cable in the wrong way.

Then the different shapes and colors started piling up. USB 2.0 in 2000 brought the mini connectors, and micro followed because phones and cameras wanted something thinner. The colors were a loose convention for telling ports apart: black for USB 2.0, blue for USB 3.0 and red or yellow for ports that keep supplying power while the computer sleeps (even orange ports don't mean the same thing everywhere). Manufacturers were free to ignore all of it, and plenty did. Most of them were B-style ends on the device side, so you still had a Type-A on one end and some flavor of Type-B on the other.

USB Type-C was finalized in August 2014, and it addressed nearly every complaint people had with the older versions. It was thin, so it fit even the thinnest phones and gadgets. It was reversible, so gone were the days of guessing which way to plug it in. And it dropped the idea of two different shapes, so the host and device split moved out of the connector and into the devices themselves.

Since the shape stays the same, there is no need for color coding either. The two ends talk to each other and agree on how much data and power to send, and newer cables simply move more through the same connector. USB4 Version 2.0 now reaches up to 80Gbps over that same Type-C connector.

USB Type-C was received positively by consumers and tech companies thanks to its speed, small footprint and versatility. When it first came out, people mostly praised the reversible connection, but it's still not a perfect connector. It has a delicate, thin plastic tab (the tongue) inside the socket that holds the pins, and if it bends or snaps, the port is ruined. It's also still a little finicky to plug in.

At the moment, there are no clear plans for a Type-D connector, but if we were picking, we'd go for a simple ball-shaped plug that attaches to its socket magnetically.

Cogent Unveils VR-1.1, Its Latest Frontier Cyber Model, Which Tops LiveCVEBench for Vulnerability Repair

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On 190 recently disclosed CVEs, VR-1.1 produced working repairs for 57.9%, compared with 45.3% for both Claude Code and Codex.

SAN FRANCISCO, Nov. 3, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Cogent Security today released VR-1.1, the latest version of its frontier reasoning model for cybersecurity. VR-1.1 tops LiveCVEBench, a public benchmark that tests whether AI agents can repair recently disclosed vulnerabilities in real codebases. It repaired 57.9% of the benchmark's 190 tasks, compared with 45.3% for both Claude Code and Codex.

Security teams already know about more vulnerabilities than they can fix, and AI has sharply increased the rate at which new ones are found. In July, high- and critical-severity disclosures from major software makers hit five times the monthly record set before Anthropic announced Claude Mythos Preview in April. Each repair still depends on an engineer who has to understand the flaw well enough to change the code without breaking anything else, and that work sets the pace for most vulnerability programs.

"Finding vulnerabilities got a lot faster this year. Fixing them is still slow, and that's where security teams are stuck," said Vineet Edupuganti, co-founder and CEO of Cogent Security. "Every company is going to need AI that can fix security problems as fast as AI finds them, without breaking the systems the business runs on. GTA 6 Could Save You $5,000 According To Self-Made Millionaire

GTA 6 is the first new game since Mario Kart World to have an $80 standard edition, with a $100 ultimate edition if you want access to all of its in-game stores. That’s a lot of money for just one game, except a self-made millionaire has explained how the one-off, $100 expenditure could end up saving you thousands in the long run.

The millionaire in question is Mark Tilbury, a British millionaire who, among other things, uses social media to share investment and money-saving tips. His latest video has quickly gone viral as it explains why buying a $100 copy of GTA 6 could end up saving you $5,000.

Tilbury begins by breaking down how much entertainment you’re getting per hour in GTA 6, based on the reveal by Rockstar that its story will take 80 hours to play through. Dollars-per-hour isn’t a metric I typically like to consider when weighing up the value of a game, but for the sake of this fun exercise, let’s hear Tilbury out.

If you were to play GTA 6 for 80 hours and then never touch it again, you’d have paid $1.25 for the experience. That’s not bad at all. This is where Tilbury loses me a little, though. He then notes that once you add in side content and map exploration, the price-per-hour for GTA 6 drops to just 50 cents. That’s 200 hours, and while I’m sure many, if not most of us will spend that much time in Leonida, if not more, he doesn’t explain where that 200-hour figure comes from.

Regardless of how much you end up paying per hour for GTA 6, Tilbury still hasn’t explained how buying it for $100 could end up saving you $5,000. The start of the road to that number begins with the assumption that GTA 6 owners will play the game in four hour chunks. Divide that 200-hour total by four, and you get 50 play sessions.

That means, at least according to Tilbury, that your GTA 6 playthrough will result in you opting to not go out and paint the town red at least 50 times. The millionaire hypothesizes that you might spend $100 on any given night out, multiply that number by the 50 nights out you’re not having because you’re at home running around Vice City, and that’s how he concludes that a $100 copy of GTA 6 could end up saving you a cool 5k.

There are a lot of assumptions being made here. Assumptions that not only won’t apply to everyone playing GTA 6, but probably won’t even apply to the average GTA 6 player. From the hours spent with the game to how long they’re playing it each time. Most of all, though, the idea that someone is choosing to stay in and play GTA 6 over going out and spending $100 each time not once, not twice, but 50 times - that’s just not happening. The big takeaway here, again, as much as I don’t like to boil a game down to dollars spent vs. hours played, is that for $80, or $100, you are almost certainly going to get your money’s worth out of GTA 6.

Rising temperatures may have a dangerous effect on mental health

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

New research reveals how extreme heat may increase suicide risk by disrupting sleep and emotional regulation, highlighting a hidden mental health threat.

Extreme heat can do more than exhaust the body. It may also disrupt sleep, weaken emotional control, and increase vulnerability to serious psychological distress.

A new study has identified temperatures at which extreme heat is associated with increased psychological distress, revealing how disrupted sleep and emotional regulation may contribute to suicide risk.

The research was led by scientists from Georgetown University and the World Bank. It draws on a nationally representative panel study of nearly 7,500 adults in Bangladesh.

The researchers found that extreme heat was associated with an increased risk of suicide-related thoughts, with disrupted sleep playing a surprisingly important role.

“Rising global temperatures threaten human mental health on a massive scale,” said lead author Syed Shabab Wahid, an assistant professor of global health at Georgetown.

“In climate-vulnerable nations such as Bangladesh, this heat stress can translate into severe psychological strain and elevated suicide risk.”

The study tracked two interconnected pathways between heat and suicidal cognition: disrupted sleep and emotional dysregulation.

The association between temperature and suicide-related thoughts became statistically significant at 28°C and was strongest at 35°C.

Temperatures this high are common in Bangladesh during the summer months.

At that upper threshold, nearly 30 percent of the total association between heat and suicide risk ran through poor sleep quality alone. Emotional dysregulation accounted for a smaller but still significant 1.8 percent.

Together, poor sleep and difficulties regulating emotions statistically accounted for roughly a third of the observed relationship between heat and suicide-related thoughts.

The study design is worth understanding, because it shaped what the researchers could and couldn’t conclude.

Participants were interviewed twice – once in winter (January–February 2024) and once at the height of summer (May–June 2024).

Suicide risk was measured using the Brief Suicide Cognitions Scale, a validated tool that captures the sense of unbearability, unlovability, and unsolvability that precedes suicidal behavior.

Heat exposure was operationalized as the number of days with maximum temperatures at or above 35°C in the two weeks prior to each interview.

The panel structure meant the same individuals answered both rounds, which allowed the team to compare the same person’s mental state across seasons.

Summer scores were meaningfully worse: average suicide cognition scores rose from 10.99 in winter to 12.96 in summer.

Sleep quality declined. Emotional regulation worsened. Both effects were consistent across age groups, though older adults and women showed the steepest deterioration.

“Imagine a smartphone with a drained battery, overheating processor, and critical apps crashing all at once. Sustained ambient heat acts on the human brain and body in the same way,” Wahid explained.

“The body cannot cool down at night, leading to broken rest and depleting mental reserves. When the emotional processor overheats without restorative recovery, coping mechanisms collapse under stress.”

The study found that risk was notably higher during summer among less educated and poorer populations.

These differences may reflect unequal access to cooling, greater exposure to outdoor heat, and housing conditions that make it harder to escape high temperatures.

Individuals with disabilities showed consistently elevated risk across seasons. Unemployed people reported higher suicide cognition scores than those in most other employment categories.

Urban residents were more susceptible than rural ones. This may reflect the urban heat island effect. Concrete and asphalt absorb and radiate heat in ways that push nighttime temperatures higher.

There’s a global dimension to these findings that goes beyond Bangladesh.

Previous meta-analyses have found that a 1°C increase in monthly mean temperature is associated with a 1.5 to 1.7 percent increase in suicidal outcomes.

Bangladesh sits in a region the IPCC projects will experience some of the worst heatwaves of the coming decades. But the mechanisms the study identified are not unique to South Asia.

“Rather than treating climate-related mental health crises as unpreventable events, public health systems can now focus on intermediate, modifiable targets,” Wahid said. Sleep is one of them.

“Health agencies can monitor heat waves alongside sleep quality disruption indicators to mobilize preventative interventions. Interventions centered on sleep preservation, household cooling access, and community-based emotional regulation training should serve as effective suicide prevention tools during heatwaves.”

The paper points toward green roofing, passive architecture, and low-cost cooling technologies as structural interventions with documented effects on indoor temperatures in Bangladesh.

A pilot study found that buildings with vegetation-covered green roofing maintained temperatures 3.5°C lower than bare rooftops.

Such reductions could help make indoor environments more comfortable during extreme heat, potentially protecting sleep and reducing heat-related stress.

The study has some important limitations.

First, it relies on ambient outdoor temperature rather than indoor measurements. That’s a known proxy problem in Bangladesh, where air conditioning is rare and indoor heat tracks outdoor heat closely.

Humidity data wasn’t available, meaning the full thermal stress experienced by respondents – particularly relevant in a country where wet-bulb temperatures can reach dangerous levels – is likely underestimated.

What the study offers is new evidence that poor sleep and difficulties regulating emotions may help explain the relationship between extreme heat and suicide-related thoughts.

These findings point toward practical ways to reduce the psychological toll of rising temperatures.

Protecting sleep, improving access to cooling, and supporting mental health could become increasingly important as extreme heat becomes more common.

The study is published in the journal Environmental Research.
