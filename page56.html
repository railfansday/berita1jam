No Matter What Happens to the S&P 500 in November, These 3 Dividend Stocks Belong in Your Portfolio

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A retailer, a spice maker, and a food distributor have raised their dividends for a combined 151 years.

November has a way of making investors feel like they need an opinion about the market. The index is near a record, the AI trade is wobbling, and every headline wants you to decide whether this is the top. Nobody knows, and the portfolios that can thrive during the long stretches of uncertainty share one trait: They include a healthy helping of reliable dividend payers.

The three consumer companies below have paid and raised their dividends through recessions, pandemics, and the bursting of more than one market bubble. Two of them are in the middle of the biggest deals in their histories. All three sell things people keep restocking for their homes in good months and bad.

In June, discount retail giant Target (TGT +0.29%) increased its quarterly dividend by 1.8% to $1.16 per share, making 2026 the 55th straight year it has raised its payout. At the current share price of near $163, that works out to a yield just over 3%, but the dividend still consumes less than half of its earnings, which leaves the company plenty of room to conduct share buybacks and invest in the stores themselves.

The reason Target belongs in retail investors' portfolios is not the yield alone. It is that the company keeps acting like a retailer that expects to be around for another 55 years. This fall, it relaunched Threshold, its house brand for home goods, finished a full remodel of its SoHo store in Manhattan, and promoted a shopping list showing that folks could pull together a Thanksgiving dinner for six in its stores for just $30.

The chain's dividend-hiking streak started in 1971, which means Target raised its payouts through every market the S&P 500 has produced since Richard Nixon was president.

Spice and sauce powerhouse McCormick (MKC -3.32%) is paying a quarterly dividend of $0.48 per share, up from $0.45 per share a year ago, and has raised its dividend annually for 40 consecutive years. From its 2020 peak above $100, the stock has fallen to about $48 today, which pushed its yield up to nearly 4%, the highest it has been in a generation.

What has investors nervous this year -- and what contributed to its year-to-date decline of around 28% in the stock -- is McCormick's pending combination with Unilever's foods business, which the two companies announced in March. That deal would create a company with about $20 billion in annual revenue, with roughly $600 million in expected cost savings, according to Reuters. It's slated to close by mid-2027.

Meanwhile, McCormick's existing business keeps doing what it does. Third-quarter sales rose 17%, gross margin expanded by 190 basis points, and McCormick de Mexico, which it acquired a majority interest in back in January, is already integrated. Spices and sauces are among the least cyclical products in the grocery store -- even in a recession, people are still going to season their food. So McCormick is the kind of business you want dishing out quarterly payments to you while the market argues about chip stocks.

Sysco (SYY +2.15%) has raised its dividend for 57 straight years and now pays $0.55 per share each quarter. Because its streak of hikes has hit the 50-year mark, it has earned the rare title of Dividend King. It's also the most ambitious of these three companies right now. In March, it agreed to buy Restaurant Depot, the cash-and-carry chain where independent restaurant owners go to load up on a wide array of the items they require, from paper towels to chicken. That deal moves Sysco into a higher-margin channel that it previously didn't have.

Sysco already delivers to more than 700,000 customer locations, and every one of those food service locations needs its trucks to show up every week, regardless of whether the S&P 500 is up or down. That is my whole case. The company reported its fiscal 2027 first-quarter results on Nov. 3 and held its full-year guidance steady. Its payout ratio of around 60% is high for a distributor, but Sysco has sustained that for years. Add the Restaurant Depot deal, and you have a reliable dividend grower that is also, for the first time in a while, a growth stock story.

Target gets you the consumer, McCormick gets you the pantry, and Sysco gets you every restaurant in between. None of them are exciting, which is actually the point. To me, the right way to own them is to make them the ballast in a portfolio that also holds the growth names everyone is nervous about this month.

If November goes badly for Wall Street, these three will pay you anyway. If it goes well, you will own three companies that have been raising their dividends since before many of today's investors were born.

Ocarina Of Time Remake Is Being Review Bombed On Metacritic

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

After quickly becoming one of 2026’s highest rated games, The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake is unsurprisingly in the process of being review-bombed.

Considering the original Ocarina of Time still holds the top spot on Metacritic’s top-rated games of all time after nearly 30 years, it’s no surprise that the recently released remake has been an instant critical darling. As of the time of writing, it’s following in the original’s footsteps by being 2026’s highest-rated game so far, a crown that only GTA 6 has a chance of stealing.

Even though The Legend of Zelda is one of the most beloved series in all of gaming and always has been, those high scores have made the remake a new target for review bombers wanting to drag it down from its pedestal. In a nice twist, though, it looks like Zelda fans are having none of it and are actively fighting back against the naysayers.

As pointed out by Redditor Kikisilvania in a post on the Zelda subreddit that’s been removed for arbitrary reasons, the Ocarina of Time remake’s Metacritic page has recently seen an influx of negative reviews, with 187 as of the time of writing. While it’s fair that not everyone is going to love Ocarina of Time, the reviews and their justifications seem very insincere.

We finally got a chance to play The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake and it's still one of the best games ever made.

Many of the negative reviews call the game “outdated” and pin the positive reception on nostalgia from those wearing “rose tinted glasses”, a phrase that comes up more than once. Others make it clear that this is a response to Zelda being the highest-rated game of the year, including one that tries to suggest reviewers feel pressured to praise it like the original.

To be fair, there are some complaints mentioned that I’ve been seeing around, like the dialogue changes and character designs. It still seems like a lot to give a flat zero, though.

There also seems to be some distaste for a remake getting such glowing reviews and coming out on top over wholly new games. One user points out that a “faithful port of an existing work” that doesn’t add anything new “does not merit excessive praise”. If that doesn’t show what this is all about, I don’t know what to tell you.

As you can probably tell from the still incredibly high user review score, the review bomb attempt isn’t exactly going well. Beyond the fact that there simply aren’t enough negative reviews to make a dent, Zelda fans have come in swinging with over 6,000 positive reviews, some of which outright say they’re doing so to fight the bombing. It looks like Ocarina of Time’s review score isn’t going anywhere any time soon.

Building Reliable iOS Apps With AI Agents

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Senior iOS engineer at Uber. Indie app developer. 8+ years of professional experience.

One year ago I wrote Breaking Free from Xcode: Unlock AI's Full Potential in iOS. At that time, getting an agent to build, run and check an iOS app took a pile of MCP servers, VS Code extensions and some patience.

A lot has changed since. Models are much smarter. Xcode ships with agents built in. Claude Code can control the simulator on its own: it builds, launches, taps through screens and checks the result with screenshots.

For prototypes and MVPs, mostly yes. You can describe an idea and get the app “one-shotted”. But if your software has to be as reliable as running water (a phrase I borrow from Uber's mission: "transportation as reliable as running water, everywhere, for everyone"), two principles still matter: design and harness.

This is the biggest shift I've seen. While building a feature, I don't keep in my head which method calls which method, which file it lives in, or how state moves through each type. The agent handles that.

That doesn't mean you can stop reviewing code. Where quality matters, you still read PRs. But your attention moves up a level. You own the design of the whole system, not just one module, so architecture is now the most valuable skill you have.

At the design level you are pretty high in the hierarchy of components, that’s why visualization helps. A diagram with the right amount of detail shows how the pieces fit together, which is hard to see in plain text. The C4 model gives you that visualization at a few zoom levels.

Last year I suggested putting the project architecture in AGENTS.md. I've changed my mind: a dedicated docs/architecture/ folder is a better place. You don't need the entire architecture loaded into the context every time you do something. Instead, AGENTS.md keeps a reference to docs/architecture/context.md, so the agent can retrieve it when on demand. Luis Herrera's deep dive walks through C4 levels on a sample system.

System Context. Your app, its users and the outside systems it talks to: the backend, push notifications, analytics. This is context.md, and it links to the containers.

Containers. Conventional C4 container usually stands for a piece of software that can de deployed, like a micro-service or app. I had to adapt the original idea: a container is a feature, a flow or an SDK module, like Feed, Checkout or Networking. containers.md shows how they connect, explains what each one owns and what it may depend on, and links to components where they exist.

Components. The parts inside a container. Only draw these for complex areas, like a post composer with drafts, uploads and retries, or a sync engine. A stateless FeedService with a single getFeed() call doesn't need a component diagram. C4 itself says you only need the levels "that add value".

Code. No diagrams. Component docs just link to the specific files. The code is the source of truth, and an agent can read it faster than you can draw it.

Think about C4 as a pattern that generalizes an approach to structure progressive complexity of the architecture.

This is what I call discoverable architecture. The agent doesn't load the whole architecture up front. AGENTS.md points it to context.md, and from there it follows links down only as far as the task needs. Humans onboard the same way.

The script needs jq, and don't forget to chmod +x it. The stamp file matters: the docs pass runs once per new set of Swift changes, not after every question you ask.

Models are far more capable now, but they still need guidance. I regularly see agents get confused by strict concurrency and SwiftUI performance. The code compiles and the tests pass but the produced code can crash, that why your hard skills still matter.

Hilton Is Growing Faster Than Marriott, Hyatt And IHG

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hilton opened 182 hotels and 27,000 rooms in the Q3 2026, which works out to 23,000 net additional rooms and net unit growth of 6% from a year prior, according to its earnings release. Net income was $536MM, up from $421MM, and adjusted EBITDA was $1.058bn. Management and franchise fee revenue rose 9.5% which is the company’s core business model.

Comparing that to the other three big loyalty hotel companies, Marriott added roughly 20,000 net rooms and grew 4.6%. IHG reported net system growth of 4.9% after opening 12,900 rooms across 92 hotels. Hyatt posted net rooms growth of 3.8% over the trailing 12 months. The companies define growth a little differently, but Hilton leads on every version I can find, and it did so while adding more net rooms in a quarter than Marriott, the larger of the two companies.

More rooms does not necessarily mean higher earnings. Hyatt’s comparable system-wide RevPAR rose 7.2%, Marriott’s rose 6.5% worldwide and 7.4% in the US and Canada, while Hilton’s rose 5.8%, and IHG’s rose just 5.3%. Hilton ranks third of four on that list which starts to eat into its net rooms added lead. Hyatt’s caveat is its all-inclusive business, where Net Package RevPAR fell 2.5%, which matters because every big hotel brand wants your all-inclusive money right now. This, for me, probably deserves its own post if the drops continue into the fourth quarter compared with YoY.

Net profit confirms the rate growth relative to both peers and net rooms is more tied to RevPAR than absolute growth. Marriott earned $794MM in the quarter and $1.52bn in adjusted EBITDA, well ahead of Hilton. Hyatt’s net income was $58MM, or $122MM adjusted, which is considerably less but HYatt is far smaller (and underperforms.) I wrote last week that Royal Caribbean out-earned Hilton’s whole 2025 in a quarter, and the hotel sector is a reminder that growth and earnings are different races.

Marriott’s development pipeline hit a record of more than 634,000 rooms across over 4,200 properties, with 44% of those rooms under construction. Hilton’s pipeline is 543,100 rooms across 3,869 hotels in 135 countries, with nearly half under construction and more than half outside the United States. IHG’s pipeline is 353,000 rooms, up 3.2% and equal to 33% of its current system. Hyatt’s is about 158,000 rooms, up 12.1%.

Marriott has the biggest pipeline in absolute terms, Hyatt’s is growing fastest of the three that disclosed growth, and Hilton grew 5%. Conversions matter here too. Marriott said conversions (existing independent hotels or those being reflagged) made up over a third of signings and over 40% of openings in the first nine months, and IHG says development splits roughly evenly between new builds and conversions. Hilton opened its first Select by Hilton hotels this quarter, one of several newer brands it is leaning on to fill that pipeline.

As someone whose agency sells hotel rooms, both of these numbers are important but for different reasons. My clients and I prefer to see the rate creep slow or stop (rate regression might be a dream) with more stays. But more properties to choose from is good news too and increases competition.

For travelers, the bigger question is what all this growth does to loyalty programs especially when comparing Hilton vs Marriott. Marriott Bonvoy and Hilton Honors each span well over a million rooms, and their luxury brands include Waldorf Astoria, Conrad, St. Regis, Ritz-Carlton and JW Marriott. But with so many of the new rooms being conversions and limited-service hotels where elite status buys less, that can reduce the shine of the programs. Lounge access and complimentary breakfast (a daily food and beverage credit at most U.S. Hilton hotels) are what business travelers chase but lounges are almost gone completely, and those perks are thinner at a select-service property. Hilton offers elites a fifth night free on standard reward stays and waives resort fees on free nights, and Hilton points pile up quickly through its brand credit cards with American Express. Long term, more hotels should mean more places to redeem, not just more places to earn. But the question also becomes how important those advantages are, and with both Bonvoy’s accelerated status earning, and Hilton’s Diamond via credit card option, the true value of those benefits continue to diminish.

Hilton returned $879MM to shareholders in the quarter, $2.9bn so far this year, and projects about $3.5 billion for 2026. It also moved its stock listing to Nasdaq on November 5, 2026 under the same HLT ticker. IHG has completed $707MM of a $950MM buyback and expects to return over $1.2bn in 2026. Hyatt projects $375MM to $425MM in capital returns. Hotels are on a tear over the last couple of years and Hilton’s growth in room count demonstrates confidence in the market for the future.

Hilton is winning the room race and not the rate race, and Marriott is winning the profit race. I expect Hilton to hold the unit growth lead through 2027, but Marriott’s larger pipeline will make that lead uncomfortable. Hyatt will continue winning on rate while sitting out the growth sprint barring another acquisition which it has been drawn to as of late, which is exactly how a luxury-leaning brand should behave. Still, it’s impressive to see Hilton with such strong numbers for growing the brand.

Are Tree Saddles Really Better Than Treestands for Hunting Whitetails?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 8, 2026 12:01 PM EST

Early in my bowhunting career, I set up a ladder stand on top of a ridge that had oaks and plenty of deer sign. On opening morning, I watched a deer parade go down a trail that was 20 yards out of range. I sat in that stand several more times and never had a deer come within bow range. If I had a mobile stand, I could have easily adjusted my hunting strategy to move closer to the active trail.

Mobile hunting for whitetails is all about making aggressive moves and constantly hunting fresh spots. Mobile hunters carry everything they need for a hunt so they can set up on the fresh sign and easily adapt to the conditions. That’s why they need lightweight and easy to use hunting platforms.

That old faithful ladder stand just won’t cut it when you need to slide another 100 yards closer to a bedding area. A mobile setup is also great for public land where you can’t or don’t want to leave your stand. It’s also less expensive than buying multiple permanent stands.

There are three main choices for the mobile hunter: a climber, a hang-on stand, and a tree saddle. I’ve hunted from all three of these platforms in my search for the perfect mobile hunting set up. To help with this comparison, I also reached out to Aaron Warbritton, from The Hunting Public YouTube channel. He’s been at the tip of the spear of the mobile hunting trend and offers a wealth of experience. Together we’ll see how each platform stacks up against each other in five categories: weight, setup, huntability, comfort, and price.

A heavy pack can feel fine for a few minutes, but when yards turn to miles, you feel every pound. For hunting deep in public land, a lightweight set up is a must. Weights will differ depending on the model of stand and climbing method. These weights will give you an idea of what you can expect a complete kit to weigh. Here’s how all three systems compared:

Model: Tethrd Mantis Saddle and Predator platform.

Kit Includes: Saddle, tether, platform, lineman’s belt, and three climbing sticks.

Total Weight: 14.1 pounds. *The three climbing sticks separately weigh 7.5 pounds.

Kit Includes: Stand, safety harness, lineman’s belt, and three climbing sticks.

Total Weight: 23.9 pounds. *The three climbing sticks separately weigh 7.5 pounds.

If weight is your primary concern then a saddle is the clear winner. You can even make the saddle lighter by using a lighter climbing system.

Weight isn’t only a factor when carrying a stand, it also matters for setting it up. This is especially true when hanging a saddle platform or hang-on stand. When you climb to the height you want to hunt, you have to hold the stand or platform in one hand while tightening the stand’s straps with the other. A light platform for a saddle, like the Predator platform (3.3 pounds), is much easier to hang then the heavier base of a hang-on stand (12.2 pounds).

“I would say that now 90 percent of my hunts are with a saddle and a platform; just because it’s lighter, the footprint is smaller, and it’s faster for me to set up,” Warbritton says.

With a saddle and a hang-on, you’ll need your choice of hang-on stand and a climbing method. I use the Lone Wolf Alpha stand and climbing sticks. The stand and sticks were designed to pack neatly together making it a very convenient and easy to use system. You can climb the tree and hang your stand in one trip with practice and some paracord. I’m pretty slow and careful when climbing and it usually takes me 15 to 20 minutes to climb and get set. The hang-on is fast, but the saddle is slightly faster and easier for me to set up.

A climber is attached to the tree before you climb and doesn’t require climbing sticks. There’s also no platform to hang once you reach hunting height. You can scale a tree fairly quickly with one, and it’s a less technical climb than a saddle or platform. However, that speed comes at the cost of versatility. Climbers need a straight, limbless tree, which isn’t always available. I much prefer the versatility of other platforms that allow me to climb almost any tree. If you live in an area that has a lot of trees suitable for a climber, then these negatives might not be such a big deal.

The size of a climber is another negative. “I haven’t hunted out of climbers a ton, but I’ve hunted out of them enough to know I don’t want to use them. Their footprint is so big that when you’re packing them in, the stand gets tangled on stuff,” Warbritton says.

Another reason to move away from climbers is the noise they make when climbing. It’s discouraging to put a lot of effort slipping into your spot, but then make a scraping noise as you ascend into position. This is especially true when hunting close to bedded deer.

By comparison saddles and hang-ons are much stealthier to carry and set up. If you are proficient with either of them, you can be up a tree within earshot of a bedded buck without issue.

Proficiency doesn’t come overnight with any of these platforms, but tree saddles especially take time to learn. The saddle has a steeper learning curve because it’s so different than a traditional treestand. The first time you lean back into your saddle it might feel like leaning back on a rocking chair at the edge of a cliff. However, that uneasy feeling quickly fades and I personally feel much safer in my saddle than I ever felt in a hang-on or climbing treestand. That security comes from the fact that if you fall off your platform it is extremely easy to get back on because the tether is always pulled tight—making the drop almost unnoticeable.

Beyond the saddle’s learning curve, the hang-on and saddle are neck and neck when it comes to packing, setting up, and hunting out of them.

“They’re really close. They definitely have their differences. Whichever one that you decide to use, you need to practice with it. Whether you’re using climbing sticks or strap on steps, you need to practice packing, hanging, and then shooting out of them because they’re different,” Warbritton says.

When the rut kicks in and you basically live in a tree all weekend, a comfortable stand is a must. While all three mobile options are pretty comfortable, the standard treestand seat is hard to beat. Warbritton agrees with that preference.

“The only thing I like about a treestand is if I’m going to sit all day, I’d rather have a treestand,” he says.

Tree saddles are comfortable, but a lot of the comfort in a saddle depends on your tether height because it controls the weight distribution. You’ll need to find a sweet spot that works for you and that takes some practice. I haven’t found that sweet spot for comfort just yet, but I do think that I’ll eventually get the saddle to be as comfortable as my treestands.

Like weight, the price will depend on the manufacturer and model. Just like our weight evaluation, we’ll compare the products I have personally owned and used.

A Tethrd mantis tree saddle, platform, lineman’s belt, tether, and a set of Lone Wolf climbing sticks will run you around $650. Without climbing sticks the system is about $500. If you want to add some accessories like Ropeman ascenders, pouches, and a recliner, you’ll be looking at spending around $900. It’s not a cheap investment, and you’ll have to decide if the above-stated benefits outweigh the cost.

Hang-ons and climbers are less expensive. A premium climber like the Summit Explorer SD and Hunter Safety System safety harness will run you around $450. A Lone Wolf Alpha hang-on, climbing sticks, Hunter Safety System safety harness, and lineman’s belt will set you back around $500. Again, prices will vary depending on what models, climbing methods, and add on accessories you choose.

Warbritton and I both prefer tree saddles because they are lightweight, easy to pack, fast to set up, and make mobile hunting easier. Is it the best system for you? You’ll have to evaluate the features against your hunting needs to make that decision. The good thing is, all three systems will give you the ability to be a mobile hunter.

“I just think that either way you’re going to be hunting mobile and you’re going to have a lightweight setup that’s going to allow you to see more country, learn more stuff, and potentially kill more deer,” Warbritton says.
