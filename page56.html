JSX Buys Bankrupt Airline In Major Expansion Beyond 30-Seat Semi-Private Flights

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

JSX has acquired bankrupt New Pacific Airlines, securing a regulatory pathway to operate aircraft with more than 30 passenger seats. The airline, which abandoned its ambitious Boeing 757 operation in November 2025, was acquired in August 2026, although the deal only became public following a November 20 Department of Transportation (DOT) filing, as reported by AeroTime.

Rather than introducing new aircraft, JSX intends to transfer existing Embraer ERJ145-LRs into the acquired airline, increasing seating capacity from 30 to 42 passengers. The acquisition also creates opportunities beyond its immediate plans, potentially including higher-capacity turboprops, while providing greater flexibility amid ongoing regulatory scrutiny of JSX's operating model.

JSX reportedly acquired New Pacific for approximately $3.1 million in consideration. According to the November 20 DOT filing, the airline intends to restart operations under the JSX Air brand using two ERJ145-LRs leased from sister company Delux Public Charter, increasing to six aircraft by the fifth month. Projections include 10,106 flights and approximately 309,000 passenger boardings during the first year.

The key acquisition is New Pacific's FAA Part 121 operating certificate. JSX currently flies under Part 135, selling individual seats through the Part 380 public charter framework. This limits its aircraft to 30 passenger seats, even though its Embraer jets were originally designed to carry more. New Pacific's certificate provides a pathway to operate 42-seat aircraft, subject to regulatory approvals.

The move also offers regulatory flexibility. AP New previously reported how the FAA has proposed tightening oversight of public charter operators, following criticism from major airlines and pilot unions. Although those changes remain unresolved, a Part 121 operation could provide JSX with an alternative growth platform. Responding to questions about the acquisition, the company told View From The Wing:

"JSX is continually evaluating opportunities to support its long-term growth and bring its distinctive flying experience to more customers and more communities. As part of that work, we are pursuing additional pathways that could provide even more ways to deliver our award-winning service in the future."

Originally known as Northern Pacific Airways, the airline planned to connect North America with Japan and South Korea through Anchorage, using Boeing 757-200 aircraft. Inspired by Icelandair's connecting-hub model, the strategy unraveled following Russian airspace restrictions in 2022. The carrier lacked the ETOPS approval required for alternative routings, forcing management to seek other markets.

In July 2023, the airline instead launched domestic flights between Ontario, California, and Las Vegas, subsequently adding Reno and Nashville. Rebranded New Pacific Airlines in September 2023, it abandoned scheduled operations in April 2024 to focus on charter flights. Management then pursued corporate and sports-team charters, converting its Boeing 757s from 181 seats to just 78 premium seats.

Paxex.aero reports that proposed routes would average 230–345 miles (370–556 km), with approximately six daily sectors per aircraft. Higher capacity could improve economics, although retaining JSX's spacious cabin, currently featuring individual seats and side tables on some aircraft, may become challenging.

The acquisition could also benefit JSX's turboprop strategy. The airline introduced ATR 42-600 flights in December 2025 with 30 premium seats per aircraft, and has outlined plans for up to 25 ATR aircraft, potentially including the larger ATR 72-600. Under Part 121, both types could theoretically accommodate substantially more passengers, provided New Pacific secures the necessary aircraft-specific operating approvals.

Only the 42-seat ERJ145 configuration has been announced. Higher-capacity ATR operations remain hypothetical.

Ultimately, JSX could develop two complementary offerings: its established 30-seat premium service and higher-capacity flights under Part 121. Its expanding ATR operation already demonstrates a willingness to diversify aircraft types, while New Pacific's certificate offers flexibility to accommodate further growth. The acquisition therefore gives JSX more than additional seats: it provides an alternative regulatory platform without requiring the airline to abandon its existing business model.

What you just touched can change how the next thing feels

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

A study in rats found that frontal brain activity helped predict how an earlier vibration would bias judgments about the next one.

What you feel right now depends partly on what you felt a moment ago. In rats trained to judge vibrations, the primary sensory cortex mainly tracked the current vibration.

A region of the frontal cortex represented both the current and previous vibrations, and its activity helped predict how the previous vibration biased the rats’ judgments.

The study was carried out by neuroscientists at SISSA, the International School for Advanced Studies in Trieste, Italy.

“Perception is often thought of as a direct mapping of the external world,” explained Mathew Diamond, a cognitive neuroscientist at SISSA.

“Yet what we experience depends not only on what is happening now, but also on what happened moments ago. In short, every percept is constructed against a backdrop of recent experience.”

Scientists call this effect serial dependence, and it’s easy to picture. “Imagine feeling a vibration with your fingertip (for instance, from the cell phone) and then feeling a second vibration shortly afterward,” Diamond said.

“The second stimulus will not be experienced as an independent event; instead, perception is systematically biased by prior inputs. The brain, in effect, carries traces of the recent past into the present.”

“Perception is not a photograph of the world. It is more like a film, in which the experience of each frame is influenced by those that came before.”

Psychologists have known about the effect for a long time. Where the brain does the mixing, and how, has been much harder to work out.

Theories of “predictive processing” propose that the brain constantly combines incoming signals with expectations built from experience. What nobody had clearly shown was the neural machinery that brings the two together.

To find it, Diamond’s team trained rodents on a simple touch task. On each trial the animal felt a stimulus and had to decide whether it was weak or strong.

Meanwhile, the researchers recorded the activity of hundreds of neurons.

The animals’ judgments were consistently pulled by the previous trial. After a strong stimulus, the next one was more likely to be judged weak.

After a weak one, the next was more likely to be judged strong. A medium-strength touch could feel faint or firm depending on what had come right before it.

“The key finding emerged from comparing two cortical regions,” said lead author Iacopo Hachen.

“Activity in the primary sensory cortex mainly reflected the stimulus currently being processed, a direct representation of the ongoing input.”

“By contrast, neurons in a frontal cortical area carried information about both the current stimulus and recent sensory history. There, neural representations of the present were embedded within a context established by the past.”

The first stop for touch signals in the cortex, in other words, stayed true to the present. The frontal region carried the present and the recent past together.

The frontal region also helped predict the animals’ choices.

When its activity rose or fell from one trial to the next, the animals’ judgments shifted in step.

“Variations in this frontal-cortex activity predicted corresponding variations in perceptual judgments from one trial to the next, suggesting that while the sensory cortex faithfully reports the ongoing input, the frontal cortex integrates that input with recent experience to shape perception,” Hachen explained.

One region reports what is happening, and the other decides what it means given what just happened.

The researchers also identified a possible mechanism at the level of individual cells.

Fast-spiking neurons played a prominent role in how the frontal region represented both vibrations. Their firing patterns suggest they may be inhibitory neurons.

There was also an unexpected finding. While a touch was happening, the frontal neurons encoded it as an ongoing sensory event.

Once the animal committed to its answer, the code switched to a different, memory-like format. That stored version lasted and went on to bias the next decision.

In other words, the brain doesn’t just keep a recording of what happened. It rewrites the experience into a new format as it moves from perception into memory.

That rewritten trace then colors the next moment.

The authors see the work as evidence for a basic principle of how the brain works. Perception comes from a constant back-and-forth between new sensory evidence and traces of the recent past.

“Our findings suggest that the brain does not treat each moment in isolation. Instead, the recent past becomes part of the neural representation of the present, a unitary construct that mirrors our experience,” Hachen concluded.

Understanding this exchange could help explain how prediction normally supports behavior, and how it might go wrong in neurological and psychiatric disorders.

The study is published in the journal Science Advances.

Why SK Hynix Stock Skyrocketed Today

The chipmaker rose after reports of interest from a high-stakes bidder.

By Danny Vena, CPA – Nov 23, 2026 at 5:36PM EST

Shares of SK Hynix (SKHY +20.00%) charged sharply higher on Wednesday, jumping as much as 23.7%. By the time the market closed, the stock was still up 20%.

The catalyst that sent the semiconductor specialist higher was news of a high-profile potential suitor.

SK Hynix was reportedly the subject of takeover interest from Japanese investment holding company and tech conglomerate SoftBank Group (SFTBY +19.27%). Chairman Masayoshi Son approached numerous Wall Street banks to discuss financing options to acquire the chipmaker for $1 trillion, according to a report that first appeared in The Information. The price under consideration would have been roughly 1.7 times SK Hynix's market cap of $576 billion as of Friday's market close.

The sheer magnitude of the offer and the potential national security implications were just a few of the obstacles that reportedly scuttled the deal, though it isn't clear whether a formal offer was ever tendered.

SoftBank has made no secret of its ambition to achieve end-to-end control over all elements of the artificial intelligence (AI) semiconductor supply chain. The company already owns data center chip designer Ampere Computing and power infrastructure specialist SB Energy, as well as an 86% controlling stake in chip designer Arm Holdings. SoftBank also holds a position in AI start-up OpenAI.

Bringing SK Hynix into the fold would have given the company a direct pipeline to DRAM, NAND flash, and high-bandwidth memory (HBM). However, in a statement, it said, "SoftBank explores many opportunities, and this one is no longer under active consideration." The wording suggests the deal was, in fact, explored.

So is SK Hynix worth $1 trillion? It's doubtful. A premium of that magnitude would likely have drawn the ire of smaller SoftBank investors, even as Son holds a dominant 33% stake in the company. Moreover, as Korea's dominant maker of HBM, it's unlikely the deal would have passed regulatory muster.

At less than 7 times earnings, SK Hynix stock appears attractively priced. However, the historical boom-and-bust cycle has some investors concerned, and the jury is still out on whether the company can maintain its juicy profit margins while the future of AI plays out.

Written, directed by and starring Markiplier, Iron Lung left critics somewhat divided but was a thorough smash with audiences and at the box office.

Now, nearly a year after the film hit theaters, Markiplier has offered a new update on his Iron Lung adaptation, revealing that it is officially getting a DVD and Blu-ray release. The horror hit, which is currently available for pre-order in both of its versions, is slated to release in three weeks, with the filmmaker indicating it will arrive around December 13, when he will also host a special livestream offering a complete behind-the-scenes look at the creation of the film and its unanswered questions.

In addition to the film itself, the Iron Lung Blu-ray and DVDs, which cost $30 and $20 respectively, are said to come with over three hours of never-before-seen bonus content, including a behind-the-scenes documentary entitled One with the Blood running just over two hours long, as well as various other featurettes and yet-to-be-announced content.

The long wait for an Iron Lung physical release is sure to have been a surprising one compared to the general three-to-four-month turnaround for other films. However, as Markiplier explains in the video above, the reason for this stemmed from a desire to prevent losing creative control over his hit movie, and the desire to give an in-depth Blu-ray experience.

It certainly falls in line with the way the filmmaker treated Iron Lung throughout its initial development, which was in and of itself a slow-but-steady process. Air New Zealand has been looking to return to the UK market for some time. Since then, passengers traveling between the UK and New Zealand have had to rely on connecting flights through hubs in the Middle East, Asia or Australia.

That being said, for the upcoming summer 2027 season, the New Zealand flag carrier had sought to secure slots at Heathrow. According to Airport Coordination Limited's (ACL) Initial Coordination Report for London Heathrow, the Star Alliance member requested 434 slots for the season. However, none of these were approved.

Air New Zealand had been flying to London for several decades. The airline initially served London Gatwick Airport (LGW) from Auckland, with stops in Papeete, Tahiti, and Los Angeles. In 1994, it moved its operations to Heathrow, where it continued to fly via Los Angeles. Between 2006 and 2013, the airline also offered flights via Hong Kong.

However, the Star Alliance member eventually decided to discontinue its London service due to low passenger numbers. It is unclear what routing it would have used had it secured the slots for summer 2027. Los Angeles appears to have been one possibility, as a spokesperson previously told Simple Flying that the airline would most likely operate the service via the US city using Boeing 777 aircraft.

Interestingly, Air New Zealand had also applied for slots at London Gatwick for the previous summer season and was actually successful in securing them. However, the airline never launched the flights, as it was facing aircraft availability issues at the time. Up to four of its 14 Boeing 787-9 Dreamliners were grounded simultaneously due to problems with Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 engines and maintenance delays.

These aircraft have since returned to service. Nevertheless, aircraft availability remains a concern. According to ch-aviation, Air New Zealand currently has nine 777-300ERs and 16 787-9s, although only 11 of the latter are in active service. The airline is also awaiting deliveries of three additional 787-9s and five 787-10s, with its outstanding Dreamliner orders expected to be fulfilled by 2028.

However, Boeing has faced difficulties increasing 787 production to its target of ten aircraft per month, partly due to supply-chain delays involving General Electric GEnx engines. These issues have affected deliveries to customers, including Air New Zealand, although planespotters.net data shows that the airline received two Dreamliners in October 2026.

Apart from new aircraft deliveries, the Star Alliance member is also refurbishing its existing widebody fleet. As of late October, 13 of its 14 787-9s had completed cabin upgrades, which include replacing the older inward-facing Business Premier seats with private suites, updating Economy cabins and introducing bunk-style sleeping pods on selected aircraft.

The final Dreamliner is expected to return to service before the end of the year. The airline is also scheduled to begin refurbishing its 777 fleet in March 2027. All of which are expected to undergo the upgrades by the end of 2027, meaning aircraft will need to be temporarily withdrawn from service while the work takes place.

Air New Zealand is one of the principal airlines operating in the South Pacific, with a fleet for NZ's domestic needs and ultra-long-haul routes.

The exact reason why Air New Zealand did not receive any slots has not been confirmed. However, Heathrow is one of the most slot-constrained airports in the world, and securing additional slots is difficult. According to ACL's report, airlines requested a total of 26,832 slots for summer 2027, but only 151 were allocated, just 0.6% of the requests.

Xiamen Air, which recently launched a weekly service between Xiamen and Heathrow, received most of the approved slots. However, several other airlines were unsuccessful. These included Alaska Airlines, which only began flying to Heathrow from Seattle in May and requested 434 slots for next summer. Emirates also failed to secure any of the 1,920 slots it requested, while Virgin Atlantic, Delta Air Lines, Air India and Riyadh Air were denied all 1,798, 556, 1,736 and 868 slots they sought, respectively.

Put out of mind the scenes of cheering you may have seen of Jensen Huang’s return to the headquarters of perhaps the world’s most important company, Nvidia, marking his personally triumphant comeback from being unanimously ousted by his board.

Get out of your thoughts any happy notions that you have reason to celebrate just because your money, like the money of so many millions of Americans and people well beyond the country’s borders, is tied up in the U.S. stock market, which soared at this surprising turn in the news after slumping badly in response to the scandals that had swirled around Huang.

What has happened in recent days, culminating in Huang’s return in the face of allegations he sustained a secret affair with a woman who was in touch with China’s spy agencies, is bad news for an economy that has grown increasingly imbalanced with the enormous growth of artificial intelligence. Far better for the U.S. economy and for everyone who has a stake in it would have been a reckoning for Nvidia that forced it to undertake the hard work of corporate reform and rebuilding trust.

Nvidia, for those who have not been paying attention, makes the most indispensable product in the world of AI: graphics processing units, a specialized type of chip, so capable and advanced that it has few real competitors, including, crucially, among well-funded Chinese companies. Nvidia, however, is much more than even this. As I have written before, it sits at the center of a highly incestuous economy of AI companies, including most of the big service providers, which it funds to keep purchasing its chips. The circular funding arrangements that Nvidia sustains with its largest customers—companies with extraordinary financial leverage and seemingly bottomless capital needs—is what keeps the AI economy’s party going. Without this, the merry-go-round of this new sector would grind to a halt and make what many thought looked like a bursting bubble in recent days seem like a minor blip.

Add to this the fact that the U.S. government and President Donald Trump have seemingly become convinced that everything and anything must be permitted with Nvidia, and with AI in general, or else China will overtake the United States in the most important technology of the near future. What this gives you is something more frightening than too big to fail: a company declared too strategically important to question or to hold to account.

The reality is that almost everything about the present picture is messed up and deeply worrisome. Let’s start with the fact that Trump, who already leads a government largely unconstrained by Congress and with wildly inflated executive powers, has taken so freely to intervening in the running of major companies. In Huang’s case, he played a central role in his reinstatement, as Monday’s news revealed.

There is nothing reassuring about this, but it gets worse. There is no reason to believe that Trump has an even remotely sophisticated grasp of the technologies at the heart of Nvidia’s business or its race against competitors. What we do know is that Trump has a weakness for very powerful and wealthy men, and Huang fits this billing. In the president’s universe, he is a winner, and Trump loves to associate himself with anyone invested with such an aura. Trump even posted an AI-generated photo of Huang intended to mirror a famous image of himself after surviving an assassination attempt, with the big difference that Huang was shown hoisting a red bra.

Trump, furthermore, is not known for a deep concern about ethics, and that is putting things gently. Where do ethics enter into this? Everywhere, from Huang’s honesty and the extent to which he was forthcoming about his affair with the suspicious Chinese national and shadowy customer, Alice Huang, as well as with at least one other woman who was his direct subordinate at Nvidia, in violation of corporate rules. Ethics are also involved in the way Huang ran his company, with a historically supine board, minimal internal controls on his behavior and oversight, and the way he promoted his family members. What matters is that nothing, inside the company or outside it, checked him. In fact, it seems likely that Trump, with his own record of serial marriages, rumored affairs, and children on the make, can relate.

But even these are mere starters. If competition with China deserves inclusion in any serious conversation about the future of AI, Trump’s treatment of Huang’s situation at Nvidia has been a running chronicle of flagrant disregard for national security. Let’s count the ways. First, when the U.S. government began to look into Alice Huang’s behavior on the suspicion that she might be a spy, it never informed Jensen Huang or gave him what would have been typical: a so-called defensive briefing so that he would know what to look out for and avoid giving away valuable trade secrets or selling his company’s most advanced chips without adequate controls preventing them from being shipped onward to China.

More broadly, historians may come to see Trump’s casual disregard for normal considerations of national security to be one of the most important and unusual hallmarks of his time in power. The examples are numerous. When shown evidence that Russia or China or some other powerful adversary is conducting secret operations against U.S. interests, Trump has often waved away concerns—in effect saying, well, we do that to them, too.

Last week offered the spectacle of Trump mugging in China with that country’s leader and Russia’s Vladimir Putin, with no hint that he had complained to them about offering targeting information to Iran that has enabled that country to attack U.S. bases in the Middle East, killing U.S. troops, as is the case with Russia, or supplying drone components to Iran, as with China. Trump went yet further, though, looking like a kid in a candy shop as he entered a room to greet an awaiting Putin and exclaiming, “He’s my friend.” Lest the message be lost on anyone, he later posted a picture of the meeting with this same message on social media.

Second, there is further reason to distrust the judgment of the U.S. government in the unfolding Nvidia saga. It lies in the vague and unforthcoming way its officials have spoken about Huang and his erstwhile Chinese mistress since their affair became public knowledge, always carefully avoiding clarity about whether or not she might have been a spy or whether his behavior damaged U.S. national security interests.

In fairness, the question of whether she was a spy involves some nuance. Alice Huang could very well have compromised U.S. national security without being a member or agent of any Chinese intelligence service in a formal sense. The lack of openness and straight language about the other question leaves little wiggle room, though. Through the Singapore-based company she worked for, Alice Huang was purchasing Nvidia’s most advanced chips, which fall under explicit U.S. export controls to China. This puts the public in a bewildering place. On the one hand, people are asked to believe that the United States must beat China in a zero-sum race toward the AI future. On the other, given the lack of candor about these Nvidia sales and the damage they may have caused to U.S. interests, we are being told, in effect, one of Trump’s favorite messages: Smile, and be happy. Lots of money was being made.

Taken together, Nvidia and Jensen Huang seem to concentrate enormous and growing risk for the entire public, as does the broader AI sector. Remember that Anthropic, the leading AI provider that went public recently, has warned of the potential power of its systems becoming so awesome as to having the potential to lead us all to an extinction-level event. And when the world’s top AI power is led by someone with such a casual disregard for safeguards, for strong governance, for ethics and for transparency, a company too important to question invites a peril that risks falling on us all.
