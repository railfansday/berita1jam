ByteDance is reportedly working on its own AI agent

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Chinese company wants to compete with Meta’s Muse.

ByteDance wants to take on Meta's Muse with its own AI agent. The company is readying an AI assistant that could be revealed in "the coming weeks," Bloomberg reports.

The report doesn't detail what the agent will be called or how it will work, but says that it will "serve a similar role" to Meta's Muse. Muse has already drawn more than 10 million users and CEO Mark Zuckerberg has predicted the AI agent could generate more revenue per user than its existing "family" of apps. The agent is also expected to play a significant role in Meta's AI glasses, and the company has previewed a standalone "charm" for Muse.

It's not clear if ByteDance is also planning on incorporating its agent into hardware. The AI assistant is one piece of the Chinese company's strategy to compete with frontier AI companies. ByteDance cofounder Zhang Yiming recently told employees that it would likely take "until at least 2029 or 2030" for the company's AI efforts to catch up, Bloomberg reported. Within the AI space, ByteDance has been primarily known for its video generator, Seedance. Clips from the video model went viral on social media platforms earlier this year, which eventually drew legal scrutiny from US-based studios.

If the TikTok maker does introduce its own AI agent, it would also likely raise new concerns about data privacy and how much access personal agents should get. ByteDance didn't immediately respond to a request for comment.

For Singles Day, World explains why real is the new attractive

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Singles’ Day falls on 11 November each year and has become the world’s biggest online shopping event.

New Harris Poll research on behalf of World finds that 81% of singles would be more likely to use a dating app if every profile were confirmed as a real person.

As millions of singles celebrate Singles Day on November 11, the biggest question in online dating is no longer “are we compatible?” but “are you even real?”

A new survey conducted by The Harris Poll on behalf of World shows that trust has become the most important factor in online dating. Nearly 3 in 4 online daters (74%) say they have suspected that someone they matched with on a dating app was not a real person. Catfishing, AI-generated profiles, bots and romance scams have turned swiping into a guessing game, and singles say they are tired of it.

World’s answer is Proof of Human: a way for anyone to show they are a real, unique person online without revealing who they are.

The survey paints a clear picture of how much doubt singles now bring to every match:

In other words, being real now outranks looks. For a growing number of singles, authenticity is the new chemistry.

Singles are not giving up on dating apps. They are asking for proof. According to the research, 81% would be more likely to use a dating app if every profile were confirmed as belonging to a real person.

They are also willing to do their part. Four in five singles (80%) say they would take steps to prove there is a real person behind their own profile, as long as their matches did the same. Verification, in other words, is becoming a two-way expectation rather than a burden.

World ID is a universal set of human Proofs that lets people show they are a real, unique person online without sharing personal data such as their name, birthday or address. It is built for a world where AI can generate convincing faces, voices and conversations at scale.

The core of the system is the Orb, a spherical verification device. A person stands in front of it, and the Orb uses images of the eyes and face to confirm they are a unique, living human. That check is turned into an anonymous cryptographic credential, a verified World ID, stored in the World ID App. The process does not require a name, email or gender.

The result is a simple promise for singles: the person on the other end of the chat is real.

The largest names in dating are moving in this direction. World first partnered with Match Group, the parent company of Tinder, on a World ID pilot in Japan in 2025. In April 2026, at World’s Lift Off event, the partnership expanded to the United States.

Tinder users who verify with World ID can display a human badge on their profile, signaling to potential matches that a verified, real person is behind it. Verification is optional.

Dating is only one use case. World ID is also being integrated into video calls, document signing, live event ticketing and gaming, with partners including Zoom and Docusign. The common thread is the same one singles describe in the survey: in the age of AI, knowing you are dealing with a real human is becoming essential.

The survey was conducted online in the United States by The Harris Poll on behalf of World from August 20 to 21, 2026, among 892 single adults aged 18 and over, of whom 649 have used online dating apps. Results are accurate to within +/- 3.6 percentage points at a 95% confidence level.

Primary Wave and Kobalt CEOs Discuss Their Billion-Dollar Partnership — And Why ‘You’ll See a Lot More’ Consolidation in Music

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In the latest episode of Billboard On the Record, Larry Mestel and Laurent Hubert talk about Primary Wave's acquisition of Kobalt publicly for the first time.

In February, Billboard broke the news that Primary Wave was nearing a historic deal to acquire Kobalt, arguably the world’s largest independent frontline publisher. Together, it was estimated that the newly combined company would be worth a staggering $7 billion in assets.

By July, the deal finally closed. But given that no one from the two companies had yet spoken to the press, major questions remained about how this new arrangement would operate in practice. All that was disclosed at the time was that Kobalt would operate independently of Primary Wave, one of the largest owners of iconic music catalogs in the industry, with a roster spanning from Whitney Houston to Stevie Nicks to Bob Marley to Prince.

But would Primary Wave move all of its catalogs to Kobalt? Did the deal include assets from Kobalt’s $700m catalog fund with Morgan Stanley? And how do Kobalt’s other verticals fit into the picture? The independent publisher is also home to AMRA, a global digital rights collection service that aims to speed up royalty collection and cut out middlemen outside of the U.S., and Kosign, its digital-first publishing service for middle-class songwriters who are not ready to sign a traditional deal.

News of the acquisition also came at a time of seismic change in the music industry. Universal Music Group’s Virgin division was finalizing its acquisition of Downtown Music Holdings and BMG was moving toward its purchase of Concord, reshaping the state of independent music in a matter of months.

To explain Primary Wave and Kobalt’s new deal for the first time and why all of this consolidation is happening now, Primary Wave founder/CEO Larry Mestel and Kobalt CEO Laurent Hubert joined Billboard’s music business podcast, On the Record.

To watch this episode of Billboard On the Record, check out the YouTube video below or click here for more viewing and listening options. An edited and condensed excerpt of the conversation can also be found below.

When did you guys start discussing this deal?

Hubert: I think the beginning of the conversation was some time in the summer of 2025. I think the first conversation was not a direct conversation with me but with another party at a particular dinner.

Mestel: I remember that dinner, and Primary Wave has historically been administration-agnostic. We’re into the value creation business, not the administration business. So we’ve always used Kobalt or Universal or some other administrators instead of creating our own internal administration platform.

The dinner Laurent was speaking about was a dinner that I believe Goldman Sachs had organized. I remember at that dinner, Jenny Kim, who is head of music now at Goldman, came up to me and said, “Have you considered Kobalt as an acquisition and as a partner?” And I said, “Well, we’re administration-agnostic, but I’ve known Laurent forever. Culturally, I think we’re very similar companies.” She said, “Well, you’re going to consider it because I’m bringing you the deal.” And so I said, “Great, let’s consider it.”

At the end of the day, it made a lot of sense for us because you could never have on your team too many great managers, and Laurent’s team are fantastic managers. We saw some jewels inside of Kobalt, not just the administration platform, which is really fantastic, but divisions like AMRA and Kosign, too.

Why not do administration in-house at Primary Wave?

Mestel: For us, the focus has always been on marketing, on branding, on creating content, doing Broadway, doing movies, doing immersive experiences, creating cosmetic lines, you know, gaming, etc. So there’s a real core competency to that, and it’s very hard for other companies that are competitors to recreate what we recreate. To build an administration platform takes a significant amount of capital and takes a significant amount of effort. And we always viewed it as a commodity, where there were a lot of industry players that did administration, but not many — in fact, I can’t even name any — that does really great marketing and value creation. So we’ve always stayed away from the administration side of the business because we could always rent it at relatively insignificant cost.

You’ve said before that Kobalt is going to stand on its own, but you guys have complementary strengths. Where are the two companies going to work together?

Hubert: I have to credit Larry for the vision for the business because initially, when we first talked, I understood the interest, but I did not fully understand the vision. And then I think Larry explained that very clearly, saying, “Hey, we understand Kobalt. We understand what it means in the market and that it’s a destination today.” He says also he built a destination — which I think is very unique — and we create value differently. You know, for Primary Wave, it’s really about how do you create engagement on the properties that they acquired, and for us, it’s how do we unlock greater value from a collection perspective.

From that perspective, we saw a direct opportunity, and some of it will be that we will cross some functionality that we have, and we’ll compound those benefits without talking about merger or integration. That was also interesting for us from a management perspective because we felt we didn’t have to go through an education process about Kobalt. We don’t have to be disrupted day one. We could keep going and do what we do the best we can do.

Larry, you mentioned that there were some jewels within Kobalt that you were super interested in. You mentioned AMRA, and it’s interesting because I’ve heard similar phrasing back when Francisco Partners purchased Kobalt in 2022. They called AMRA an “unpolished gem.” So I’m curious why you think it’s a gem as well?

Mestel: Those that aren’t using AMRA are going local. How long does it take for you to collect your money? Eight months, 12 months, 15 months. AMRA is going direct, and it’s very hard to recreate AMRA because of the history of AMRA and the licenses that they’ve put in place. So, it’s touching your money sooner, and there are less people with their hands in your pocket. It’s really that simple. I think the more people discover AMRA, the more people are going to want to use AMRA. We have $7.5 billion worth of assets now within Primary Wave. We’re going to be putting as much of our repertoire through AMRA as we possibly can.

Larry, do you plan to switch all the Primary Wave catalogs over to do publishing admin through Kobalt now as well?

Mestel: We have some great partners around the world, and I’ll name two: Kobalt and Universal. We give Universal quite a bit of our administration worldwide. They are an excellent administration organization. We have a great relationship with Jody [Gerson, CEO of UMPG] and Mark [Cimino, COO of UMPG] and David [Kokakis, chief counsel of UMPG] and the team there. Kobalt will be getting much more of Primary Wave repertoire than it has gotten in the past, but we won’t be taking anything away that we’ve given Universal, and we’ll continue to give Universal more work in the future because they’ve done a great job.

I guess you also can’t just stop in the middle of an administration agreement with another company, like UMPG, just because you bought Kobalt this year.

Mestel: We have very short-term deals, but we’re not going to do that. The reason is that when you do short-term deals, you keep partners engaged. If you put up a long-term deal, maybe there’s less incentive. So I like to do month-to-month, quarter-to-quarter, year-to-year. I don’t like much more than year-to-year.

We are very well capitalized, and the fact that we’re very well-capitalized means we don’t need advances because when you take advances, that means you lower your margin. I’d rather have a much higher margin, no advance.

Luminate CEO Rob Jonas recently came on this podcast and revealed that now 150,000 songs are being uploaded to streaming services every day. With the sheer volume of music now, how has that changed your approach?

Mestel: I think that’s much more relevant for Laurent than it is for me because we are in the icon and legend business. We’re buying only the greatest of the great.

Hubert: Frankly, in the past 10-15 years, the way we manage publishing has totally transformed. We went from an ownership to a consumption model. Obviously, we went from a few hundred income sources to literally thousands of income sources.

We also have seen fragmentation of copyright. It’s not uncommon to see five, seven writers on a song, and you have fragmentation of consumption. So if you look at those factors, it has compounded volume in [many] ways, and the challenge with volume is you need to manage that with great velocity because we have to account every quarter. [Just] because you have more to manage [doesn’t mean] that you can delay your accounting period, obviously. Plus, you also have [more] challenges with accuracy. Our job as we build the infrastructure — it’s a continuous exercise — is, how do we manage this?

I’ve often had a conversation with my CTO: How do we manage [Amazon Web Services] cost? All of it is in the cloud. You have volume growing at 30%-40% a year, and you have your cost of AWS growing at 10%-12% a year, and we have to manage the business. You have to find ways to, again, optimally manage that ecosystem without affecting velocity. It’s frankly a job of every day. It really is. It’s a natural tension in the business.

So, are publishers spending more than ever to try to collect all these royalties all over the world?

Hubert: We are. The challenge is that the value of that micro license or micro transaction is so small that you have to do it cost-effectively; otherwise you’d be losing money [to collect it]. Technology is obviously a major aspect of what we do. AI is, of course, but it’s not only AI — it’s about building infrastructure that can handle the volume. It’s a challenge. This is a topic that we talk about almost every day.

Kobalt announced a $700 million fund in 2023 to buy music catalogs. Is that part of this deal, or do you retain that at Kobalt?

Hubert: We retain [it], and that’s a conversation that I had [with Larry] because we have a different IP strategy and we deploy on different type of copyrights. For us, the idea here was to provide an additional service to our clients, and we provided all the services under a lending situation. But if people needed a capital event, we wanted to be able to say yes, we can do that for you as well. We created a fund in ’11 and a second fund in ’16. We created that fund in 2023, and we’ve been deploying the capital, obviously, not only on clients that we have because we know them and they come to us, but we’re also buying third-party copyrights.

Mestel: We turn down many more deals than we do, and so Laurent’s team is participating in our deal meetings. When there’s a deal that we’re reviewing that isn’t right for Primary Wave, we’re turning it over to Kobalt [now].

Larry, I think one of the most interesting things Primary Wave has done from a marketing perspective is the Whitney Houston slot machines in Las Vegas. How are those going? Are they a success?

Mestel: It has been a giant success for us. We started this in 2024, and we budgeted 200 machines a year. Now, we’ve done over 500 a year. We just renewed the deal with [slot machine manufacturer] IGT. We are having great success, but how do you measure success? Well, you can measure success on shipping machines and shipping more than your budget, but the real measure is that we’re making more money for the estate of Whitney Houston, which we own 50% of, [on slot machines] than the entire estate was generating six and a half years ago when we bought into the estate on that one deal.

Larry, you often talk about how you guys partner with the estates when you do these deals, but I know estates can be really sensitive about certain brand partnerships. Like, anything that involves sex, alcohol or even gambling. What was that conversation like with the Houston estate to decide that this was right for her legacy?

Mestel: It was easy. Whitney loved to play slot machines. It was very organic. We will not do a brand deal, a marketing deal, any type of brand alliance, movie, anything unless the artist estate 100% signs off and says yes.

Willard Ahdritz, founder of Kobalt, is stepping away from his role as chairman as part of this deal. Who is now filling that role?

Mestel: The reality is there’s only one person that matters, which is the CEO, which is Laurent. We were clear with each other day one that the reason why Primary Wave was doing this, a big chunk of the reason, was the management team in Laurent and Kobalt. It is an entrepreneurial company. Laurent runs it with very little interference at all from Primary Wave. We talk a couple times a week, so we’re very active on what we’re doing together. Kobalt is and will remain a separate entrepreneurially-run company.

Both Primary Wave and Kobalt have active funds to buy more catalogs. Kobalt’s is a $700 million fund, announced in 2023 with Morgan Stanley, and Primary Wave’s is a $2.2 billion fund with Brookfield, announced in the spring. How much of these funds have you both deployed so far?

Mestel: We are way ahead of schedule. We’ve deployed almost 60% of our most current fund, which we only closed back in April of this year. There have been some very, very large-scale deals, and our pipeline is pretty significant. So we anticipate being probably 75% invested at the end of the year and fully invested the first quarter of next year. We have many pools of capital. We have a permanent evergreen fund with Brookfield. Brookfield put $2 billion on [the] primary balance sheet four and a half years ago, whenever that was, 2022, and we have a lot of surprises.

Hubert: We’ve deployed about half a billion dollars of the $700 million, and we intend to spend the [remainder] of that $200 million going forward. So there will be a new fund [soon] … [Primary Wave and Kobalt] don’t go after the same targets at all. We typically buy more recent vintages. We buy copyrights where we have high incentives of digital royalties because we want to take advantage of the AMRA capabilities.

2026 has been a year full of major music business acquisitions. UMG’s Virgin Music Group acquired Downtown, BMG bought Concord and Primary Wave acquired Kobalt. On a macro scale, why do you think these acquisitions and this consolidation is happening now?

Mestel: I think you can see a lot more. I think as interest rates go up, I think you will see a lot of our competitors that are financially driven looking to sell. Primary Wave, by the way, has no debt, so we’re generating significant returns without that because of the alpha we’re creating as we market. A lot of our competitors are highly leveraged, so they’re taking very safe assets that generate cash flow every single day that would normally go back out into acquisition, or go back out in the form of dividends to investors. These companies are paying interest to banks, and as interest rates go up and they have to refinance their significant debt, they’re going to have a hard time. I don’t name competitors, but you’re going to see a lot of people that are going to be selling, I think, over the course of the next two and three years. So you’ll see a lot more consolidation, and that’s great for companies like Kobalt and Primary Wave, especially ones that are very well capitalized and have almost no debt.

But is it better for artists and songwriters?

Mestel: Well, it’s better if you’re with a company that has an infrastructure to create content for them and to actually market [their music]. Is it any good when an artist is acquired by a private equity firm? Probably not, because the first thing on their mind isn’t legacy protection, and it’s not turning a hit song that was a hit 30 years ago into a hit song again. It’s, how are they going to extract as much cash out of the asset as they possibly can, and then likely flip it? So yeah, I think it’s very good if the artist is being acquired by a company that has the ability to do something.

SpaceX Wants To Build Data Center Powered By Its Newest Product: Natural Gas

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

SpaceX filed its intention to build a new AI data center at its facility in Starbase, Texas, which will be powered by a new natural gas plant that it will also build. None of that sounds like space, but this is the company that SpaceX has become. The initial phase of this build-out is projected to cost $24 billion, though more phases are to come. Critically, this gives the rocket company, which is also an internet company, which is also an AI company, which is also a social media company, a way to make use of its newest product: natural gas. Again, space!

The company has been moving towards an expansion into fossil fuels for the last few months. In June, SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell said the company was making plans for "basically mining our own natural gas... to develop our own propellant and bring it to the rocket." The rocket in question being Starship, which SpaceX is essentially betting its future on. That takes a mixture of methane and liquid oxygen to fire up its thrusters; methane can be extracted from natural gas. For that reason, the company has made major moves to ensure its own supply. It hired for its own natural gas trader, normally an oil industry job for buying and selling the stuff, and is building its own pipelines in both Florida and Texas, per Bloomberg.

In addition, SpaceX rapidly spun up AI data centers in Tennessee and Mississippi earlier this year, powered by, you guessed it, natural gas. It spun them up so rapidly that the company is now being sued for ignoring environmental laws. SpaceX is apparently trying to go fast again for this new Texas endeavor, likely trying to get things rolling before a newer, bluer state government takes over. SpaceX wants to grab both the air pollution permits it needs plus a few tax breaks; Texas is expected to make both harder to get early next year, per Politico. To that end, Starbase's city government will convene on the matter in December. There's good reason to think the company will get what it wants: two of the city's three commission members are SpaceX executives. That's handy.

As for the power plant itself, it will feature 35 mobile natural gas turbines and produce around 750 megawatts of electricity. So it's now in the business of building power plants, buying and trading natural gas, and laying out pipelines, all in service of the enough data centers it's putting up. No wonder Vice President of Launch Kiko Dontchev is now saying, "Fundamentally we are an infrastructure company at this point." You don't say, Dontchev.

At this point, it's not too much of a stretch to say that SpaceX is just the Elon Musk Show, going into whatever industry he feels like at the moment. If his long-desired merger with Tesla goes through, then it will really just be a conglomerate with all sorts of fingers in all sorts of pies. Space will still be a part of what SpaceX does, but now that AI is earning it more revenue than rockets, it may become a smaller and smaller part. Mars has never looked so far away.

PIF could be looking for a way out of Newcastle United behind the scenes.

A Saudi Arabia Public Investment Fund-backed takeover was sealed at St James' Park back in 2021.

There was mass hysteria in the days after the news as the days of Mike Ashley could finally be forgotten.

Things started well and ultimately culminated with the League Cup victory in the 2024/25 campaign.

Eddie Howe was representative of the new era, but with his departure, things just haven't been quite as enjoyable.

PIF could now have their eyes on a potential departure with reports emerging from throughout the Premier League.

There has been no shortage of annoyance from the Magpies owners towards financial rules in England and Europe.

They believe they have been put in place to hamper smaller sides from challenging the established elite sides.

Many owners are feeling the same frustrations and are looking to jump ship.

The Independent reported on Wednesday that "at least" eight Premier League clubs are either officially or unofficially up for sale.

There is said to be so few buyers that it has become difficult to find new investors.

The report details a "stagnation" in the market, partially due to "ongoing global uncertainty".

It also notes the "rumours" of PIF's willingness to sell Newcastle, but that is all they are, rumours.

Whether it be true or not, it feels as if they are now stuck where they are, so investment is necessary.

There may be Magpies fans who point towards the latest cash injection from the owners as reason that they are sticking around for the long term.

The Athletic reported earlier in November that £95million was injected into the North East club in October.

This was the 11th time they have done so, taking their funding to over £600m in almost exactly five years.

Although many may get excited by that news, it is a way to deal with losses and not breach SCR.

The £95m is largely due to cover operational costs, something which would be necessary whether they are planning to stay or go.

It could actually be a way of leaving the Toon in good stead for a fresh investor to come in and move from there.

It would be interesting to know how PIF see the future, but it seems that no one is 100 per cent sure in the Premier League.

Postpartum depression vs. baby blues: 10 things to know, per the NIMH

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Learn how postpartum depression differs from the baby blues, plus risk factors, treatment, and where mothers and families can find help.

Feeling low in the days after giving birth is so common that many families treat it as part of having a baby. The harder question is when that feeling stops being normal. Far more American mothers are being diagnosed with postpartum depression than a decade ago. The share of U.S. mothers reported to have it was 9.4% in 2010 and 19% in 2021, according to a JAMA Network Open study reported by The Associated Press. Even that higher count leaves many women out. Perinatal depression goes unrecognized in up to 50% of cases, according to StatPearls. The rising count shows that doctors are noticing more mothers, not that the problem is under control.

Many mothers miss the illness because they are watching for it in the wrong weeks. The familiar picture is the baby blues, a stretch of low mood that comes soon after delivery and then fades. Because of that timing, families come to think of postpartum sadness as something that begins with the birth. Yet about 50% of major depressive episodes in the postpartum period start while the woman is still pregnant, according to StatPearls. A mother waiting for a dip after the birth may already have an illness that started during her pregnancy. As a result, she and the people around her may watch the weeks after the birth closely and overlook the months before it. Telling a passing dip from an illness depends on knowing which signs matter and where to go when they appear, and a plain guide can supply exactly that.

The National Institute of Mental Health set out to explain perinatal depression, how to spot it, and where to get help. The federal mental-health agency wrote its guide in plain language and covered what family and friends can do, the rare emergency that calls for a hospital, and the treatments now on offer. A guide can tell a mother what to look for, but whether she gets help depends on what happens after she looks. The slides that follow set each part of the guide beside the evidence, showing where its advice holds, where newer research adds to it, and where care still falls short.

The low feeling that new and expecting mothers are told to ride out is sometimes a diagnosable illness. Perinatal depression belongs to the group of illnesses known as mood disorders, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. It is a medical condition rather than one of the ordinary emotions of pregnancy and new parenthood. Mood changes may point to postpartum depression when they become severe or last more than two weeks. Either one can be a warning sign, so a mother whose symptoms are severe has reason to speak up without waiting out a second week. The symptoms can also be mild, and a mother who is still getting through each day can have the illness. Coping is not the same as being well. The signs to watch are how severe the symptoms are and how long they last.

Calling perinatal depression an illness matters because leaving it untreated carries risks well beyond sadness. University of Utah Health reports that a mother whose perinatal depression goes untreated may struggle to connect with her baby or neglect the child, and that she may think about suicide or try to take her own life. In Utah, suicide is the leading cause of maternal mortality, which means more mothers there die by suicide than from any other cause. An illness that can end this way needs to be caught early, not waited out in the hope that it lifts.

Recognizing the illness is where help begins, not where it ends. A diagnosis does not mean a mother stays with the care she is offered, and 80% of diagnosed women don't act on the treatment advice they receive, according to University of Utah Health. Stigma is one of the barriers between these women and care. Mothers who see their symptoms as an ordinary rough patch can miss the illness, and mothers who feel ashamed may stay away from treatment even after a diagnosis.

Knowing what perinatal depression is gives a mother words for what she is feeling. Getting from those words to care is the harder step.

Most new mothers will recognize themselves in at least one warning sign of depression, and that match is a reason to talk with a provider, not a diagnosis. The National Institute of Mental Health says that a woman who notices even one symptom from its list should get checked by a health care provider. That advice leans toward caution rather than alarm. Many mothers will feel low in the first days, because the baby blues affect approximately 50% to 75% of new mothers, according to StatPearls. The sign that separates depression from the blues is one that involves the baby. The NIMH lists trouble bonding with the baby as a symptom, while the blues leave a mother's daily functioning untouched. A mother who can't connect with her child has gone past anything the blues explain. The useful question is whether her symptoms get in the way of her days and her baby, not whether she feels them at all.

Depression doesn't always show up as sadness, and its first signs can be physical. The NIMH counts physical aches with no clear cause among the symptoms. According to StatPearls, a diagnosis requires at least five symptoms together, and one of them must be depressed mood or a loss of interest and pleasure. Aches therefore matter most when they come with a flat, joyless mood, and neither one should be dismissed as tiredness. A single hard day or one stray symptom isn't depression, but several signs that pile up and stay can be. A mother and the people around her should watch for a pattern rather than a moment.

One set of symptoms needs no weighing at all. Postpartum psychosis brings delusions, hallucinations, mania, paranoia and confusion, and no family could mistake those signs for ordinary low mood. A mother with these symptoms needs emergency care, and the NIMH tells families to call 911 or take her to an emergency room. The condition is rare, at 1 to 2 per 1,000 pregnancies, according to StatPearls.

Knowing the signs means knowing which ones call for a talk with someone soon and which ones mean go now.

A mother who falls ill wants to know why, and the honest answer is that perinatal depression has no single cause, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. She has no one trigger to find, fix or regret. "A woman is not to blame or at fault for having perinatal depression: It is not caused by anything she has or has not done," the agency said. Doctors can use risk factors to decide which women need closer watching, but they can't use them to tell any one mother whether she will get sick.

Researchers are now examining medications as one possible contributor, and so far the evidence is thin. Researchers at Dongguan Maternal and Child Healthcare Hospital counted 643 cases of perinatal depression linked to a drug in safety reports, according to Frontiers. With only a few hundred cases, medications remain an early lead rather than a known cause. Hormonal contraception, one of the drug groups that appeared in those reports, is not a new suspect. Swedish researchers had already examined the contraceptive levonorgestrel and depression in 703,157 women, according to a 2023 study reported by Frontiers. Many women of childbearing age use contraception, and the link may partly reflect who takes these drugs rather than what the drugs do. The same caution applies to lurasidone, another drug flagged in the reports. The illness it is prescribed to treat, rather than the drug itself, may account for the link. A flagged medication is a question to ask a doctor, not a verdict.

Researchers can't yet draw a reliable portrait of which mothers face the most risk, because 48.1% of the reports left out the mother's age, according to Frontiers. Any profile built from these cases would rest on an incomplete record. Scientists can use such data to choose which questions to test, but they can't use it to sort mothers into safe and unsafe. The practical lesson is narrow: a mother's medications belong in the conversation about her mood, alongside everything else in her life. No single finding gives her reason to blame herself or her prescription.

Treatment for perinatal depression is a set of options that a mother can weigh with her provider, not one fixed path. Antidepressants are the standard treatment for keeping depression from returning, University of Colorado Boulder reports, and the National Institute of Mental Health says the risk that they cause birth defects when taken in pregnancy is very low. A pregnant woman deciding whether to stay on her medication can make that choice on the evidence rather than out of fear. Stigma, cost, transportation and a shortage of therapists still keep many mothers from care. The institute also lists zuranolone, which comes as a pill, among the treatments for the illness, and a mother can take a pill without first finding a therapist or a ride to one.

Women who have been depressed before have the most at stake, with rates of perinatal major depression as high as 77% among them, according to University of Colorado Boulder. For these mothers, keeping the illness from coming back is the main goal, and a free program called MMB for Moms gives them another way to pursue it. "What we do know now is that for patients looking for a non-pharmacologic alternative to mitigate their risk for recurrence of depression, this program helps," said Dr. Lee Cohen, the study's senior author and director of the Ammon-Pinizzotto Center for Women's Health at Massachusetts General Hospital. For a woman with that history, the best outcome is an episode that never begins, which is why prevention belongs among the treatments.

A mother who is already depressed needs a different kind of help. MMB for Moms is designed to prevent relapse rather than to treat active depression, University of Colorado Boulder reports, and its developers say it is not meant to "treat" the illness. A mother in crisis can reach the 988 suicide crisis line. Having more options helps only when a mother matches the option to where she is: still guarding against a return, or already in an episode.

A mother who suspects she is depressed does not have to begin with a specialist she must find, book and wait for. Many mothers assume help means a psychiatrist, and that assumption can stop them before they start. The National Institute of Mental Health counts primary care doctors among the providers who can help with the illness, alongside psychologists, psychiatrists and social workers, and its list is a sample rather than a complete roster. Her first step can be an appointment she already keeps. She can also search for care through an online treatment locator from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration. Clinics and doctors' offices keep business hours, though, and some mothers need to talk to someone sooner.

The fastest help is a phone line built for pregnant women and new mothers, and it is open at night, when a mother may be alone with the sense that something is wrong. The National Maternal Mental Health Hotline, which the NIMH recommends, is available 24/7, according to the CDC. Both agencies describe the hotline as free and confidential, so a mother worried about a bill or about who might find out can still reach out. Help is available in English and Spanish, and a Spanish-speaking mother can describe what she feels in her own language. Mothers can call or text the line at 1-833-TLC-MAMA (1-833-852-6262).

Help can also start with other mothers, before any clinician has made a diagnosis. Postpartum Support International is one support group the NIMH names, and the CDC lists it as a community resource. A mother who isn't ready to call a stranger or book an appointment can find women there who have been through the same thing. Each kind of help serves a different need: a doctor for treatment, the hotline for an urgent moment, and other mothers for company along the way.

Often the hardest part is deciding that help is needed, and after that a mother has more places to turn than she might expect.

The people around a depressed mother are not bystanders, and what they do can shape whether and how quickly she gets better. "Treatment is central to recovery," the National Institute of Mental Health said. A relative can help keep her moving toward that care. Getting her treated also matters for the rest of the household. The federal child health institute NICHD warns that older children may miss their mother's support while she is ill. A relative who pushes for treatment is looking out for the whole family, not only for her.

One concrete way to help is to take baby care and household chores off her hands, the tasks NICHD lists for mothers to request from family and friends. When other people handle the feedings and the housework, a struggling mother has time to rest and to seek treatment. Asking for that help is one more task for her, and a depressed mother may find it hard to do. Relatives and friends who offer a specific task without waiting to be asked spare her that step entirely.

Support works best when people arrange it before the baby arrives rather than piece it together in a crisis. Expectant mothers should work out during pregnancy who can help after the birth, according to NICHD. With that plan in place, help is ready when the weeks after the birth begin. A support plan belongs with the other preparations for a baby, as ordinary as buying a crib. No one should treat it as a rescue to organize only after something goes wrong.

The family's job starts before the birth and doesn't end until she is getting care.

A mother who wants to know more than the guide covers will have to look in several places, because the federal government spreads its information on this illness across several offices. The National Institute of Mental Health lists five resources for further reading, each from a different federal health office, so she needs to know which office to turn to. Some of those offices write about her condition as one form of depression in women. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, for example, covers depression among women in general rather than the months around a birth. The Office on Women's Health writes about postpartum depression, or PPD, specifically, and its advice speaks to a new mother's situation directly. Which page she reads first matters, because general guidance on depression and guidance written for pregnancy and new motherhood answer different questions.

A woman worried about her risk may also want to read about prevention, not only treatment. "PPD is very difficult to treat," Mi Hillefors, deputy director of the NIMH Division of Translational Research, said. The reading list includes guidance from the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force on preventive interventions for perinatal depression. When an illness is hard to treat once it starts, it makes sense to learn about prevention before symptoms appear. A woman who is pregnant and concerned about her risk can start reading during the pregnancy rather than wait until something feels wrong.

The reading list is most useful as a guide to where to turn next, and a mother gets the most from it by going straight to the pages written for her situation.

A mother who hears that clinical trials matter may assume that a better treatment is ready for whoever signs up. The National Institute of Mental Health lists clinical trials among the ways a mother can find help, but the medicines in those trials are still being tested. The drugmaker Lipocine is studying an investigational pill version of brexanolone for postpartum depression, and the drug has not been approved, according to Psychiatric Times. A mother who joins a study like this one helps answer whether a new treatment works. She does not receive a treatment that is already known to work, and that is the clearest way for any mother to think about enrolling.

For a mother caring for a newborn, the hope is that she could feel better quickly without leaving home. "Our goal is to develop a treatment that provides rapid symptom relief through a 48-hour oral course taken at home," Mahesh Patel, president and chief executive officer of Lipocine, said, as reported by Psychiatric Times. The researchers will measure success by how much the mothers' depression scores change 60 hours after treatment begins, according to Psychiatric Times. In other words, they want to learn whether women feel better within days. Rapid relief is the goal the study is designed to confirm or rule out, and it should not be read as a finding.

The answer will come from a small group of women. Lipocine plans to enroll about 120 participants in the study, which began in September 2026, according to Psychiatric Times. With results still to come, nobody can yet say whether the pill helps mothers with postpartum depression.

The existence of a trial is no proof that a drug helps, and the mothers who enroll are the ones who will help researchers find out.

For readers who want to go further, the National Institute of Mental Health points to a single resource: MedlinePlus, from the National Library of Medicine. The same resource is offered in Spanish, so Spanish-speaking families get the same next step as everyone else. Reading can help a mother put a name to what she feels, but nobody on the other side of a web page will check on her or connect her with care. Reaching a person matters more because the stakes go beyond her own health to her baby's.

A newborn whose mother shows signs of depression after the birth is much more likely to die in infancy. Newsweek reports that infant deaths ran at 7.2 per 1,000 births when mothers had postpartum depressive symptoms and at 2.0 per 1,000 births when they did not. The higher risk also held for babies born preterm, so early birth alone doesn't explain the difference. "In this study, we found a consistent association between postpartum depressive symptoms and infant mortality, but this study can't tell us whether this association is causal," said Rebecca Woofter, a postdoctoral associate at Rutgers School of Public Health and the study's lead author. The link gives a mother reason to act on symptoms early, for her baby's sake as well as her own. It doesn't show that a mother's depression causes her baby's death.

Newsweek reports that the study identified mothers with symptoms through the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, a screening tool. A high score on that tool marks a mother for a closer look rather than confirming depression. When a screening flags her, she should reach a person.

Once she has finished the reading, a mother's next step is a conversation with someone who can help.

Anyone may copy the words of the federal guide to perinatal depression and pass them along, and that freedom matters because recognizing the illness depends on the warning signs reaching whoever is close enough to notice them. The guide's written text belongs to the public domain, and no one has to seek approval before reusing it, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. A clinic, a support group or a worried relative can copy that text and give it to a new mother without asking anyone first. The only condition is that every copy credit the National Institute of Mental Health, part of the National Institutes of Health. A mother reading a photocopy can then see that the advice comes from the federal mental-health agency, not from an anonymous flyer. Family members and friends who want to help can use that permission in a concrete way and put the guide's words in her hands.

Anyone who shares the guide should first make sure the copy is current. The guide was last revised in 2023, and a printed copy is only as up to date as the day someone printed it. A clinic or relative preparing copies should check the NIMH website for the latest version rather than hand out an old one. Passing along the warning signs takes no one's approval, but the larger gap between a mother knowing the signs and actually getting care remains.

The guide is useful only when someone reads it, recognizes the signs in her and makes the call.
