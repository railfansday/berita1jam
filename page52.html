Half of voters said they are paying more this year in premiums and out-of-pocket costs, according to a new national poll.

Lake Research Partners and Bellwether Research & Consulting surveyed 1,000 voters between Oct. 20 and Oct. 28 on behalf of the National Health Council, and 50% said their premiums and out-of-pocket costs are higher this year compared to last year. Among those with commercial insurance, 63% said their premiums have risen and 60% said their out-of-pocket costs have grown year-over-year.

In addition, 25% of those surveyed said they had a claim for a prescription denied, and 24% said they waited more than two weeks for a prior authorization decision. Twenty-one percent said that they struggled to find a specialist.

Healthcare costs and the broader economy were major factors in Democrats' significant victories in the latest midterm election. And the NHC poll finds that voters expect Congress to act in addressing the costs that they face.

Most of the surveyed voters (78%) said they believe that the healthcare system warrants major changes or a complete overhaul, and 84% said they would support a candidate that would make it a priority to reform the healthcare insurance system.

However, 74% of those surveyed rated the effort from elected officials to address affordability and access as either fair or poor.

“Holding onto a plan isn’t the same as being protected by it. Patients are watching their costs rise and their coverage change, and they’re worried about what that means for their families down the road,” said Randall Rutta, CEO of the National Health Council, in an announcement. “The new Congress has a real opportunity to change that, and patients across the country will be watching.”

The survey highlights a number of potential policies that would have broad voter support. For example, 91% of those surveyed said they would support eliminating surprise medical bills and policies to require price transparency.

In addition, 90% said they would back efforts to reduce out-of-pocket costs, while 89% said they favor requiring health plans to cover treatments that were prescribed by doctors. Eighty-eight percent support efforts to increase screening and early detection for medical risks.

"Voters aren't asking lawmakers to talk about health care differently," said Christine Matthews, President of Bellwether Research and Consulting, in the release. "They want to see it work differently. Reform is a priority they expect the new Congress to deliver on."

The U.S. is preparing to raise tariffs on China, the E.U., and 14 other countries

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Trade Representative is expected to unveil results of its industrial subsidies investigation as soon as Tuesday

Allen J. Schaben / Los Angeles Times via Getty Images

The U.S. is getting ready to hike duties on imports from China, the European Union and 14 other economies in response to their industrial subsidies, according to The Wall Street Journal.

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer could announce the results of his office's investigation as soon as Tuesday, according to Bloomberg. The timing could change, according to The Wall Street Journal. Tariff rates and an effective date are unclear.

The probe falls under Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act, a provision that lets a president act alone in levying tariffs in response to unfair trade conduct, according to Bloomberg. The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative opened the investigation in March, following the high court's invalidation of President Donald Trump's earlier worldwide tariffs.

Greer has said that state backing, subsidies among it, has left the targeted economies with more industrial production capacity than they need. He has said that this lets companies in those economies undercut competitors abroad on price, which hurts American manufacturers. The U.S. Trade Representative's office declined to comment, according to The Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg. The White House did not respond to requests for comment from The Wall Street Journal or Bloomberg.

The new tariffs are expected to apply to the bulk of goods coming from the targeted nations, among them China, Japan, South Korea and the E.U.'s 27 member states, according to The Wall Street Journal. Steel, aluminum and automobiles are expected to be left out because they are already subject to duties under another law tied to national security.

Administration officials have said they plan to set rates close to the ones Trump put in place during 2025, ahead of the Supreme Court's invalidation of his initial round of tariffs in February 2026, according to The Wall Street Journal. Countries with trade agreements, including Japan, the E.U. and South Korea, are expected to face rates of 15% or lower. Vietnam and India could be hit with steeper rates that reflect the agreements they struck with Trump.

China is expected to be charged a steeper tariff than many of its peers because Trump officials have described its subsidies as especially far-reaching, according to The Wall Street Journal. The administration could add wording that would let it increase tariffs if a country's overcapacity in industry grows worse or if the country fails to change its policies during later talks.

The new duties are expected to stack on top of tariffs announced in July over forced labor, according to The Wall Street Journal. Those tariffs, which run from 10% to 12.5% on about 60 economies, are being contested in a lawsuit before the Court of International Trade, according to Bloomberg.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/providers/academic-hospital-heads-petition-congress-2-year-delay-cms-340b-cuts" hreflang="en">Academic hospital heads petition Congress for 2-year delay to CMS' 340B cuts</a>

Academic hospital heads petition Congress for 2-year delay to CMS' 340B cuts" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Dozens of academic health system and teaching hospital chief executives are turning to Congress in a bid to blunt a controversial subsidy cut coming to Medicare Part B in the new year.

Earlier this month, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services locked in plans to reduce reimbursements for outpatient drugs dispensed by participants in the 340B Drug Pricing Program during the 2027 calendar year.

Specifically, the agency said it will move from the current rate of average sales price (ASP) plus 6% down to ASP minus 28%, with a corresponding 7.5% increase in non-drug outpatient payments to maintain budget neutrality. CMS said the change will reduce Original Medicare drug payments by an estimated $4.35 billion and drug payments from beneficiaries by $1.1 billion during the policy’s first year.

Hospital groups—particularly those that chiefly represent nonprofit or safety-net hospitals—have denounced the plan’s disproportionate effect on hospitals participating in the decades-old subsidy program, as well as a gift to for-profit hospitals that do not participate but will benefit from the budget-neutral offset.

With their formal opposition to the policy’s proposal set aside by CMS, 95 executives whose organizations are members of the Association of American Medical Colleges have now penned a letter to leaders of the House and the Senate pleading for a two-year delay to the rate cut’s implementation.

“This policy will exacerbate already negative Medicare margins at a time when teaching hospitals are already grappling with rising financial challenges resulting from inflation, workforce shortages, manufacturer-driven restrictions on the 340B program and an expected rise in uncompensated care costs,” they wrote.

The executives acknowledged that the final policy’s rate of ASP minus 28% is “slightly smaller” than CMS’ initial proposal of ASP minus 33.4%, but said the change was still a threat to the country’s healthcare safety net. They noted that the cuts represent a nearly $5.5 billion reduction that would be disproportionately shouldered by AAMC-member teaching hospitals, who comprise 17% of all impacted hospitals but will see almost $3 billion of that total.

Those funds are necessary to finance critical care services at academic hospitals including trauma care, inpatient behavioral care, transplant surgery and labor and delivery, the signatories warned lawmakers. They also noted that their institutions are major referral hubs that accept “the most complex transfer cases from lower acuity settings, such as rural and community hospitals,” in a way that for-profit hospitals and others who will benefit from the final rule do not.

“While we understand that Congress has an interest in improving the 340B program, and are broadly supportive of meaningful transparency and accountability for covered entities, we also strongly believe that cutting Medicare payment to 340B hospitals is not the appropriate policy mechanism to achieve these goals,” they wrote. “Therefore, we respectfully request that you support a two-year delay in the [Outpatient Prospective Payment System] rule’s payment cuts for 340B-acquired drugs.”

Among the letter’s 95 signatories were the heads of Cedars-Sinai, Stanford Health Care, Yale New Haven Health, Tampa General Hospital, Rush University System for Health, Mass General Brigham, Nebraska Medicine, RWJBarnabas Health, NewYork-Presbyterian, UNC Health and School of Medicine, OHSU, Jefferson Health, Penn Medicine, Vanderbilt Health and Froedtert ThedaCare.

The academic health systems and hospitals’ turn to lawmakers comes after their rebukes of CMS’ 340B policy have fallen on deaf ears. The agency and broader Trump administration have often sided with critics of the subsidy program on related initiatives like a pilot rebate program, citing 340B’s rapid growth and contribution to increased healthcare spending. A Health and Human Services Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation policy brief defending the rate cut made those views crystal clear with claims that the program has incentivized provider consolidation while doing little to serve patients’ needs.

Meanwhile, Congress, spurred by public pressure to reduce healthcare costs, has proposed its own legislative fixes to 340B. Though each bill aims to preserve the program and subsidize safety-net care, some are more friendly to the hospital industry than others.

In the early years of the twentieth century whitetail populations were making a comeback and deer hunting was a growing sport. Lever action rifles ruled the woods for at least the first half of the century and beyond. Even in my early years of hunting in the late sixties, lever actions seemed to fill the gun rack in every deer camp. The most popular was the Winchester Model 94, usually in .30-30, and this rifle dominated the deer woods.

While the 94 was the gun for the masses, the gun guys, the hunters who “knew stuff” had a different pick. The Savage Model 99 rifle was often their choice. While the tube magazine on the Winchester and Marlin lever actions limited the rifles to use with blunt nose bullets, with its rotary box magazine the Savage 1899 could use pointed bullets for a ballistic advantage. It was also strong enough to handle powerful cartridges using that newfangled smokeless powder.

The lever action Model 99 featured an internal hammer and many advanced features like a cocking indicator and even a counter to show the number of cartridges in the magazine. It was simply a more sophisticated design than the other lever actions. The introduction of the .250 Savage cartridge for the Model 1899 in 1915 ushered a new world of deer cartridges. At the time most cartridges barely broke the 2,000 ft/s barrier, but this one hit 3,000 ft/s with an 87-grain bullet. The savviest hunters recognized the advantage over the big, slow, hard kicking, straight walled black powder cartridges that were still in vogue at that time.

Five years later in 1920, Savage changed the name to the Model 99 and introduced the .300 Savage, a cartridge that at the time matched the ballistics of the .30-06. With these advanced cartridges the Savage Model 99 rifle became the thinking man’s deer rifle.

Prior to WWI bolt action rifles were almost unheard of for deer hunting and the Savage Model 1899 lever action rifle led the industry in innovation and performance. Even after the war when bolt action sporting rifles began to emerge into the market, the Savage remained one of the most popular hunting rifles on earth.

So how did this rifle come to be? As with many of our great firearms in history it was because of one visionary, Arthur William Savage.

Savage was born in Kingston, Jamaica on May 13, 1857. He received an education in the United States and in England, which was probably designed to have him follow in his father’s footsteps and become a government bureaucrat.

But Savage wanted adventure, so as soon as they handed him his diploma he hightailed it for Australia to look for black opals and interesting experiences, as Teddy Roosevelt called them.

At some point he was captured by aborigines and held captive for a year. One version of the story says that his captors demanded a ransom, but his family refused to pay. The historian was unable to provide details as to why, but I suspect that Christmas dinners with his family were a bit tense after his escape.

Savage explored the wild Australian interior in a covered wagon. Later he became the owner of the largest cattle ranch down under. He married Annie Bryant while there and eventually had four sons and four daughters. After eleven years in Australia, Savage returned to Jamaica at the age of 34 to grow coffee.

Savage loved firearms and machinery. He was always tinkering on new ideas. He improved the British service rifle. Among his inventions was a torpedo that he sold to the government of Brazil. He also developed the recoilless rifle and the detachable rifle magazine. Savage soon recognized that the future was in America, not Jamaica.

He traveled to New York where he took a job managing the Utica Belt Line railroad in Utica, NY. In his spare time he developed a hammerless, lever-action rifle. He patented the design in 1893 with an unwavering belief that this gun would lead the way into a new world of firearms. With a few investors, he founded Savage Arms in 1894.

It took courage to open a gun company in a field that was dominated by Winchester, Remington, and Colt, but Savage recognized that the newly invented smokeless powder was about to change the rifle world and that the low-pressure, big-bore, rifle cartridges were going to become obsolete. Europe was already using smokeless powder in some cartridges and here in America in 1892, the Army had adopted the .30-40 Krag as our first smokeless powder cartridge. Some sporting rifles were chambered for this new cartridge and Savage could see the future was with small bore, high velocity smokeless powder cartridges.

The first Savage rifle was the Model 1892, although it never got into production. Savage built it to compete for a military contract, which ultimately went to the .30-40 Krag bolt action. Savage continued to work on the design and it evolved into the Model 1895. That rifle won a contract to supply rifles for the New York National Guard. But politics prevailed, as it always has and probably always will in NY, so the contract was cancelled. The poor soldiers were sent off to fight in the Spanish-American war with obsolete single-shot trapdoor Springfield rifles.

With the Model 1895 rifle Savage also introduced a new cartridge, the .303 Savage, a bottle-necked, .30-caliber (.311 diameter bullet) cartridge designed for smokeless powder. Savage created the cartridge with a military contract in mind, but that never developed so he turned to the sporting rifle market.

The Model 1895 rifle, which was reported to have been built by Marlin Firearms Company for Savage, was revolutionary as it was designed to use modern, bottle-necked, smokeless powder cartridges and could handle pointed bullets. The new, smaller .303 Savage cartridge had to compete with the physically larger and more popular cartridges of the day, like the .45-70 Government. At the time the public still equated killing power with physical size and convincing them that technology would allow this diminutive cartridge to perform better than those claiming brute size as their biggest asset would define the struggle for success.

Proving he understood marketing as well as design, Savage started running testimonials in his catalog about the effectiveness of the new cartridge. In those days the catalog not only displayed the products, but it was a source of information for hunters and gun owners in the era before internet, television, or radio. Savage recognized that and ran first person accounts of one-shot kills with the .303 Savage on moose, deer, caribou, sheep and even grizzly bears. But the clincher was from an Alaska resident who killed a whale with a single shot from the .303 Savage.

The Model 1895 evolved into the Model 1899 rifle. That went on to become of the most successful hunting rifles in history. On March 22, 1960 Savage presented the NRA with the one millionth rifle made. No one is quite sure about the total sales number, but it would continue to be made for 38 more years.

A little known fact is that Savage did much of the development work on his early rifles using the machinery at the Remington factory. It’s said that he offered his lever action design to Remington.

Remington and Winchester were owned by the same company at the time and it’s thought that Remington was being locked out of the lever action market by upper management. Winchester didn’t want the competition. It’s what led to Remington developing their semi-auto and pump action line of hunting rifles.

But if you think about it, if those in charge had a little more foresight and had allowed Remington to dip a toe into the lever action market the Model 99 would have had the Remington name on it and Savage Arms might never have existed.

Savage was a restless soul and in 1904 he sold his interest in the company to a group of businessmen and headed west to California. There he started the Savage Tire Company, which grew to employ as many as 2,000 people at one time. It’s said that he invented the radial tire. Later he would have citrus groves, drill for oil and mine for gold. He died in 1938 at the age of 81. His was a life well lived and his lasting legacy would be the Model 99 rifle.

Savage Arms of course continued on without Arthur Savage and by the 1920s it became the largest firearms company in the free world.

In what was probably the start of a Savage tradition, the Model 1899 and 99 rifles were made in a dizzying number of models and configurations. At one time there were more than 700 SKUs of the Savage Model 110 rifle. I am not sure the Model 99 matched that, but it sure tried. Entire books have been written about the various models of this rifle.

There were octagon and half octagon barrels. Carbines and rifles with barrel lengths from 20 inches to 26 inches. The military musket had a 28-inch barrel. The 1895 had a 30-inch tube. There was even a model with a .410 shotgun barrel. Some had a saddle ring and others had deluxe wood. Still others had deluxe wood and fancy engraving. Some rifles had cut checkering and many had no checkering at all. In later years as they tried to cut production costs and ultimately ruined the rifle, some of those stocks had pressed in rifling. Some 99s had straight levers and grips, but most had curved levers with a pistol grip. The forends ranged from Schnabel to a plane Jane straight forend. Some rifles were take-down and others were not. Some came with tang mounted peep sights and others with a very innovative scope mount. It wasn’t until 1950 that Savage started drilling and tapping the rifles at the factory for scope mounting. Some carbine models had a barrel band, but most models did not. The rotary magazine was at some point modified to remove the counting window and the traditional brass was replaced with steel. In 1965 they introduced the “C” model which featured a detachable “clip” magazine. (Yes, they called it a clip. No matter what they tell you on the internet, “clip” was a commonly used term to describe removable magazines in sporting rifles for decades.)

The Savage Model 99 features a hammerless design with a much faster lock time than a traditional lever action. The lever is connected to the breech bolt by a long, wide, curving piece of metal. This gives the rifle a distinctive profile when the action is open. The breech block locks into the action as the lever is closed creating a strong action design that is suitable to modern high-pressure cartridges. While it’s not often noted, the Model 99 action is a controlled round feed design. That means that the extractor captures the cartridge as it exits the magazine and holds it against the bolt face during its travel into the rifle’s chamber. There is a cocking indicator in the form of a pin that sticks out of the top of the action when the gun is cocked and recedes inside when it is not.

The stock is two pieces with separate forend and buttstock. The bottom of the metal action is rounded which makes this rifle a delight to carry in your hand.

The safety is a small catch on the right side of the lever. Pushed forward it locks the lever shut and prevents the rifle from firing. Pulled back it is in the fire position. This is opposite of the more common safeties seen on modern rifles where back is safe and forward is fire. Shooters were smart enough to know the difference back then. Sometime in the mid-sixties a top-tang shotgun style safety was added on some models, but the traditional style lever locking safety remained more popular.

The .303 Savage was the first cartridge introduced in 1895. It was more or less a ballistic twin to the .30-30 introduced the same year. These were the first sporting cartridges designed for smokeless powder and it’s debatable which one was first. The .303 Savage is a handloading-only deal now, but I have always wanted one just because. My friend Lane Pearce just bought one and called to gloat. I hate him.

The .22 Savage High Power was next. It was designed by Charles Newton and introduced in the Model 99 in 1912. It was based on the .25-35 Winchester cartridge necked down to take a .228-diameter bullet. With a 70 grain bullet and a muzzle velocity of 2,800 ft/s it was marketed for big game. In fact, Savage ran testimonials of foolish feats like a 400-pound Tiger taken by Reverend H. R. Caldwell with .22 Savage Hi-Power. Or W.M.D. Bell, the demigod of inadequate rifle cartridges, shooting a Cape buffalo with one. I am sure that probably sold some guns and no doubt got some people killed. But, the truth won out and the cartridge lost favor. Not saying I don’t want one; I do, but not for hunting big game.

Next came the Newton designed .250-3000, which is simply called the .250 Savage today. Newton designed it with a 100 grain bullet, but the marketing folks at Savage wanted that “3000” in the name and reduced the bullet weight to 87 grains to achieve that goal. Terminal performance was erratic until the ammo companies finally listened to Newton and made ammo with a 100 grain bullet. Sadly, he never saw it come to fruition as he died just before the ammo was introduced.

The .300 Savage is the flagship. It was 1920 and the war was over. The doughboys coming home had seen the .30-06 in action and they were impressed. Savage knew that the cartridge would not work in the Model 99, so they created one that would. They shortened the .30-06 case, made a short neck and a sharp shoulder so they could pack all the powder possible into the case. The result was a 150-grain bullet at 2,700 ft/s. That matched the sporting load for the .30-06 at the time. (The velocity was later increased in the .30-06 to 2,900 ft/s.) The .300 Savage soon became one of the most popular hunting cartridges and many gun makers other than Savage chambered it in their rifles. In addition to my Savage Model 99 I have a Remington Model 760 pump action and a Remington Model 722 bolt action rifle in .300 Savage, two of the more popular rifles in that cartridge.

When the military was looking for a new short action cartridge they looked at the .300 Savage. In the end they modified the case to create the 7.62 NATO which was introduced to the sporting world as the .308 Winchester. The pity is that the .308 killed off the .300 Savage. It wasn’t long before the .308 was outselling the .300 even in the Model 99. I don’t think any major rifle maker offers the .300 Savage today. Not even Savage.

The Model 99 has been chambered for a lot of cartridges over its life span. See the list below. In addition to the .303 and .22 HP my bucket list includes a .358 Winchester and the very rare .284 Winchester. The trouble is, the folks I have encountered selling them are asking prices that make a gun writer question his career choices.

The cartridges chambered in the Savage Model 1899 and Savage Model 99 over the years.

The early guns were not clearly marked with model information and trying to figure out which model a gun is can drive a Baptist Deacon to a hard drink. Mine is a 99-EG as far as I can figure out. It was made in 1936 and has smooth, non-checkered wood, which adds a premium to the value. But, it hardly matters now. At some point the gun was drilled and tapped for a scope, which detracts from the collector’s value but adds to the “usability” of the rifle. There is a Weaver 1.5-4.5 Wide Angle scope on it with Weaver mounts. This TV screen scope was introduced in 1973 to compete with the Redfield Widefield scopes and it makes me think it was added to the rifle in the mid-seventies. Sling swivels have also been added and at some point a skilled craftsman repaired a cracked stock. Which means this is no collector’s rifle. That suits me fine; I bought it to hunt with, just as Arthur Savage intended.

My rifle has what I think is the 1926 style front sight ramp that is integral with the barrel. I have to wonder what that would cost to reproduce today. The rifle sold in 1939 for $47.00. So I am guessing that when mine was made three years earlier it was maybe $45.00? That barely buys a box of ammo today.

It was a sad day in 1998 when I got a call from the marketing director of Savage to inform me that the Model 99 was being discontinued. I had a gun on loan for an article which with that call changed its focus. The gun was shabbily made and unlike the elegant 99s of the past, it felt like a dead fence post in my hands. The truth is, if this is all they could produce, it was time to stop. I should have bought it at the very good price he offered, but I didn’t. I returned it to Savage and mourned the end of a dynasty.

Savage experienced some hard times shortly after the Model 99 was dropped. Ron Colburn took over the company and made a drastic move to drop every single product except the Model 110. Everybody said he was crazy, but he saved the company. Savage today is healthy, vibrant, and is building good guns.

Once Ron’s brilliance became apparent and Savage began growing and prospering we all asked when he would bring back the Model 99.

“Never,” he said with his Irish brogue. “It’s just too expensive to make because it requires a lot of hand fitting of parts. Besides, the market isn’t there anymore. I wish it wasn’t true, I dearly love the rifle, but that’s reality.”

Ron never wavered from that until the day he retired.

The same question came up with the current owners during a press conference in early 2020 and the Savage folks provided the same answer almost by rote.

Most recently I contacted Jessica Treglia, Sr. Brand Manager at Savage Arms. Here is what she said:

“We have no current plans to bring back the M99. The cost to manufacture this gun would push the price above $1500 and would not be well received by our customers and thus the volume would not cover the cost to tool up to make the rifle. That said, a lever action is not in our current plan but is on the long term one. It will not, however, be a M99.”

I am of the belief that this “conventional” wisdom is wrong. Much has changed since the Model 99 stopped production. The “high cost due to the labor of hand fitting the parts” is no longer an excuse.

With modern computer controlled machinery and new manufacturing techniques Savage can reduce greatly the labor cost. I am of the belief that the rifle could be made and sold at a price the market will accept.

The question is simply that, the market. Is it there to support a return of the rifle? Sadly, I think the answer is no. The world has moved on. There is a bit of a nostalgic return of interest in lever-action rifles and surprisingly some movement to using them as self-defense rifles in states with harsh gun laws. But, that is aimed at the more traditional lever actions. Sure, a lot of the diehard gun guys would buy a new Model 99 if it was properly made. But, sadly, there just are not enough of us to justify the cost of bringing it back. However, with Savage’s new ownership … who knows what the future might hold.

Google DeepMind’s 544-Prompt HFE Test Exposes LLM Consistency Gaps

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google DeepMind on November 24, 2026 launched Humanity’s First Exam on Kaggle, a benchmark of 544 elementary text and image prompts designed to test whether large language models answer routine questions consistently, not just correctly once.

The accompanying technical report evaluated 18 frontier and fast-tier models. Its primary measure, family pass^4, estimates the probability that all 16 sampled attempts across four related prompts are correct; family pass@4 instead asks whether at least one of those attempts succeeds.

The report ranks GPT-6.2 Astra first at 92.4% family pass^4, followed by GPT-6.2 Sol at 86.9% and Gemini 4 Neon at 85.8%, although all three reached 100% family pass@4. The report calls the difference the Family Reliability Gap: 7.6, 13.1 and 14.2 percentage points, respectively. That difference matters for automated work in which an occasional correct answer is insufficient.

The 544 prompts form 136 families, each containing a seed and three controlled variants that preserve the underlying task while changing entities, clause order or visual attributes. The 20 subcategories include object counting, color identification, spatial reasoning, arithmetic, constraint satisfaction and false-premise verification. Of the total, 316 prompts combine text and images, while 228 are text-only.

One public example asks for the colors, in order, of the third letter of each word in a neon sign. The reference answer is green, yellow, white, blue, blue. Such tasks are deliberately easy to explain, while their variants test whether a correct answer depends on a particular phrasing or image.

Researchers evaluated the models zero-shot in a single turn without external tools, drawing six independent answers per prompt and grading the visible final answer with a deterministic Gemini 3.1 Pro judge. The analysis found that among the top 12 models, 61% to 85% of families with any failed rollout had just one problematic prompt while the other three were solved on all six runs.

The report’s failure analysis attributes 73% of wrong-answer rollouts to visual perception, including 31% involving position, direction or paths in images and 23% involving object counting. Among the top nine models, family pass^4 ranged from 83% to 97% on text-only families but fell to 64% to 89% on multimodal families.

The report’s reasoning-effort tests show that moving Gemini 4 Carbon from low to high effort raised family pass^4 by 17.4 percentage points overall, with a 42.4-point gain on Math and Algorithmic tasks versus 3.1 points on Vision tasks. The pattern indicates that additional test-time computation can catch symbolic and procedural slips while leaving visual-perception and variant-robustness failures largely unresolved.

The report’s screening process began with 315 complete five-prompt families. The researchers retained only families whose seed was solved by at least three of four independent human raters, audited ambiguity and variant fidelity, and standardized each surviving family to four prompts. Single-rater human accuracy on the 136 retained seeds was 90.8%. Raters could not use LLMs, web search, calculators, code or writing assistants.

Only 112 prompts in 28 families are public under CC-BY-4.0; another 432 prompts in 108 families remain private to reduce contamination and overfitting. The Kaggle benchmark uses an isolated evaluation environment without public internet access and a 16-model leaderboard. It separately documents configuration and sampling differences from the paper’s 18-model evaluation, so their scores should not be treated as equivalent runs.

The authors caution that tasks were adversarially selected to expose model blind spots rather than sampled uniformly from everyday queries, and list synthetic imagery, reliance on one Gemini autorater family and seed-only human testing as further limitations. HFE therefore functions as a stress test of elementary reliability; its scores do not estimate how often models will fail across ordinary use.

Google DeepMind is also inviting submissions through a three-week Kaggle competition ending December 15, 2026, asking for everyday questions that current models still answer incorrectly so HFE can be refreshed. The central deployment question is whether a system can reproduce an elementary success on every call, particularly when small wording changes or basic image interpretation can break it.

NASA’s Northrop Grumman CRS-25 Mission Overview

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

NASA’s Northrop Grumman Commercial Resupply Services 25 mission, or Northrop Grumman CRS-25, will deliver about 11,000 pounds of science and supplies to the International Space Station. The mission will be the third flight of the company’s Cygnus XL, the larger, more cargo-capable version of the company’s solar-powered spacecraft.

The Cygnus XL will launch aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket from the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. Following arrival to the station, astronauts aboard the space station will use the Canadarm2 to grapple the spacecraft before ground controllers robotically install it to the Unity module’s Earth-facing port for cargo unloading.

In addition to food, supplies, and equipment for the crew, Cygnus will deliver several new experiments aimed at improving drug delivery for cancer and osteoarthritis treatment, predicting cancer spread, advancing treatments for muscle loss, improving radiation sensing, and supporting food production for future long-duration missions. This research includes:

Cardinal Muscle 2.0 uses the microgravity environment to explore potential treatments for muscle loss. Muscle atrophy and strength loss experienced by astronauts after spaceflight share many features with age-related muscle decline on Earth and occur more rapidly in microgravity without exercise. Researchers will use a muscle tissue model to test different Food and Drug Administration-approved drugs used to treat other conditions can be repurposed to prevent or reduce muscle loss.

The DNA Nano B investigation uses microgravity to manufacture tiny, DNA-inspired particles designed to deliver medicine into dense tissues, such as cartilage and solid tumors. Microgravity may enable more uniform particle assembly, helping researchers refine production methods. The results could advance targeted treatments for cancer and osteoarthritis on Earth.

The Active Radiation Environment Sensor (ARES) will test a small radiation monitoring device to support future Artemis program and lunar missions. The system is designed to integrate with spacecraft systems and provide real-time radiation monitoring inside crew living areas. The results could inform radiation monitoring strategies and crew safety measures for future exploration missions.

Encapsulate-1 uses miniature replicas of patients’ tumors to study how cancer cells move, invade surrounding tissues, and potentially spread. Researchers aim to develop a space-based diagnostic tool that could predict cancer progression months or even years in advance, improving early detection and treatment decisions for patients on Earth.

Ohalo III is a new plant growth facility designed to help food crop production on future long-duration missions. The mostly automated system uses innovative water recovery and air exchange technologies to grow a variety of crops. This research could help lead to better food production methods to keep crew healthy and fed on future exploration missions, including those to Mars.

JOining demonstrations IN-Space (JOINS) will mature technologies that allow robotic systems to join materials directly in space. In-space joining could enable large structures to be assembled and integrated after launch rather than being constrained by the size and volume of a launch vehicle. These capabilities may be needed to build and repair large infrastructure, such as structures that will to support future exploration under Moon Base.

Testing technologies in space helps validate their performance in unique environmental conditions, matures their readiness for operational use, reduces technical risk, and builds confidence for future mission applications.

The Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) has approved the FICO 10T credit score model for use by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. It also released an implementation road map that would make FICO 10T broadly eligible and retire Classic FICO by September 2028.

The staged rollout, announced Tuesday, mirrors the VantageScore 4.0 timeline and is meant to give lenders, mortgage insurers and loan origination system providers time to analyze historic FICO 10T data, rework pricing and risk models, and update technology before a full transition, according to the agency.“The timeline below prepares lenders, mortgage insurers and loan origination system providers for the final step in credit score modernization: lender choice between FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0,” the agency said. At the Federal Housing Administration (FHA), VantageScore 4.0 and FICO 10T are expected to go live alongside the existing Classic FICO in January.

The FHFA initially validated both FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0 for use at the government-sponsored (GSEs) enterprises in October 2022. In July 2026, Fannie and Freddie released historical performance data for FICO 10T along with additional VantageScore 4.0 data, a key prerequisite for lenders and investors to back-test new score models and recalibrate credit policy.A FICO 10T pilot is expected to begin around December 2027. In this “launch and refine” phase, lenders will deliver a subset of loans underwritten with FICO 10T while FHFA, the GSEs and industry stakeholders monitor data quality, pricing and delivery performance.

Based on pilot results, FHFA expects upfront pricing grids and private mortgage insurer eligibility requirements, or PMIERs, to be updated to reflect the new score model. That step will be critical for secondary market execution and risk transfer structures that currently rely on Classic FICO.

A limited FICO 10T rollout is targeted for April 2028, followed by a scale-up and transition phase that leads to broad eligibility around September 2028. During this final stage, FHFA plans to confirm industry readiness, fully implement Uniform Loan Delivery Dataset (ULDD) Phase 5, expand FICO 10T use across GSE channels and retire Classic FICO.

FHFA emphasized that dates after November 2026 are targets and are subject to change.

It covers both Classic FICO, which has been the mortgage standard for nearly three decades, and FICO Score 10T, the company’s trended-data model.

The Trump administration has launched a new online portal for payers, providers and other stakeholders to manage and monitor disputes under the federal Independent Dispute Resolution process.

The IDR Gateway went online Nov. 13 and was officially unveiled Nov. 20. It replaces the web forms parties previously used to manage disputes, most of which will no longer be available outside of the centralized platform after Jan. 15, 2027, per a government fact sheet.

Payers will eventually be required to register on IDR Gateway so that it is easier for providers to identify the correct party, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services said earlier this year when finalizing an overhaul of No Surprises Act protocols. That registration feature will be added “in the future on a rolling basis,” CMS said last week, with others like in-portal negotiation similarly rolling out over time “to improve accountability and reduce unnecessary filings,” the agency said last spring.

For now, the IDR Gateway allows parties and their representatives to initiate a dispute, respond to proposed selections of IDR entities and submit a payment offer. Its other tools allow users to view, search, set up notifications and broadly analyze open disputes for their organization; an IDR Gateway Dashboard, CMS said, offers a color-coded donut chart breaking down an organization’s different types of open disputes by phase, while another tool can generate detailed reports of dispute line items.

The administration also touted security measures inherent to the new portal. These include a new sign-up process that requires users to verify their identity with an approved credential service provider, and other protocols like a terms and conditions agreement that notes unauthorized users could be subject to criminal prosecution.

The baseball-style IDR process enacted under the No Surprises Act, intended to handle out-of-network billing disputes away from the patient’s purview, has become a point of contention amid dispute volumes and payouts well beyond legislators’ expectations.

Arbitrators’ decisions have largely favored providers over payers (85% of decisions went toward providers last year), which along side court decisions that tweaked CMS’ processes further toward provider preferences, has triggered outright condemnation from payers who have turned to lawsuits, restrictive policies and lobbying in a bit to reform the process. Recent analyses have pegged the IDR process’ impact for employer plans at $8.4 billion in 2025 and total costs associated with the process at $22 billion for that same year.

Lawmakers including the No Surprises Act’s chief authors have said they hope to pass new legislation to fix or fully replace the IDR process, with December’s must-pass budget bill now looking like a likely vehicle for such a change.

Freddie Potts on the pain of leaving West Ham, why he had to move on this summer, upsetting the odds in the Champions League with Club Brugge - and why they don't fear Liverpool!

Published: 08:00 EST, 24 November 2026 | Updated: 14:15 EST, 24 November 2026

There are few transfers that get West Ham supporters talking quite like the sale of an academy graduate.

Who can forget the uproar after Grady Diangana joined West Brom in 2020, when club captain Mark Noble released a public statement voicing his anger and disappointment at the move.

Noble’s thoughts were echoed by team-mates at the time, while supporters were similarly up in arms at the sale of the winger.

So, when Freddie Potts joined Club Brugge for £10million on the eve of the season to mark the controversial departure of another product of the proclaimed Academy of Football, you can imagine the reaction.

Potts represented more than a graduate. A boyhood West Ham fan whose dad Steve is a club legend and still serves as a first-team coach, he joined the academy aged six. His brother Dan also came through the ranks.

The midfielder had just enjoyed a breakthrough campaign despite West Ham’s relegation, and many felt he was the perfect player to build their Championship promotion push around.

Freddie Potts is shining in Belgium with Club Brugge after joining from West Ham in the summer

Potts joined the Hammers academy aged six and enjoyed a breakthrough year last season

Hammers favourites Kevin Nolan and Tony Cottee weighed in with their disapproval at his sale, while celebrity fan Ray Winstone expressed his shock and anger live on talkSPORT.

‘Of course you see it (the division about the move) on social media,’ Potts tells Daily Mail Sport. ‘Every player does. But even though last season was tough I always felt I had a connection with the West Ham fans.

‘No matter if I played well or bad I always felt I understood what it meant to play for West Ham. It's a great club with great fans and people. In the end, I had to do what was best for me. I wanted to play this season, and I felt like this was the best place to do that.’

Potts, 22 at the time of his exit, admits it was extremely tough and emotional for him and his family to leave a club he had been at for 16 years.

But, during pre-season he became aware he wasn’t likely to play as much as he wanted to under Nuno Espirito Santo.

‘In the end it’s the manager’s decision,’ Potts says. ‘I wasn’t getting the minutes I wanted. The feeling was that I wasn’t guaranteed to be in the plans as much as Club Brugge were promising me, so I thought it was best for me to move.

‘It was very hard. West Ham have been my club my whole life and I have a lot of love for West Ham. But I had to take into account what was best for my career and I’m loving it at Brugge. It’s a really exciting place full of great people and a great manager.’

Potts had multiple options in the summer, including Premier League interest, yet the chance to play in the Champions League and compete for titles with Brugge attracted him.

Their record of developing young players and a talented squad that includes the likes of Carlos Forbs and Nicolo Tresoldi was equally compelling, and the midfielder hasn't looked back.

Potts started Brugge’s first 10 league games, missing their final one before the international break due to suspension. He returned at the weekend as they thrashed Mechelen 5-1. Ivan Leko’s side are one point adrift of Union Saint-Gilloise at the top of the Belgian Pro League, and they have also impressed in the Champions League.

In Europe, Brugge have beaten Inter Milan, PSV and Lens on their way to nine points from 12, only narrowly losing 3-2 to Aston Villa. Potts has started each match, including their memorable 4-3 win against Inter at the San Siro, and they host Liverpool on Wednesday.

‘It's a really good group of lads,’ he says. ‘Everyone speaks English as well which helps me. It's a good team and we're happy playing with each other.’

He is clearly enjoying life and is just the fifth English player in history to play for the club. Potts did take time to settle though, and admits he initially suffered from homesickness.

‘I found it difficult because East London and Essex have been my home for my whole life,’ he says. ‘West Ham was my life. It was tough in terms of moving into a new language and everything but you get used to it and I’m really enjoying it here. It’ll be strange going back home to England.'

Potts has been a regular and has had a key role in Brugge's impressive early exploits in Europe

Potts’ family have popped over to see him and he has enjoyed exploring his new surroundings. He is yet to learn the local language and scolds himself for being lazy for relying on English, although he has picked up little bits of Flemish.

He has immersed himself in the culture too, and reveals he has tried the world-famous Belgian fries and a few local beers, albeit only when he has enough time to recover between games.
