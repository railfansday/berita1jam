What a city’s sewage can reveal about the health of the people upstream

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Eight Ohio cities' sewage carried gut bacteria whose mix lined up with local obesity, blood pressure, and stroke rates, a pilot study finds.

During the pandemic, health departments learned to read a city’s sewage for the virus behind COVID-19.

The same water carries something else from every household that flushes into it: the bacteria living in people’s guts.

In eight Ohio cities, the mix of those gut bacteria in the sewage lined up with how the cities scored on chronic disease.

Cities with more of one group of bacteria that produce hydrogen sulfide had higher rates of obesity, high blood pressure, stroke, and several other conditions.

The study rests on 27 samples collected over seven weeks in May and June 2022, and the authors wrote that the links do not show cause.

What they show is that a sample from a treatment plant can carry a readable signal about the health of the people upstream.

Emma Lancaster, then a doctoral student in the College of Public Health at Ohio State University, led the study with Jiyoung Lee, professor and chair of environmental health sciences.

Lancaster is now an environmental health consultant in Boston.

“During the pandemic, the world learned how to use wastewater to track infectious disease. We asked, what else can it tell us?” Lancaster said.

Sewage arriving at a treatment plant is a blend of everything a community has flushed.

One earlier study the authors cite found up to 97% of the microbes in human stool in untreated sewage.

So the water is a pooled sample of a whole population’s gut bacteria, collected without asking anyone to take a test.

Earlier sewage studies elsewhere had tied a community’s bacterial profile to its rates of obesity, high blood pressure, and smoking.

The Ohio team set out to build full gut bacteria profiles for a set of cities and test them against a wide range of health and social measures.

The team collected a liter of untreated wastewater from each of nine treatment plants, on three days in May and June 2022. Two of the plants serve Columbus, the state capital.

The rest serve smaller cities in central and southeastern Ohio: Zanesville, Marysville, Newark, Lancaster, Athens, Circleville, and Marietta.

Together the plants handle sewage from more than 1.3 million people.

The smallest serves about 15,000 and the largest nearly 548,000. So the cities range from small towns to a major metro area.

Four of the cities count as rural under the federal definition the team used, with fewer than 50,000 residents. The other four are urban.

Each sample was processed the same day. The team sequenced a marker gene to identify the bacteria present. Then they kept only those known to live in the human gut, about 37% of what they read.

For the health side, they pulled public estimates for the census tracts inside each plant’s service area, 329 tracts in all.

The CDC’s PLACES database supplied rates of 23 health conditions and behaviors.

The agency’s Social Vulnerability Index supplied the social and economic measures, such as poverty and unemployment.

Each city’s sewage had its own bacterial profile, and the profiles differed enough that the cities could be told apart.

Several of those differences lined up with health. Cities where smoking was more common had more of one large group of bacteria, the Firmicutes.

Cities with less physical activity had more of two genera, Desulfovibrio and Tolumonas.

Desulfovibrio, a genus of bacteria that produce hydrogen sulfide, appeared again and again. Its abundance was associated with eight health measures.

They were high blood pressure, obesity, stroke, mobility disability, chronic kidney disease, coronary heart disease, chronic lung disease, and cancers other than skin cancer.

Studies of patients have found more Desulfovibrio in people with colorectal, stomach, and breast cancers than in healthy people, the authors wrote.

Researchers suspect its hydrogen sulfide may damage cells.

Asthma ran the other way. Cities with more asthma had a less even mix of gut bacteria and less of a genus called Akkermansia, which is widely treated as a marker of a healthy gut.

Social conditions showed up too. Cities with more unemployment had less diverse gut bacteria overall and more Firmicutes.

The four rural cities carried more Desulfovibrio, more Akkermansia, and more of a major group called Bacteroidota than the urban ones.

They also generally had higher rates of the chronic conditions in the dataset.

“In many cases, poor health outcomes are worse in rural areas and to see these distinct indicators of poorer gut microbiome health in these rural areas offers us clues to some of what is driving that,” Lancaster said.

Marysville, an urban city, sat at the healthy end of nearly every measure, with the lowest rates of smoking, depression, unemployment, and poverty among the eight.

“Wastewater offers a unique way to understand the health of whole communities, not just the people who show up in clinics,” Lee said.

Eight cities give eight data points. The authors wrote that their results should be read as exploratory, not as reliable estimates for any one city.

Columbus alone accounts for most of the 1.3 million people, which could tilt comparisons.

The health figures are also modeled estimates for census tracts, not counts of diagnosed patients. And the samples cover seven weeks of one year.

A deeper problem is in the pipes. Desulfovibrio and Acetobacterium live in sewers as well as in guts, and their numbers in wastewater can rise or fall with how long sewage stays in a pipe.

The authors wrote that future studies should record sewer conditions to separate the human signal from the plumbing.

A larger study could test whether the same patterns hold across more cities and over longer periods while accounting for diet, access to health care, and sewer conditions.

Wastewater already tracks viruses such as those behind COVID, flu, and mpox, Lee said.

Tracking a community’s gut bacteria the same way, she said, could eventually show whether a nutrition program is improving its gut health.

The full study was published in the journal Environmental Research.

Add This Crunchy Ingredient To Ground Beef For Lighter Steakhouse Burgers (Hint: It's Not Breadcrumbs)

The actual difference between a hamburger and a steakburger comes down to the quality of meat used in the grind, but the difference between a homemade steakburger and one from a steakhouse is all about a lighter texture. Melissa Clark, author of "The Big Book of Dinner: Recipes and Rules (and How to Break Them)," tells us that achieving that characteristic is quite easy if you use ground cracker crumbs.

"Any kind of cracker or breadcrumbs will make a burger lighter," Clark says. "Ritz and saltines work really well. They are very airy, which helps lighten the ground meat." Beef is rather dense, even if you avoid overworking it into a firm, puck-like consistency that's more similar to sausage than a tender patty. But a quarter cup of ground crackers per pound of meat can make a world of difference.

When meat cooks, it contracts and releases juice and fat. Crackers, however, absorb them rather than allowing them to seep out of the patty, preventing it from shriveling up too much. Since steakhouse burgers typically use thick patties, this also keeps them nice and moist. Whereas a thin sliver of well-crusted beef may be just what you want in a smashburger, retaining moisture is a vital component to keeping your steakhouse burgers not only light, but also at their peak flavor.

Using plain, unsalted crackers can still keep your steakhouse burgers light, but it would be a missed opportunity for better flavor. While you also don't want to use something starch-free, like a nut- or seed-based variety, Melissa Clark is a big believer in picking a tasty brand and smashing them up properly.

"Using crackers can add great flavor – Ritz, with their butteriness, are especially lovely," Clark explains. Flavorful but unobtrusive, Ritz crackers are the perfect addition to any savory dish because just about everything can benefit from their toasty richness. But if you want to get a bit experimental, you can also just as easily use Cheez-Its or anything seasoned. This is an easy way to avoid coming up with a specialty spice blend since you'll be adding enough to thoroughly flavor your meat.

Clark is also a proponent of using whatever method you can to grind the crackers into a fine powder. "Grind the crackers in a food processor, blender or tuck them in a plastic bag and crush them under a rolling pin," she added. Large chunks can make some bites unexpectedly soggy and, if using a seasoned cracker, a little too flavorful. Plus, if some parts of your blend don't have a starch, they may end up expressing their juices too much, leaving some bites juicy and light and others dense and dry.

Sierra Club Submits Comments Urging SEC to Preserve Shareholder Rights & Climate Accountability

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

WASHINGTON, D.C. — On Thursday, Sierra Club submitted two comment letters opposing the Securities and Exchange Commission’s (SEC) proposal to rescind Rule 14a-8, which allows shareholders to put proposals addressing climate risks and other corporate issues before fellow investors for a vote, and amend Rule 14a-4(c), which governs how companies may exercise discretionary voting authority over shareholder proposals. The Sierra Club submitted both a technical comment and a grassroots comment, reflecting concerns from over 1,100 of its members and supporters.

Together the submissions urge the SEC to withdraw its proposed changes and preserve investors’ ability to raise concerns directly with corporate management and press companies to address material risks, including climate-related risks, that can affect their long-term financial interests.

Jessye Waxman, Campaign Advisor on Sierra Club’s Sustainable Finance Campaign, released the following statement:

“Climate change and other systemic risks have real consequences for companies, investors, and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors an important way to bring those risks to management’s attention, push for meaningful action, and hold companies accountable when they fail to act. The SEC’s proposals would weaken those tools, making it harder for investors to protect their long-term financial interests and leaving corporate management with less accountability for the risks its decisions create. The SEC’s proposals would shield corporate management from meaningful shareholder scrutiny, while undermining the very investor protections the Commission is charged with upholding.”

Rule 14a-8 establishes when public companies must include qualifying shareholder proposals in the materials they send investors ahead of shareholder meetings. The SEC’s proposed repeal would leave decisions about shareholder proposals to state law and individual companies’ governing documents, eliminating a federal framework that investors have used for more than 80 years.

Sierra Club’s technical comments explain that material risks, like climate change, can both have significant financial consequences for companies and create systemic financial risks that extend across investment portfolios and the broader economy. Shareholder proposals give investors a way to bring these concerns to company leadership and fellow shareholders, including by surfacing risks that management may overlook or have limited incentives to address. The letter emphasizes that, for decades, this process has served the broader market by helping investors collectively identify and address financially material risks.

The letter warns that replacing a consistent federal framework with varying state laws and corporate bylaws could make shareholder engagement less predictable and push disputes over proposals into costly litigation. It points to the increase in shareholder lawsuits challenging proposal exclusions during the 2026 proxy season as an indication that disputes do not disappear when the SEC steps back from administering the process; instead, they can shift to more costly and uncertain avenues.

The submission also opposes proposed amendments to Rule 14a-4(c), which would expand company management’s discretionary authority to vote shareholders’ proxies on proposals excluded from company proxy materials, unless shareholders affirmatively opt out. Sierra Club’s letter warns that these changes could allow management to vote on proposals that investors have not had an adequate opportunity to evaluate.

Conservation Groups Warn Senate Bill Could Put Bears Ears, Boundary Waters and More at Risk

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A permitting reform bill currently before the US Senate would “effectively lock in project approvals that irrevocably open up recreation landscapes to development, including places like Bears Ears or the Boundary Waters,” says outdoor recreation advocacy group Outdoor Alliance.

The Senate is currently voting on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA), which is co-sponsored by Senators Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV), Mike Lee (R-UT), Martin Heinrich (D-NM), and Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI). The bill’s aim is to speed up energy and infrastructure projects by streamlining the permitting process.

Proponents argue the bill would lower energy costs, provide more regulatory certainty for developers, and facilitate future clean energy projects, but critics say it would weaken environmental protections and curtail public input.

Bears Ears National Monument. Photo via Bureau of Land Management

The bill would narrow or change several existing environmental review and permitting requirements under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), Clean Water Act, and Endangered Species Act. Crucially, outdoor recreation groups like Outdoor Alliance also warn that provisions designed to create “project certainty” for developers would make it difficult or impossible for future administrations to reconsider or modify permits.

The Center for Western Priorities, in partnership with Earthworks, has identified dozens of public-land landscapes that could be affected by the legislation, including Bears Ears National Monument, the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness, and Tongass National Forest. The map highlights areas where existing or proposed mining, drilling, logging, and infrastructure projects could benefit from the bill’s provisions protecting federal development approvals from future reversal.

More than 110 mining claims have been filed in the Bears Ears area since the monument’s boundaries were shrunk, for instance. “Any mining plans approved by this administration would be nearly impossible to revoke, even if the monuments are restored,” the Center for Western Priorities writes.

If BAAJA passes in the Senate, it will go back to the House of Representatives. Conservation groups are urging the public to express their support for public-lands integrity to lawmakers. Outdoor Alliance created an Action Alert to make it easy to contact members of Congress.

'I tell my son it's just fireworks': People inside besieged Yemeni city speak to BBC

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Houthi attacks on Taiz have killed more than 140 civilians since August, local government officials say

In the Yemeni city of Taiz, ringed by jagged mountains and Houthi rebel fighters, Ashwaq has only one way to try to soothe her frightened child - lie.

"When my 11-year-old son hears a loud sound, I tell him, 'Don't be afraid, it's just fireworks'," she says. "I don't want him to panic, but deep down I know he is old enough to understand what is happening around him. He has lived through war for most of his life."

Taiz has long been a prize - and a front line - in Yemen's civil war.

Houthi forces tightened their grip last month, fully encircling the government-controlled city, and the population - estimated to be between 600,000 and 1 million.

Since then, Yemen's third-largest city has been cut off from the world, and its residents have been largely unheard. We reached seven people inside the city who mapped out the anatomy of the siege.

They gave detailed accounts of the Houthis' death grip on Taiz. Only a minimal amount of food can get in. Prices for staples like rice, sugar and cooking oil have doubled or even trebled. Essential supplies, like medicine, water and fuel, are dwindling.

Burning piles of rubbish are producing toxic smoke that hangs over the city

On streets that used to hum with traffic and background chatter, a silence has fallen. Schools are closed and restaurants are shuttered. Suffering is being heaped on suffering. Piles of rubbish are trapped inside the city, alongside the people. When residents try to get rid of it by burning it, there is a toxic smoke. Even the air is now a Houthi weapon.

From positions on the hills, rebel fighters - and snipers - can wage war on the city below.

Estimates for the death toll vary widely. At the Office for Siege Affairs, a local government department, they say 148 civilians have been killed by Houthi attacks since August.

Some of those we contacted in Taiz do not want to be identified because they fear for their safety. Many in the city are reluctant to even speak on the phone because the Houthis control key mobile carriers.

"Sometimes it feels like the city has become a huge prison," says Ashwaq, a teacher and mother of three, who only wanted her first name to be used. She yearns for the old Taiz.

"It used to be a lively city," she says. "People loved life, worked hard, visited one another, and had hopes for the future, despite all the difficulties. Now, we hardly go out anymore. The [Houthi] shelling can happen any time, and sniper fire near the front lines makes even a short trip dangerous."

She keeps her son and two elder daughters indoors.

"I worry about them every single day, every hour, and honestly every minute," she says.

Taiz has been a major battleground in Yemen's civil war for more than a decade

War is not new to Taiz. The strategic city is the gateway to Aden - the temporary seat of the internationally recognised government - and to southern Yemen.

Houthi rebels - backed by Iran - reached the outskirts of Taiz in 2015, cutting off roads, and laying siege from three sides. A year earlier, they had seized Yemen's capital, Sanaa.

More than a decade on, the government, which is weak and fractured, has been unable to reclaim the capital and much of north-western Yemen. That's despite the backing of Saudi Arabia, and its air strikes.

In recent months, the conflict has flared up and expanded after a four-year lull. The Houthis made a lightning advance, taking territory from the government, and striking Saudi Arabia with missiles and drones.

A long-running civil war has become a threat to regional stability.

An emergency room doctor, whom we are calling Yaser, bears witness to that.

Among the war wounded recently brought to his hospital were four children, shot by Houthi snipers. They usually aim for the head and chest, he tells us. Two of the four children died.

The hardest thing he faces, as a father himself, is treating wounded children. "I have to rise to the responsibility, compose myself and support the families," he says. "It's a difficult situation that I cannot even put into words, when I see a father looking at his badly wounded child right in front of him."

Most of the injured are victims of shelling. Reaching the hospital can be an ordeal. Some families have to walk for hours from outlying areas.

They arrive exhausted, like the doctors who will treat them, and who are struggling with "everything", says Yaser. Even sterilising fluid for surgical instruments is in short supply.

He tells us the hospital is running on empty, and can carry on for two more weeks, "maybe".

Taiz's hospitals have lost 70% of their capacity and 120 health centres have closed, its governor says

If the emergency rooms are busy, so are the graveyards.

"Funerals heading to the cemeteries have become the defining scene in Taiz, around the clock," says Taiz's Governor, Nabil Shamsan.

It's not just bombs and bullets that kill, he tells us.

"The people of Taiz are losing their lives due to supply shortages, lack of food, and loss of income," he says. "Every single day, we bury dozens of Taiz residents."

He remains inside the city, and inside the siege. "I live among my people," he says.

On a video call, he looks weary but delivers a lengthy chronicle of a city being starved of oxygen - literally.

He tells us that hospitals have lost 70% of their capacity, and 120 health centres have closed. Diseases are spreading, neonatal deaths have increased, and malnutrition is surging. Hundreds of kidney patients are not getting dialysis because they can't get to hospitals or because fuel is short when they do.

The Houthi leader, Abdul Malik al-Houthi, has denied Taiz is besieged. Late last month, he claimed that routes remained open for food, medicine and humanitarian aid.

That is denied by the government, residents of Taiz, aid agencies and the United Nations.

What's at stake in the battle for the city is more than territory, says Shamsan.

He says the Houthis - who mostly follow a branch of Shia Islam - want to change the character of this predominantly Sunni city, long known as a seat of learning and a bastion of resistance.

"Everyone knows that Taiz is a hub of culture, a city characterised by freedom, political pluralism and all forms of civic life," he says. In sharp contrast, he says, the Houthis aim to impose their strict religious doctrine and drag the city "back to the dark ages".

Whatever the pressure on the city and its people, the governor insists the Houthis will never win.

"Until the population perishes," he replies.

Essential supplies, like medicine, water and fuel, are dwindling

They are defending their city, alongside relatives, friends, neighbours, and five brigades of pro-government troops, who can't do the job on their own.

They don't have heavy weapons or enough numbers for the expanding front line.

Training camps have sprung up for volunteers, but the lessons can be as brief as a day or two before they are sent to join the fight.

Akram (not his real name) is already a veteran. He's 31, with a child on the way.

When Taiz was fully encircled before, in 2015, he broke away from his studies and took up arms. In that round of fighting, the Houthis gained control of his district on the outskirts, and he was uprooted. He hasn't seen his old home since then.

Last month, when Taiz was completely cut off once again, he bought a rifle, paying about $1,200 (Â£910) for a Chinese version of a Kalashnikov. It used to cost less than half that sum.

He tells us this fight is different to 2015.

"This time the Houthis have new technology and new missiles," he says. "They can wage a war in the air with drones. We fear the death of our families and our civilians, and ourselves."

He has already lost four friends in the past month, who left behind wives and children.

But Akram, and many others, see defending Taiz as their duty. He says that "all the sons of Taiz" will fight the Houthis "and their extremism".

The armed group is currently designated as a foreign terrorist organisation by the US (which has gone back and forth on the issue) but not by the UK or the European Union.

8 Thriller Books That Are 10/10 Masterpieces, Ranked

Thrillers can fool readers with speed. Hide one fact, delay one reveal, add a killer, and the pages will probably turn. A masterpiece has a harder job. The tension has to survive after the secret is known, and the characters must stay interesting after the danger passes.

That is the standard here. Every book on this list can be reread without losing its grip. The shock may change, but the psychology, fear, moral pressure, and human damage become clearer. These are stories people finish quickly and continue thinking about for years. That’s also perhaps why most of these were later adapted into books as well.

The Day of the Jackal should have no suspense. Frederick Forsyth is writing about a fictional attempt to assassinate Charles de Gaulle, and readers already know de Gaulle was never killed. Forsyth turns that limitation into the smartest part of the book. The question shifts from whether the Jackal succeeds to how close an unknown professional can get before the French authorities identify him.

The preparation is incredibly detailed without becoming lifeless. The assassin builds false identities, commissions a custom weapon, changes his appearance, studies security, and removes anyone who threatens the plan. On the other side, investigator Claude Lebel has almost no useful description and a deadline that keeps shrinking. Forsyth respects procedure enough to make paperwork, border checks, phone calls, and hotel records genuinely tense. The Jackal remains frightening since he treats murder as a technical assignment rather than a personal cause. You understand every step he takes, yet you never really know him. That distance makes the hunt colder and much harder to shake.

Most plot twist-driven novels become smaller once the answer is revealed. Shutter Island becomes crueler. U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels arrives at Ashecliffe Hospital in 1954 to investigate the disappearance of patient Rachel Solando. A hurricane cuts the island off, the staff answers questions carefully, and Teddy suspects that the hospital is conducting experiments on people who cannot defend themselves.

Dennis Lehane lets Teddy’s investigation operate as both a detective story and a personal escape. His memories of Dachau, grief over his wife Dolores, anger toward the doctors, and obsession with a patient named Andrew Laeddis all influence what he sees. The clues are real, but Teddy has arranged them into a version of events he can survive. That is much more painful than a cheap reveal. The doctors are also divided over treatment, punishment, and whether a damaged mind can be reached without violence. Once the truth is known, every earlier conversation changes. The staff was never hiding only one secret. They were trying to find out whether Teddy could face his own life.

In Misery, Stephen King traps bestselling novelist Paul Sheldon in a farmhouse with Annie Wilkes, the nurse who rescued him after a car accident. Paul initially thinks he has been saved. Then Annie reads his latest book, learns that he killed off her beloved character, Misery Chastain, and decides that he owes her a new novel.

Annie is terrifying since her violence comes with rules that change whenever she needs them to. She calls herself Paul’s number-one fan, but she has no interest in Paul as a human being. She wants ownership over his imagination. The captivity also forces Paul to confront his own contempt for the books that made him successful. Writing becomes survival, punishment, and eventually a source of control. King understands the physical misery of the room too. Paul’s injuries, painkillers, thirst, typewriter, hidden pills, and attempts to move around the house make every small decision dangerous. Annie later amputates his foot, and even that horror does not end the deeper fight. Paul has to finish a story for the person destroying him and somehow keep part of it for himself.

The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo follows Harriet Vanger, who vanished from a family gathering decades ago, and her uncle Henrik still receives a pressed flower every year on his birthday. He believes her killer is taunting him. Disgraced journalist Mikael Blomkvist accepts the investigation, partly to escape the damage caused by his libel conviction. Lisbeth Salander enters from another direction, first hired to investigate Mikael before becoming the person he needs most.

Stieg Larsson takes his time connecting the Vanger mystery to corporate corruption, misogyny, financial crime, and generations of protected violence. That slow build has a purpose. The respectable surfaces of Swedish business and family life begin to look worse with every document Mikael and Lisbeth uncover. Lisbeth is also far more than a brilliant hacker attached to a male detective. Her distrust has been earned through institutional abuse, and her methods come from knowing official systems rarely protect people like her. The investigation succeeds through journalism, research, memory, and technology working together. Then Lisbeth allows herself one small hope outside the case, only to have it crushed. The crime is solved. Her loneliness is not. This movie based on this book is worth watching before or after reading the novel, too.

Four criminal masterminds. One of them was always going to be yours. Here is who comes for you, why they picked you out of everyone available, and what they want from the encounter.

Your nemesis does not want to beat you. He wants to know you completely, and he is patient enough to wait for it. The frightening part is how good the conversation is.

Your nemesis is not after money, territory or revenge. He is after a demonstration, and you are the person he has chosen to prove his point on.

Your nemesis respects you, which sounds flattering until you work out what it means. He chose you because nobody else was worth his time.

Your nemesis has no personal interest in you whatsoever, and that is precisely what makes him unsurvivable. You are not a rival. You are a stop on the way.

The first Mrs. de Winter is dead before Rebecca begins, yet the unnamed narrator cannot find a single room at Manderley that seems free of her. She marries Maxim de Winter after a rushed courtship and arrives at his estate painfully aware of her youth, lower social position, and lack of confidence. Everyone appears to know how the new Mrs. de Winter should behave except her. Daphne du Maurier turns insecurity into genuine suspense in this book.

Mrs. Danvers protects Rebecca’s memory with frightening devotion and knows exactly how to make the new bride feel like an intruder. Maxim responds to questions with anger or silence, which leaves his wife guessing what kind of marriage she has entered. The later revelations change Rebecca from an idealized dead wife into something far more complicated, but they also expose Maxim in ways romance cannot clean up. The narrator gains confidence while becoming involved in his secrecy. That moral shift is easy to miss if the book is treated only as a Gothic love story. Manderley was never simply haunted by Rebecca. It was built around people choosing which version of her helped them live with themselves.

The Talented Mr. Ripley follows Tom Ripley, who wants Dickie Greenleaf’s money, confidence, clothes, friends, and freedom. He also wants Dickie himself in a way Tom cannot separate from envy. When Dickie’s father pays him to travel to Italy and persuade his son to return home, Tom treats the assignment as entry into the life he believes he should have had.

Patricia Highsmith puts the reader dangerously close to Tom’s thinking. His lies are quick, his shame is intense, and his ability to copy other people becomes more impressive as his crimes become worse. Dickie’s rejection humiliates him, but murder is never presented as one sudden break from normality. Tom has been studying how to disappear into other identities long before violence becomes necessary. The truly disturbing choice is Highsmith’s refusal to build the story around punishment. Readers start worrying about whether Tom will be exposed, even though exposure would be justice. That uncomfortable loyalty is the whole trap. Tom does terrible things, and Highsmith makes us anxious on his behalf. The Talented Mr. Ripley would go on to inspire a movie that doesn't quite live up to the novel.

Gone Girl turned a missing-wife plot into one of the nastiest books ever written about marriage. Amy Dunne disappears on her fifth wedding anniversary, and Nick quickly becomes the husband everyone suspects. He lies badly, smiles at the wrong moment, has an affair, and cannot control how television turns his marriage into public entertainment.

Then Amy’s account tears up the story readers thought they understood. This is written by Gillian Flynn, and she uses the competing narrators to show two people who spent years performing ideal versions of themselves. Amy created the Cool Girl Nick wanted. Nick created the attentive husband Amy expected. Resentment grew once both stopped enjoying the act. Amy’s revenge is extreme, brilliant, cruel, and built around a precise understanding of how gender shapes public sympathy. Nick is a victim of her plan, but he is never rewritten as innocent. The book refuses that easy comfort. They know each other too well, hate each other too deeply, and still find a reason to stay together. Murder would have been a cleaner conclusion. Marriage becomes the real life sentence.

Hannibal Lecter gets most of the cultural attention, but Clarice Starling is the reason The Silence of the Lambs belongs at number one. She is still an FBI trainee when Jack Crawford sends her to interview Lecter, hoping the imprisoned psychiatrist can help identify the serial killer known as Buffalo Bill. Lecter immediately notices her accent, cheap shoes, ambition, and discomfort. He understands how badly she wants to escape the life she came from. Thomas Harris makes every conversation between them a contest.

Clarice needs information, while Lecter demands personal memories in exchange. She has to remain honest enough to interest him without surrendering control. Outside the cell, Buffalo Bill’s latest victim is still alive, which gives the psychological games a real deadline. Harris also fills the investigation with forensic detail, institutional pressure, sexism, and mistakes that cost time. Clarice earns progress through observation rather than sudden genius. Her fear never disappears, especially when she enters Bill’s house alone. She continues anyway. In a book filled with men who study, test, dismiss, or hunt women, Clarice refuses to become someone else’s object.

We live in a world, at least in our hectic Western-based culture, that is driven by probability. What is probability? How does it taste, smell, or feel? Does it just linger in our complexity, waiting for a never-achieved form? We calculate within probability both with our awareness and unawareness. It penetrates our educational, economic, political, and medical systems. It assumes that we will automatically accept models of certainty. Probability is all-powerful and designed to make you believe its outcomes are absolute. It waits for our lives to pass through it with definitive rules to fit all. However, it can never seem to guarantee success. It tries to be the basis of caution, yet it can also be the source of the other side of safety. Why? Probability measures the likelihood or chance that a specific event will occur. It survives on unresolved paradoxes of this or that, and that misses the yin/yang, messy/beautifulness of life in all its complexity. It can be funny, as in humor, and puzzling with choices, but when not harnessed, it feels separate and fragmented, avoiding the ecological process of how all patterns connect.

Probability evades the wisdom of understanding how love, altruism, and empathy can breed kindness, which some might consider the regulating homeostasis basis of living systems. Anthropologist Gregory Bateson more than hinted at the misunderstanding of probability’s faults by suggesting that absolute outcomes hide how "Human actions involve play, context, and the probability of disaster or risk within a relationship." Probability makes us too busy to savor what happens between relationships, yet when we do, it can help us step back from absolute outcomes and certainty and find improvisational potential. When we do, with encouragement, this can create all kinds of possibilities. Maria Popova, for instance, mentions in the first stanza of her poem "The Wildest Bet Is the Winning Bet":

"You wouldn’t have bet on it,the battered rockorbiting a starfrom the discount binof the universe"

Probability dissolves when you zoom outward with your personal lens and widen your perspective, making room for kindness to be born. Probability, in this case, begins to blend with how we share our possessions. It becomes an advocate for tending to others' needs, which gives ultimate meaning to who we are as humans. It becomes the terrain of kindness, the quality or state of being friendly, generous, considerate, and genuinely concerned for the well-being of others. Through kindness, we can choose to blend with the paradoxes inherent in unchecked probability to see an ecological unity in a manner felt by C.S. Lewis, who wrote in Surprised by Joy: "Only when your whole attention and desire are fixed on something else—whether a distant mountain, or the past, or the gods of Asgard—does the 'thrill' arise." That is the prerequisite for kindness.

Using kindness as an ecological framework supports a sense of order in contrast to probability’s search for finite answers. This demand for finitude is what excludes kindness because it depends on producing outcomes. Kindness, however, is driven by understanding the constraints and interdependency of any living system. This is why it is woven into global popular symbolism and metaphors. It is based on maintaining what may be good for humanity rather than accumulating something. It is forming a kinship based on forgiveness, not to condone past pain but to move forward with altruism and empathy. Kindness emerges in what poet Allen Ginsberg poetically called a state that "stretches thru every probability." It is the beginner's mind of temporary emptiness, which can at first seem conceptually liable, mistaken for sheer nothingness, yet is in fact the improvisational potential for possibilities of kindness.

We must recognize this need for kindness, especially in today's struggle to accept diversity. This underscores a profound reminder, particularly useful as we celebrate Thanksgiving, that the Anglo experience in North America began as a big mistake in probability, as Garrison Keillor reminds us: "...we survived only by the kindness of strangers who had every reason to be cruel." In a quote often attributed to Mark Twain, this type of innate empathy is "...the language which the deaf can hear and the blind can see."

When the dictates of probability, with seemingly good intentions, succumb to the dark side of fragmented content in our educational systems, isolated profit-driven health mandates, and sociopolitical polarization, the antidote is massive acts of kindness. This releases feel-good neurotransmitters like dopamine, serotonin, and endorphins. It activates the brain's reward center, creating euphoria and emotional warmth. It further reduces stress by soothing the vagus nerve, which recharges and rejuvenates when it feels secure.

It is interesting how unchecked probability is the cloak of "science" that constantly tries to quantify or refute the effects of kindness, when it only needs to observe how it strengthens social bonds like a welcome adhesive. Kindness can increase trust and collaboration, and encourage greater equity in the distribution of wealth, security, and opportunity, allowing for our innate interdependency with each other and nature. Another worthwhile attribute is the resulting togetherness that correlates with lower rates of anxiety and depression. Consider this in contrast to the array of physical ailments that can occur with unchecked probability, and it's a choice worth exploring.
