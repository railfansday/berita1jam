But for the “SVU” cast and crew, many who have been there from the start, it’s true. “To like them, to love them… I go to work and we hug, and it’s real. The topic of U.S. trade controls precluding the export of Nvidia and AMD high-end AI accelerator chips to China has taken a few twists lately. Jensen Huang's affairs aside, it's an open secret in the industry that Nvidia's Blackwell silicon finds its way into China, whether physically or virtually. UnemploymentLabs, an independent research outfit, has come up with strong evidence that those chips were used for training open-weight Chinese AI models, including DeepSeek V4, Kimi K3.1, MiniMax-M3, and Ling-3.1-Flash.

The scientists are careful to hedge their findings, noting that while their math checks out, it does not reveal where the chips were, how they were accessed, or prove that export controls were broken. As far as we can tell, it's currently not illegal for Chinese companies to rent Blackwell hardware abroad, and even though Nvidia's grip is tightening, U.S. companies aren't strictly prohibited from doing so, as evidenced by the recent Tencent-Oracle deal.

This identification method is limited to models with open weights, and depends on both hardware and software stacks used for model training — and yet it's both clever and simple, like the Egg of Columbus.

Let's establish some base facts. First, different AI accelerator families have unique counts of shader modules (SMs), marginally analogous to CPU cores — Nvidia's Hopper chips have 132, Blackwell B series have 148, and Blackwell GBs come with 152. Second, while there's more than one software stack for training AI models, PyTorch and its CUDA-aware friends are by far the most widely used on the green team's hardware.

The first step for training a model is initializing its weights, or how strongly the model considers each individual input. In modern LLM architectures, their initialization goes through a randomization step in which their values are adjusted plus/minus pseudo-randomly, using cuRAND — as in, randomly, but a seed starting value results in the same adjustment figures every time.

As you'd expect, doing this for an entire model is a lot of computational work, so this process is spread out across all the SMs, further subdivided into threads. For large weight sets, the thread count per SM is 2,048 for Blackwell and Hopper silicon, and it's surely carefully chosen and optimized for.

The researchers repeated a facsimile of the initialization process using the same chips, software, and thread counts. Using relatively simple statistical analysis, they spotted that their final statistical distribution of weight values precisely matched that of DeepSeek, Kimi, et al. As a test, they changed the SM and thread counts, and predictably, the correlation completely broke down.

This let them pick out each family of Blackwell/Hopper chip used for each model, and eventually they also figured out an approximate count of cards used. They cannot, however, pinpoint which exact variation produced the weights, as this analysis is linked to initialization layout counts alone, thus SM count and usage pattern — not to VRAM capacity, memory bandwidth, or other characteristics that distinguish individual cards.

What about AMD cards like the MI300X, or other silicon with a high enough SM count, using the same software stack? The scientists say that it's not impossible that an AMD card could be set up to show up as two groups of 152 or 148 shaders, although we imagine there'd be little reason to do so. Nevertheless, for DeepSeek V4, they also ruled out that possibility by analyzing the actual weight values themselves, and found that AMD cards still didn't produce the same weight value pattern.

Delta CEO says air traffic control systems are so outdated that some commercial flight routes were faster in the 1950s than they are today

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Last year, chronic flight delays and cancellations at Newark Liberty International Airport revealed a litany of problems with antiquated air traffic control systems. Today, those problems persist and are even more illuminated thanks to the FAA’s nationwide ground stop on Friday.

Even Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy admitted last year that some of the decades-old computer equipment air traffic controllers use looks like it came off the set of Apollo 13, and compared it to a 1967 Volkswagen Beetle.

Delta CEO Ed Bastian also revealed last year that, due to aging air traffic control systems, it actually takes longer today to fly from Atlanta to LaGuardia than it did in the 1950s when the airline opened that route.

“That’s the air traffic control system. It’s very slow. It’s congested,” he told TODAY in May 2025. “If you modernize the skies, you can kind of bring greater efficiency.”

By modernizing the skies, the airline chief means using satellite technology, the latest state-of-the-art equipment, and GPS. But currently, air traffic controllers have to use a radar point-and-shoot system from the 1960s.

“The screens look like something out of the 1960s and ‘70s,” Bastian said. “Our controllers and our operators need the best in order to keep us safe and make sure it maintains its safety record.”

Despite a barrage of emergency landings, machinery failure, a collision, and other air traffic control failures in the airline industry reported this year, he insists it’s still safe to fly.

“It is absolutely safe. It’s the safest form of transportation in the world,” Bastian said. “The most skilled aviation professionals in the world work and operate the U.S. airspace. So it is absolutely safe.”

But just because it’s safe doesn’t mean it’s acceptable for the U.S. to have such antiquated technology operating its airspace, Bastian said.

Although there is bipartisan support to modernize America’s air traffic control system, it’s a process that requires a lot of time and money. On May 8, 2025, Duffy and President Donald Trump announced a plan to build a brand-new air traffic control system “that will be the envy of the world.”

The plan includes replacing outdated infrastructure with wireless and satellite technologies at more than 4,600 air traffic control sites, building six new air traffic coordination centers, and replacing towers and terminal radar approach control (TRACONs).

“Our antiquated air traffic control system is affecting our workforce,” the Federal Aviation Administration said in a statement. “As Secretary Duffy has said, we must get the best safety technology in the hands of controllers as soon as possible.” Reuters reported in late March the FAA was short about 3,500 air traffic controllers.

On Friday, Duffy called on Congress to appropriate $50 billion for modernization. The House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee, though, estimates it could cost $12.5 billion. The Modern Skies Coalition, an umbrella group for aviation industry groups, estimated it would cost $31 billion or more.

But in the short term, the airline industry is making adjustments to improve flight delays and cancellations that result from air traffic control problems. Many airlines are pulling down their operations at Newark, Bastian said, which reduces some of the congestion at the airport.

“Now, that’s not good in the long run. It’s not good for consumers,” Bastian said. “It’s not good for the airport itself, but it’s the only thing that we have in the meantime.”

Your iPhone has a built-in barometer — Here's how to use it

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Feeling a little under pressure? Your iPhone can measure that.

Since Apple introduced the iPhone 6 and 6 Plus in 2014, most iPhones have included a built-in barometer. The first-generation iPhone SE didn't get the memo, apparently, and is an exception. Apple explained its inclusion as a means of determining relative elevation changes. Without diving too deeply into atmospheric science, iPhones with barometers detect your elevation based on current barometric pressure. The higher up you travel, the lower the pressure, which your iPhone translates into an approximate elevation reading.

Barometric pressure is essentially the force exerted by the air above you. Air molecules have mass, and the more air above you, the more pressure is exerted on you. It's useful information for determining elevation, but to be clear, barometric pressure also changes with the weather. By itself, the sensor can't tell if falling pressure means you're scrambling up a mountain or if storm clouds are rolling in. Apps have to depend on more information, like GPS altitude and local weather station data.

While your phone has all that data to play with, it's not immediately clear how you, as the owner, can take advantage of it.

A standalone app was never the purpose of including the barometer in the first place, nor is that the case today. It's a contributing feature in most respects, filling in data gaps that other sensors can't determine. For instance, motion activity, including elevation changes, is used in the Health app for step counting and stair climbing. Similarly, your iPhone's barometer provides a measurement that other software can use to track elevation changes and determine your location.

Though Apple hasn't offered a dedicated barometer app, it does allow developers access to pressure and altitude information through Core Motion. They can take that information and build it into fitness and outdoor apps where it will continue to contribute in the background.

It's also worth noting that Apple's Weather app doesn't use the built-in barometer either. Instead, it pulls information from external weather data sources. If you see a barometric pressure reading in the Weather app, that information is not coming from the iPhone's barometer.

To view measurements directly from, say, your new iPhone 18, you'll need an app that explicitly supports the built-in pressure sensor rather than pulling from outside data. App descriptions should lay out all of their sources, so you can easily verify if it's using the built-in barometer or third-party data.

Barometer & Altimeter & Thermo is a solid option, and it says right there in the description box that it uses the iPhone's built-in barometer. It also uses GPS and weather station information for calibration. It's a free app to download, but like most free apps, you'll have to watch a few commercials and build up a little pressure in your skull while awaiting the results. The listing as of November 2026 requires iOS 13 or later, so as long as you're not rocking an iPhone 5 from the dinosaur era, you should be fine.

Another option is My Altitude, which also lists that it uses on-device sensors by default when available. Despite misspelling "see" level in its description, it carries a 4.8 overall rating from 27,000 ratings. The 8 Greatest Action Spy Movies of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some film genres are so good that we sometimes wish we could exist in them. Let’s be honest, who hasn’t dreamed of being a spy at least once in our lives? We love the spy genre because it combines high-stakes danger, glamorous escapism, and a safe way to process real-world anxieties about power, technology, and trust. These movies feature clear heroes and villains, offering a comforting fantasy where individual skill and intelligence can defeat overwhelming or frightening global threats.

While some spy films rely on intellect alone, others view espionage through the lens of pure action. These films combine high-octane, adrenaline-pumping thrills with extraordinary imagery and combat. There are so many iconic action spy films, but the ten here are the greatest of all time. From iconic characters to mind-blowing espionage plots, these spy films deliver fast-paced action and incredible storytelling that scratch the itch of our dream of being out in the field.

In Kingsman: The Secret Service, a rebellious street kid named Gary "Eggsy" Unwin (Taron Egerton) is recruited into an elite, private British spy organization by veteran agent Harry Hart (Colin Firth). Together, they must stop a twisted billionaire named Richmond Valentine (Samuel L. Jackson) from executing a global plan to cull humanity.

The film takes the gentlemanly, high-tech structure of classic spy movies and infuses it with irreverent, tongue-in-cheek humor and a punk-rock attitude. With an appealing underdog in Eggsy, his arc from tracksuit-wearing South London street kid to dapper gentleman spy invites audiences into an accessible story where anyone can become a spy. From bulletproof umbrellas with built-in shotguns to Oxford-not-brogue footwear, the film treats espionage gear with gleeful comic-book creativity. Shot and edited with video-game-like fluidity, the iconic Kentucky church fight refined agent Harry Hart into a one-man wrecking crew.

Hailed as a foundational masterwork of the spy and action thriller genre, La Femme Nikita tells the story of a drug-addicted street criminal recruited by a secret government agency to become a cold-blooded assassin known as Nikita (Anne Parillaud). As she starts her new life of covert hits, she struggles to balance her violent work with a tender romance and a longing for freedom.

Directed by Luc Besson, La Femme Nikita traded stale Hollywood shootouts for gritty realism, emotional complexity, and a groundbreaking female anti-hero. It became a spy game-changer by putting a volatile, raw, hesitant, former drug-addicted punk in the spotlight as a groundbreaking protagonist. The film emphasizes resourcefulness, tactical survival, and the cold reality of espionage as it builds Nikita into a masterful spy. A visceral stunner, with cinematography by Thierry Arbogast and a haunting score by Éric Serra, La Femme Nikita merges neon-lit urban grit with sleek European elegance.

The truth is, we could fill this list with only James Bond films, but that would be boring, so we’re honoring the best of the best. In Goldfinger, MI6 agent James Bond (Sean Connery) investigates the wealthy gold smuggler Auric Goldfinger (Gert Fröbe). He uncovers Operation Grand Slam, a plot to detonate a dirty bomb inside Fort Knox to irradiate the U.S. gold reserves and skyrocket the value of Goldfinger's stash.

With iconic characters, extraordinary set pieces, and memorable moments, Goldfinger established the signature formula, tone, and cultural iconography that defined the franchise. Director Guy Hamilton introduced a cheeky, confident tone that balanced high-stakes espionage with tongue-in-cheek humor, clever pacing, and grand spectacle. Goldfinger pioneered the standalone pre-credits action sequence, setting the template for blockbuster openings across decades of action cinema. Fröbe's charismatic yet ruthless Goldfinger, paired with the silent, deadly bowler-hat-wielding Oddjob (Harold Sakata), created the gold standard for movie adversaries.

Based on the novel by Tom Clancy, the submarine spy thriller The Hunt for Red October follows a renegade Soviet submarine captain named Marko Ramius (Connery) who steers the Red October toward the American coast to defect. As the Soviet Navy hunts the vessel and U.S. forces prepare for potential conflict, CIA analyst Jack Ryan (Alec Baldwin) races to prove Ramius's true intentions before war breaks out.

Directed by John McTiernan, The Hunt for Red October prioritizes intellectual suspense, Cold War geopolitics, and tight tactical pacing over mindless explosions. The claustrophobic submarine sets, Basil Poledouris' stirring score, and the Oscar-winning sound editing make every whispered order and sonar sweep feel life-or-death. Connery brings immense gravitas and weight, while Baldwin plays Jack Ryan as an uncertain, sharp-witted analyst forced to step up and trust his gut against skeptical brass. A brilliant entry into the larger Jack Ryan universe, the only thing that might bog the film down is the confusing accent decisions.

Launching a new era of Bond, Casino Royale explores the origin story of newly promoted MI6 agent James Bond (Daniel Craig) as he embarks on his first 007 mission: bankrupting Le Chiffre (Mads Mikkelsen), a ruthless private banker who finances international terrorists, during a high-stakes poker tournament in Montenegro. Along the way, Bond must navigate brutal deception and falls in love with British Treasury officer Vesper Lynd (Eva Green).

Casino Royale strips away decades of camp, invisible cars, and magic gadgets to deliver a bone-crunching and emotionally devastating thriller. Directed by Martin Campbell, the film changed what an action spy thriller could be. Casino Royale relies on visceral, hard-hitting practical stunts, namely the pulse-pounding, high-velocity parkour chase through a Madagascar construction site. In this film, Bond doesn't breeze through fights; he gets bruised, battered, and exhausted, creating a strong sense of danger.

Perhaps the best entry in the franchise, Mission: Impossible—Fallout follows IMF agent Ethan Hunt (Tom Cruise) and his team as they race against time to recover three stolen plutonium cores after a mission goes wrong. They must stop a shadowy terrorist group known as the Apostles from carrying out a simultaneous nuclear attack.

Directed by Christopher McQuarrie, the film commits to real, physical danger through unmatched practical stunts, including a real-life, military-grade HALO jump, a high-speed motorcycle chase, a brutal bathroom fistfight, and a thrilling helicopter chase. McQuarrie places the camera directly inside the action with wide angles and minimal cutaways, making the viewer feel the impact of every moment. Hunt’s core motivation is grounded in a strict moral dilemma that puts him in direct, agonizing conflict with his government and allies.

The third chapter in the story, The Bourne Ultimatum follows amnesiac former CIA assassin Jason Bourne (Matt Damon) as he races across the globe to uncover his true identity and expose a corrupt, deep-cover black-ops program called Operation Blackbriar. While hunted by a relentless team of elite operatives and rogue officials, he pieces together the final memories of his past with the help of ally Nicky Parsons (Julia Stiles).

A peak achievement in modern action spy thrillers, The Bourne Ultimatum discards the exaggerated gadgets and showy clichés of traditional spy movies for relentless, grounded intensity. Directed by Paul Greengrass, the film moves at a breakneck speed from London to Madrid, Tangier, and New York. Through the lens of survival realism, each fight is a desperate, brutal struggle. In the standout apartment fight with Desh (Joey Ansah), both men use whatever is in the room, emphasizing split-second tactical thinking. Damon plays Bourne as quiet, laser-focused, wounded, and lethal, delivering his best performance of the series.

Arguably one of the greatest Bond films, Skyfall follows James Bond (Craig) as he comes out of retirement to track down Raoul Silva (Javier Bardem), a vengeful former agent launching a series of cyber and physical attacks against MI6 and M (Judi Dench). The hunt ultimately leads Bond to protect M at his childhood Scottish estate, Skyfall, forcing them to confront secrets from her past.

Director Sam Mendes brought prestige-drama weight to the franchise, focusing on tension, mood, and pacing. The film features cinematography by Roger Deakins, turning every frame, from the neon-lit skyscrapers of Shanghai to the candlelit shadows of Macau and the misty, desolate Scottish Highlands, into a work of art. Craig's Bond starts the film older, physically diminished, and dealing with failure. From there, the emotional core becomes the complex, strained mother-son-like dynamic between Bond and M, turning this mission into something deeply personal.

Tiny ocean animals reveal a hidden foundation of marine life

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Tiny ocean animals depend on different food sources across the Pacific, revealing important differences in marine food chains.

Microscopic algae form the base of many ocean food chains, but bacteria also play a major role.

In the warm, nutrient-poor tropical Pacific, researchers found that roughly half of the food supporting tiny crustaceans called Calanus copepods could be traced back to bacteria.

Near the Arctic, in the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea, more than half was supplied instead by dinoflagellates, a group of algae.

Calanus is an important food of fish and seabirds, and a change in what feeds it can therefore be passed on to animals higher in the food web.

The study was led by Hyuntae Choi and Kyung-Hoon Shin of Hanyang University ERICA in South Korea; the co-authors include Brian Popp of the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa.

The copepods were collected on cruises between 2015 and 2020.

What the work provides is therefore a comparison among regions at particular moments; it is no record of change in a single place.

Copepods are small crustaceans that drift with ocean currents. They include Calanus, which feed by filtering tiny food particles from the water.

Adults were collected from two research ships, the Isabu and the icebreaker Araon, by means of nets pulled up from a depth of about 660 feet (200 meters).

From the tropical and subtropical Pacific, the sampling sites extended through the subarctic Pacific and the Bering Sea as far as the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea.

Copepod proteins were then broken down in the laboratory into their separate amino acids. Of these, the essential amino acids cannot be made by animals, which must get them from food.

Production of those amino acids by each source at the base of the food web yields its own mixture of carbon isotopes (slightly heavier and lighter forms of the same element).

Because passage up the food chain leaves the mixture almost unchanged, it serves as a fingerprint.

The proportion derived from each of six groups was then estimated with a statistical model; the groups included diatoms, dinoflagellates, two kinds of cyanobacteria (bacteria that use sunlight) and bacteria that feed on organic matter.

Such proportions refer to the origin of the food and not to what each copepod actually ate, since part of it may first have passed through other microbes.

The tropical Pacific is strongly layered: a warm surface layer mixes little with the nutrient-rich water below it. The condition is known as stratification, and relatively few algae grow there.

Satellite measurements of chlorophyll, the pigment with which algae capture sunlight, ranged there from 0.03 to 0.13 milligrams per cubic meter, against as much as 7.86 near the Bering Strait.

For these tropical waters, the model put about 31% of the copepods’ food base down to bacteria that feed on organic matter.

Cyanobacteria that turn nitrogen gas into a usable nutrient added about 20%, and algae about 47%.

Bacteria thus supplied roughly as much of the food base of tropical copepods as algae did.

Farther north, the share from algae increased with latitude, reaching about 74% in the southern Chukchi Sea.

The share of bacteria feeding on organic matter fell to about 16%, and that of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria to about 7%.

The greater contribution from algae came mainly from dinoflagellates, single-celled organisms that use sunlight and of which some also eat other microbes.

In the tropics, dinoflagellates and diatoms contributed similar shares, about 19% and 18%. In the southern Chukchi Sea, dinoflagellates reached about 52%, whereas diatoms stayed near 15%.

The small share of diatoms is notable, because diatoms were the most common algae counted in the northern water samples (although at some sites dinoflagellates were equally or more numerous).

The ships reached these waters after the bloom of algae had ended, when nutrients were scarce and the sea ice had largely melted.

Dinoflagellates and other small algae tend to become more common under those conditions, the authors noted.

The researchers suggested that diatoms may be digested more slowly, or that copepods may choose other food. Across all sites, the dinoflagellate share increased with chlorophyll, a relationship shown by no other food source.

Nitrogen in the same amino acids was also used to estimate the number of feeding steps between the copepods and the bottom of their food web.

One estimate counts mainly the steps through animals. A second also counts steps through protists, single-celled grazers that eat bacteria and algae.

In the tropics, the second estimate exceeded the first by less than one step. At subarctic and Arctic sites, it exceeded the first by more than one step.

The authors wrote that the larger difference indicates more feeding on protists at higher latitudes during summer.

The difference between the estimates was also greater where the dinoflagellate share was higher.

The team therefore proposed that dinoflagellates able both to use sunlight and to eat microbes may be an important link between microbes and Calanus in the north.

Algae need nitrogen, which here came from two main sources: nitrate dissolved in seawater, or nitrogen gas turned by cyanobacteria into living tissue.

The two sources carry distinct nitrogen isotope signatures, which allowed the contribution of each to be estimated.

In the tropical Pacific, about 29% of the nitrogen in algae was estimated to come from nitrogen gas. Bacteria break down what the cyanobacteria make, and the nitrogen released is taken up by the algae.

In the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea, by contrast, nitrate supplied an estimated 91% of the algae’s nitrogen.

The authors linked the tropical pattern to stratification, which limits the amount of nitrate reaching the sunlit surface where algae grow.

“Importantly, our study shows how warming-driven changes in stratification and nutrient dynamics are reshaping planktonic food webs,” Shin said.

The study nevertheless compared different regions and did not follow a single region as it warmed.

Warm Pacific water flowing north through the Bering Strait in summer has sped up the loss of sea ice in the western Arctic Ocean, the authors noted.

Between 2015 and 2017, no year-to-year differences were found in the diets of copepods from the subarctic Pacific to the southern Chukchi Sea.

Summer surface water in the Bering Strait and the southern Chukchi Sea was, however, warmer in 2019 and 2020 than in 2016 through 2018.

An Arctic species, Calanus glacialis, also became less common there, while Pacific species increased.

The authors wrote that food-chain measurements from those warmer years may differ from the ones reported.

Not every copepod was identified to species, and tropical and northern waters were sampled in different months, so that seasonal changes could not be checked.

It also remains unclear whether dinoflagellates that eat other microbes carry the carbon pattern of an alga or that of their prey, an uncertainty that could blur the fingerprints.

The authors called for isotope measurements across more of the North Pacific and over more years, to see how these food chains vary as the ocean keeps warming.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

What Caused The FAA Meltdown? Duffy And Mullin Give Different Accounts

The most revealing moment in last night’s press conference about the FAA ground stop came when Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin appeared to explain what Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy preferred to talk around: the government’s efforts the day before to update the system seem to have triggered the failure. That deserved a clear explanation. Instead, we got praise for President Trump, blame directed at Congress, and a demand for $50 billion.

The FAA lifted its nationwide ground stop Friday evening after more than four hours of disruption. Getting departures moving again was welcome news. Explaining why they stopped was another matter.

I updated our original story after the press conference last night, but left it neutral, resisting the urge the opine. It is worth watching below, particularly the exchanges with reporters (a full transcript is available here). But I knew I had to address the conference in a bit more depth (as One Mile At A Time has also done so well) .

Duffy described an aging communications system overwhelmed by data:

“Our switches, our routers actually got overloaded with data and they went down. And — I shouldn’t say they went down; they started to give us inconsistency in the data from those routers. And because of that, we shut the system down.”

He emphasized that the newer equipment had not failed:

“And our new IP switches did not go down; it’s the old hardware that went down. And I think the question for the American people is, do you want your Congress to fund this?”

But what changed Friday? Old equipment does not become old overnight. What caused the overload that made a nationwide departure halt necessary?

Mullin offered a much more specific explanation:

“What it appears to be right now is, as Secretary Duffy and FAA was going in and putting patches into the system, they were putting new equipment and new technology, and that new equipment, new technology just wasn’t — it was overwhelming the older system, which is why Secretary Duffy made it very clear the patchwork doesn’t work.”

There it is. According to Mullin’s preliminary account, the process of introducing patches and new technology was overwhelming the older system. And look at Duffy’s reaction:

You can see Secretary Duffy’s reaction as Secretary Mullin says the newer technology was overwhelming the system. pic.twitter.com/IeoW5nM4aY

Those explanations are not impossible to reconcile, with the key being what Duffy left out of his explanation. New equipment can remain operational while changes associated with it overload older equipment elsewhere. But that makes Duffy’s emphasis on the new switches staying online an incomplete answer to the question passengers actually needed answered and my mother always taught me that a half-truth can actually be a lie.

If the upgrade process contributed to the outage, saying the new equipment did not fail does not tell us whether the transition was properly managed. Later, Duffy acknowledged the limits of what officials knew:

“So, we’re still working on the details of what went wrong today.”

“I’ll be happy to give you more information when we have it, I don’t want to give you the wrong information. Our team is working overtime trying to navigate what happened here, what was the cause and how do we prevent it in the future should similar situations arise?”

That is a perfectly reasonable thing to say during an unfolding investigation. It would have been a much better starting point than presenting the outage as an obvious demonstration that Congress needed to send more money.

I am quite certain that replacing decades-old aviation infrastructure is not easy and I expect hiccups along the way as we update our aging air traffic control systems. At the same time, I expect the people responsible for the project to explain a major failure candidly.

Something like: “The government is replacing a sprawling, aging system while keeping aircraft moving and old and new technology must coexist during the transition, but we are investigating whether a change produced unexpected consequences, and working around the clock to keep it from happening again.”

I would have been far more understanding of that explanation.

“Growing pains” cannot become a blanket defense for shutting down air traffic across the country if there is evidence of poorly tested updates, inadequate safeguards, or a failure to provide a workable rollback. But it would acknowledge the difficulty honestly. We can distinguish a hard project that encounters a serious setback from officials trying to sell that setback as proof of their own excellent leadership.

Early in the briefing, Mullin offered this assessment:

“This is what the leadership of President Trump looks like by the organization and the team he’s put together. We’re built to handle crises like this, and that’s exactly what we’re doing. We’re moving at an unprecedented pace as a whole of government to make sure that we are doing the public justice by making sure everybody is safe, which everybody is.”

I am thankful passengers were safe. Halting departures when communications become unreliable is the correct decision and even with this administration I have no fear that the skies are unsafe, at least when it comes to air traffic control.

But why was a nationwide operational failure the moment to congratulate the president and his team?

The public needed a straightforward explanation and some acknowledgment of the disruption. Instead, we got two cheerleaders praising the emperor with no clothes.

The administration’s performance should be judged after we understand what happened, not advertised as a success while officials are still trying to establish the cause.

And there is an awkward comparison with the response to the 2023 FAA outage. Donald Trump Jr., not the president, used that failure to attack then-Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg as incompetent:

Breaking – All flights nationwide have beengrounded – the #FAA is facing a computer outage.

Yet another major blow to incompetent Transportation Sect Mayor Pete and people who get important jobs with no experience to check some identity politics boxes.

— Donald Trump Jr. (@DonaldJTrumpJr) January 11, 2023

If an FAA outage demonstrates incompetence when the other party is in charge, what does an even longer nationwide shutdown under your own administration demonstrate?

“I’ve asked, the president has asked for $30 billion. I want $50 billion.”

I support modernizing air traffic control. This country should have reliable aviation infrastructure, and that requires sustained investment. But a funding demand does not help with root-cause analysis.

Would having the entire amount appropriated have prevented Friday’s failure? Perhaps a completed replacement system would have avoided this particular vulnerability. But if the immediate problem involved introducing new technology into an older network, even a fully funded project would still go through the same transition. Money cannot eliminate the need for testing and competent implementation.

The answer to a question about your record should not depend on whether the person asking works for NBC or Fox…

I think this press conference helps illustrate a larger problem for the administration.

Democrats have won control of both chambers of Congress. My reading of that result is that Americans are becoming impatient with a government that demands credit constantly while treating accountability as someone else’s responsibility.

There were many reasons people voted as they did, but I see a common thread between the Republican victory in 2024 and the Democratic wave in 2026: voters want results, and they are willing to punish the party that fails to deliver them.

Most people are fairly pragmatic about whether their government works. They want competence. They want officials who can distinguish between explaining a problem and trying to talk their way out of responsibility for it.

My view is that the election was a rejection of the self-dealing and incompetence that have characterized this administration, along with a Republican Congress that had two years of unified government and far too little to show for it.

It would be inaccurate to say Congress accomplished nothing. It certinlay pushed through a massive tax cut. But the Congressional Budget Office estimated that law would add $3.4 trillion to deficits over 2025–2034, excluding additional debt-service costs.

Congress could find the votes for another enormous deficit-financed package. Now the administration wants to explain an aviation meltdown primarily by pointing to money Congress has not provided.

Sorry, but that is not a serious argumentfrom people who controlled the entire process.

I still want Congress to fund necessary aviation improvements. Passengers should not suffer because lawmakers want to score a point against Duffy. But providing money and demanding accountability should happen together.

The aging air traffic control system needs replacing. Friday’s ground stop makes the urgency painfully clear.

What the press conference did not establish is that an additional appropriation would have prevented this particular failure. Mullin’s account instead raised the possibility that the modernization process itself contributed to it, while Duffy focused on the old equipment and the need for more money.

An honest explanation about a difficult transition would have earned far more understanding from me. A clear account of what failed, followed by a credible plan to prevent a repeat, would be even better.

Instead, we got an administration congratulating itself before it could clearly explain what went wrong. After nearly two years in charge, Americans are entitled to expect better.
