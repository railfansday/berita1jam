Groundcover turns its Wand acquisition into a Kubernetes rightsizing beta

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Observability company groundcover Inc. today added Autonomous Infrastructure to its platform in beta to rightsize the processor and memory requests automatically on customers’ Kubernetes workloads based on how each workload behaves in production.

The technology comes from Kubernetes resource optimization startup Wand Cloud Ltd., which groundcover acquired in September.

The release is aimed at the processor and memory requests Kubernetes has engineers set for each workload up front, usually before it has handled any real traffic, and groundcover said most teams seldom return to those numbers. When one is set too low, the first sign is often a service throwing out-of-memory errors. Padding that goes unused ends up on the monthly cloud bill.

Because the groundcover sensor has been collecting production history all along, a customer on a supported version can open Autonomous Infrastructure for the first time without installing anything and find the data the feature needs already in place. Requested processor and memory for each workload appear next to actual usage. Estimated monthly savings sit beside a count of under-provisioned workloads, and a chart tracks over time how much of the potential saving has been realized.

Dollar figures in the beta are based on a price per processor and memory unit that customers enter themselves. Public cloud pricing is the next step, according to groundcover, and billing data such as the Cost and Usage Report from Amazon Web Services Inc. will come after that.

Most resource optimization tools size each workload in isolation, groundcover argues, so a request that looks right for one service can still starve its neighbors on a crowded node. Stability is the first constraint in groundcover’s own software. Before Autonomous Infrastructure raises a request, it checks how tightly the node is already packed so one workload’s increase does not leave the rest short of resources.

Shahar Azulay, groundcover’s co-founder and chief executive, said customers can check what their clusters stand to save, from data already held in their own cloud accounts, before they let Autonomous Infrastructure “change anything.” The feature stays off until an engineer turns it on and picks the deployments, namespaces or clusters it manages.

During the beta the software touches requests only and leaves limits where they are. Each policy carries minimum and maximum bounds for processor and memory, and an audit log records who assigned each workload to a policy and when. Groundcover suggests starting with lower-risk workloads such as development namespaces and leaving alone something like a production database that needs room to grow.

The beta is available now at no additional cost to every groundcover customer running its sensor on Kubernetes, and the company is also recruiting design partners to work with its product team while it runs. Demonstrations are scheduled for KubeCon + CloudNativeCon North America in Salt Lake City Nov. 9-12.

Groundcover is a venture capital-backed startup that has raised $160 million to date, including a $100 million Series C round led by One Peak in July.

Creators will be able to add up to 60 seconds of a track directly to their videos. They can browse trending songs, preview tracks before selecting them, filter by genre or length, and explore collections curated by the labels. Favourite songs can also be bookmarked for later.

This deal gives the major labels another route into short-form video, with licensing built into the editing process rather than handled only after a video reaches platforms such as TikTok or Instagram Reels.

New iPad mini hands-on: Refreshed design, OLED display, more

Apple’s smallest iPad has gotten its biggest update in years. The new iPad mini features a much thinner and lighter design, a larger OLED display, water resistance, and the powerful A20 Pro chip.

I’ve been testing the new iPad mini, and there’s a lot to like — but also a couple of frustrations. Here’s everything you need to know.

The new iPad mini lives up to the “mini” name even more than its predecessor. It’s significantly thinner, down from 6.3mm on the previous iPad mini to 5.4mm on the new model. In fact, that puts it very close to the ultra-thin iPad Pro and the unfolded iPhone Duo. Here’s how it compares:

The new iPad mini is also significantly lighter, dropping from 293 grams to 255 grams. That puts it in the realm of the iPhone Duo, for comparison’s sake:

The reduction in weight and thickness was immediately noticeable when I picked up the new iPad mini. It feels significantly nicer to hold, more premium, and less “dense” in my hands.

The new iPad mini features an 8.5-inch screen, a slight increase from the previous generation’s 8.3-inch display. The overall footprint is mostly the same (the new model is actually about 2.2mm narrower). The larger screen is possible because the new iPad mini’s bezels are 24 percent thinner than before.

In addition to allowing a bigger screen in the same form factor, the narrower bezels also modernize the iPad mini’s overall look and feel. The bezels are noticeably slimmer than the iPad Air and base model iPad, and slightly thinner than the iPad Pro.

With smaller bezels, a thinner chassis, and a lighter weight, the new iPad mini feels significantly more premium than before. It reminds me less of the base model iPad or iPad Air and more of the iPad Pro, just shrunken down.

The big news about the display, though, is the switch to OLED. This marks Apple’s first non-Pro iPad to offer an OLED screen. It’s not the fancy tandem OLED display of the iPad Pro, but it’s a huge improvement over the previous model’s LCD panel.

The new iPad mini offers SDR brightness up to 600 nits, up from the previous model’s 500 nits max. The iPad mini also supports HDR content, with up to 1200 nits peak brightness.

The new iPad mini’s screen is undeniably an improvement over its predecessor. All of the inherent benefits of OLED apply here, including better colors, improved contrast, higher brightness, and more.

Unfortunately, the new iPad mini’s screen has one limitation: it doesn’t support ProMotion, or a 120Hz refresh rate of any kind. Instead, it’s still locked to 60Hz. This is an incredibly frustrating decision that I can’t quite wrap my head around.

Apple touts the iPad mini’s OLED screen as offering significantly faster pixel response time than the previous generation’s LCD panel, leading to improvements like smoother scrolling and reduced motion blur.

I’ve noticed that things like swiping between Home Screen pages, scrolling social media, and system animations are noticeably smoother. They aren’t, however, as smooth as an Apple device with a ProMotion display, and they’re not a substitute for a higher refresh-rate display.

I’m sure Apple knows the iPad mini’s market, and it seems to have decided not to prioritize ProMotion based on that information. However, as someone who has longed for a so-called “iPad mini Pro” for years, I’m disappointed by the decision. The new iPad mini’s screen is so much better … I just wish Apple had gone that extra step and added ProMotion. We’ve reached a point where ProMotion should be table stakes, especially after Apple brought it to the base iPhone 17 last year.

For an extra $100, you can upgrade the new iPad mini to have a nano-texture display (on the 256GB and 512GB models).

I’m a huge fan of Apple’s nano-texture display and have it on my 14-inch MacBook Pro. On the iPad mini, I think it makes even more sense.

The iPad mini is ultra-portable, and you naturally carry it around and use it in different lighting environments. The nano-texture glass helps reduce reflections and glare in a wide variety of lighting conditions. I also really like the combination of the iPad mini’s book-like size and the nano-texture finish.

There is a slight tradeoff in color vibrancy compared to a glossy OLED screen, as on other Apple devices with nano-texture, but it’s a very minor difference in my experience. The screen still looks fantastic.

One of the more interesting changes with the new iPad mini is something Apple calls the “Acoustic Display.” Instead of traditional speakers with visible grilles, Apple has developed a new system that uses the display itself to produce sound.

Essentially, Apple has designed special woofers that vibrate the display to produce low and midrange frequencies. For higher frequencies, custom tweeters are hidden between the glass and aluminum enclosure. Apple says this new system delivers louder audio, “punchier” bass, and clearer highs, despite the iPad mini’s significantly thinner design.

In practice, I think the new iPad mini sounds great. I’d choose headphones or an external speaker for serious music or video consumption, but day-to-day the iPad mini’s built-in speakers are perfectly adequate. (I’d bet that this Acoustic Display technology shows up in future iPhones too, and the iPad mini is serving as a test bed of sorts.)

The Acoustic Display also plays an important role in another major upgrade: water and dust resistance. The new iPad mini is IP67 rated, which means it can be immersed in 1 meter of freshwater for up to 30 minutes. It’s the first iPad to have an official water-resistance rating.

Adding water resistance to the iPad mini is particularly notable given its size and portability. I naturally find myself wanting to take it everywhere, and water resistance makes that more feasible.

One use case where all these improvements really come together is reading. The thinner and lighter design, nano-texture glass, and water resistance make the new iPad mini the most compelling iPad yet to serve as an e-reader.

The new iPad mini is powered by Apple’s A20 Pro chip, which I already covered in detail in my iPhone 18 Pro review. The short version: the A20 Pro chip gives you plenty of power for anything you can throw at the iPad mini today.

One detail worth calling out is that Apple uses two different versions of the A20 Pro in the iPad mini. The 128GB and 256GB models feature a five-core CPU and 16-core Neural Engine, while the 512GB model gets a six-core CPU and Dual 16-core Neural Engine. All three configurations feature the same seven-core GPU.

I don’t think anyone should buy the 512GB version of the iPad mini just for those extra cores. You should pick your iPad mini configuration based on how much storage you need, not the version of the A20 Pro chip. You don’t need to worry about performance with the new iPad mini. And perhaps most importantly, the A20 Pro chip ensures the iPad mini has enough headroom for the future.

There’s been a lot of consternation about where the iPad mini fits in a world where the iPhone Duo also now exists. It’s a fair question. After all, when the iPhone Duo is unfolded, the two devices share a similar form factor … but that’s really where the similarities end.

The iPad mini offers the full-fledged, unapologetic iPadOS experience. It supports the full multitasking prowess of an iPad Pro, including windowing and the menu bar. The new iPad mini also supports extended display mode, so you can plug it into an external display and extend your iPad experience there.

The iPad mini also gets iPadOS app layouts, and most importantly, a desktop-class browsing experience in Safari. It’s a remarkably capable device in a remarkably compact form factor. It fits where other iPads can’t, yet it runs the same version of iPadOS as other iPads.

The new iPad mini does all of that, but better than before. The thinner, lighter design paired with smaller bezels gives the new iPad mini a meaningful visual refresh. The added water resistance means you can do even more with the compact and pocketable iPad mini design.

But all of this comes at a higher price of $699. This marks the second price increase for the iPad mini this year. It started the year at $499, rose to $599 as part of Apple’s broader price increases in June, and is now $699 as of this refresh.

The new iPad mini also still starts with 128GB of storage. Increasing the base storage to 256GB would’ve helped ease some of the pain of that price increase. It’s also getting increasingly hard to manage a device with 128GB, particularly with the storage needs of Apple Intelligence and Siri AI. The lack of ProMotion and Face ID also make the price increase sting more.

Ultimately, the iPad mini form factor is a joy to use. The design feels great in the hand, and having the full iPadOS experience in such a compact device remains impressive. It’s not quite the “iPad mini Pro” I’ve always wanted, but it’s big upgrade to a form factor that I’ve always loved.

Beyond screens: Mercedes-Benz's new holographic heads-up display

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

After pioneering the passenger screen, Mercedes has designs on the windshield.

Do screens in cars represent luxury? Judging by the latest car interiors, the answer would seem to be an emphatic “yes.” Screens aren’t just getting larger; they are multiplying across the dashboard and morphing into different forms and concepts. Indeed, an interior’s screens are a pretty good indicator of what brand made the car—or at least how much the owner paid for it.

But is this actually what customers want? Are more screens really better? And even if they are, luxury car features inevitably trickle down to cheaper, more mainstream offerings, and screens can only get so big. That leaves luxury automakers with another question to answer: “What’s next?”

Mercedes-Benz is working on a new display alternative that could provide an answer. For now, it’s known as the holographic display. Forget the images from Star Trek and Star Wars that just popped into your head, though. It’s effectively a more advanced form of heads-up display, or HUD.

Whereas a traditional HUD projects images onto the windshield glass, the holographic display has two main components. It still uses a projector, but its key element is a special transparent film laminated into the glass. The film uses holographic optical elements, or “super-small mirrors,” as Mercedes development engineer Lena Hees described them, that selectively reflect the light emitted by the projector, keeping the image visible even in direct sunlight while hiding it from the driver’s view.

Today, the passenger-side touchscreens in Mercedes and other luxury vehicles rely on driver-monitoring cameras or screen polarization to keep the driver from seeing what is playing on the passenger side.

Speaking of passenger screens, the holographic display is clearly being considered as a future alternative or replacement for this technology. Although screens in cars are increasingly ubiquitous, a recent JD Power study indicated that car owners classify passenger screens as “not necessary” and have generally negative opinions about them. Mercedes has clearly observed similar opinions but for slightly different reasons.

“We have customers who like the Hyperscreen,” said development engineer Maximilian Hilger of the pillar-to-pillar screen found in an increasing number of Mercedes models. “But there are others who want to maintain luxury materials.”

In other words, the massive screen design doesn’t seem as luxurious as the pillar-to-pillar bands of wood or other materials found in previous-generation models. And that’s not an opinion isolated to Mercedes’ customers.

New Mercedes chief design officer Bastian Baudy recently told German magazine Auto Motor Sport that “Materiality and haptics must remain tangible—with switches, buttons, and intuitive controls. Real materials are at the heart of Mercedes: metal, wood.”

Mercedes CEO Ola Källenius went further, grouping interior quality and materials among the brand’s “forever values,” along with safety and maintaining what a Mercedes is like to drive.

That presents conflicting priorities, however.

“Yes, you need to have all the [technology] that a customer could ever dream of and wants,” Källenius told Ars. “But you need to package it in an aesthetically pleasing way. That’s also part of it.”

That brings us back to the holographic display. Keep in mind that the photos in this article show early prototypes I saw at Mercedes’ research facilities in Sindelfingen, Germany. The prototype projector sits inside a rather crude, trapezoidal hole dug into the dash of a right-hand-drive, pre-2027 S-Class. It’s also loud, with a cooling fan whirring like a laptop visiting too many junk-filled websites.

The production version would have a much smaller projector opening than those of the prototype and existing heads-up displays. Manual Holstein, a manager working in Mercedes research for future displays, indicated that the projector could be small enough to be hidden behind wood or metal trim pieces. Holstein also said you won’t hear a fan in the production model and that the resolution would improve.

The resolution is certainly considerably better than any current head-up display, including Mercedes’ own, which features augmented reality elements. Full-color, commercial-like videos of the C-Class Electric played during the demonstration, and the display was sufficiently bright, even though Holstein said the prototype was operating at just 20 percent of its maximum brightness.

Issues remain, however. The resolution may be good compared to the arrows, script, and graphics of a current HUD, but it’s poor when compared to a passenger touchscreen. The video’s contrast was also too high, and as you can see in the photos, the picture was distorted into a narrow height due to the angle of the windshield. Frankly, no one would choose to watch anything on it over a phone, tablet, or conventional passenger screen. It needs to improve by orders of magnitude before becoming a viable alternative.

Again, though, this is just a prototype. Holstein said the holographic film size, or “eyebox,” can be made much bigger and be raised higher than the HUD-like height of the prototype. He also dismissed the distortion as a problem unique to the prototype.

Imagine nearly half the windshield transformed into a virtual screen that’s visible only to the passenger. Beyond videos or other entertainment, Mercedes envisions the holographic display highlighting points of interest in the view through the windshield. A similar display could be placed on the driver side, showing situational information, enabling video calls, or providing entertainment when parked. In the future, it could even let drivers watch things other than the road when Mercedes’ Level 3 autonomous driving system allows them to take their eyes off it. At the very least, the technology could replace today’s HUDs and their larger projector boxes.

Having experienced the prototype while the car was moving, I also think the holographic display could help prevent the motion sickness that can come from staring at a passenger screen while the car is moving. The higher screen angle and ability to see more of the outside world beyond created a notably different visual experience.

Of course, the question remains: “Is this what customers want?”

If the JD Power survey found that customers don’t care about passenger touchscreens, will they be any more enthusiastic about holographic video displays? That seems doubtful. But that’s just as much of an open question as whether the production version will actually live up to its promises.

There’s also another matter: What, exactly, defines luxury? Before Bastian Baudy took over as Mercedes’ chief design officer this year, his predecessor and former boss Gordon Wagener echoed Källenius’ thoughts about the direction of the brand’s interiors. He was considerably blunter, though.

“Screens are not luxury,” Wagener told ABC News. “You have a better—and bigger—TV at home, right? And every car has a big screen. So we have to create luxury beyond the screen. That’s why I talk about craftsmanship and sophistication.”

If the holographic display lets automakers emphasize traditional design and materials while still offering unique technology, it could be a promising way forward.

How Her Love of Vintage Changed Writer Keli Goff’s Life

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Her collection, which has given new life to some of the great Black designers, is now a museum show.

When Keli Goff began purchasing vintage, it was a way to put together stylish looks for her new job within her budget. But over the years, as her interest grew, collecting became a way of connecting to the stories of Black designers unrecognized during their lifetimes. Goff, a writer who has been on the staff of shows including Being Mary Jane and And Just Like That, now owns work from major fashion figures like Patrick Kelly, Isaia Rankin, Stephen Burrows, and Ann Lowe, together making up one of the biggest private collections of work by Black designers. This month, the Houston Museum of African American Culture presents “Collecting in Color: Highlights from the Keli Goff Collection,” which was curated by John Guess and Danielle Finnerman. It’s the first major exhibition focused on her collection, and some of the pieces will be donated to the Bard Graduate Center’s Study Collection.

Goff’s interest in fashion design has intersected with her writing career in interesting ways. The estate of legendary costume designer Edith Head chose Goff to adapt her story for a biopic. “It’s all so surreal. I obviously have admired Edith’s work as long as I can remember, so my collection bringing this project into my life is a dream come true,” she says. Last year, Goff completed studying to become an art appraiser. She now consults in the art and auction sphere in addition to writing.

“People have been fascinated by my collection,” Goff says. “It’s a bit rare to see, frankly, collectors in this space like me. I mean like me in all sorts of ways, not just as an African American woman, but an African American woman who’s not from a privileged background. It’s important to me that I’m not the last one like me in these spaces.”

Goff spoke to ELLE about the joy of working with a museum in her Texas hometown, the old movies that inspired her love of vintage, and the designers she wishes everyone knew.

It really started by watching old movies with my mom and watching her dress up [when I was] a little girl. When I show friends old photos of her, people are always thinking, “Oh, she looks like a movie star or very Diana Ross-esque.” We would watch old movies, especially Imitation of Life and things from that era, the real glamour, the golden age of Hollywood. She would always say, “Look at the dresses, look at the dresses,” and I would. I really fell in love with old movies, which I do believe is part of how I ended up becoming a writer.

But also, I really fell in love with vintage clothes. I moved to New York and didn’t have a lot of money, and vintage was not trendy and didn’t cost thousands of dollars like it can be today. For one of my earliest jobs, I had to go to fancy events, but I had no money. I bought a couple of pieces from the ’60s at a flea market. I remember being at this event, and all these women who obviously had a lot more money to spend on clothes, asking, “Who’s this by? What boutique did you buy this at?” And I was like, “Oh, I got it at a flea market on Avenue B.” I think they thought I was joking.

I wrote a piece for The Daily Beast several years ago about the most influential Black designers in history. It was one of the most memorable pieces I’ve written. I don’t think at the time I was aware of how profoundly it impacted me, but I learned how many seminal Black American designers died of AIDS in a really short window of time. Willi Smith, one of the first Black designers to achieve major crossover commercial success in department stores, died. And so did Isaia Rankin and then Patrick Kelly. What I took away is that so much history was lost, but I also think about how different the future of American fashion might be today because these were really trailblazing and hugely influential.

They weren’t just influential Black American designers, but hugely influential American designers. Isaia Rankin basically had invented athleisure wear. It was his idea that you should be able to go from a coffee shop to a gym to the club. [He made] one of the first pieces by a Black designer I acquired online. I’ll never forget that the person selling it said, “This belonged to my aunt. Apparently, this guy used to be somebody,” which I just think is the saddest thing. He had this profound impact and then had been largely forgotten.

During the pandemic, when everyone worked from home with too much time on their hands and easy access to shop online, I ended up acquiring quite a bit, in part because a lot of the brick-and-mortar stores, particularly in Europe, were really struggling. They were so reliant on tourist foot traffic, and so a lot of them started selling online [for the first time]. And so there were a number of French boutiques I started acquiring pieces from, including some Patrick Kelly pieces that still have the original store tags from the 1980s, which I won’t remove. Those I won’t wear. The rest of them I wear when they fit. As life goes, they don’t always fit forever.

I’ve become friendly with Patrick Kelly’s widower, Bjorn Amelan, who’s a lovely, wonderful man and the keeper of Patrick’s legacy. It is important to me, when possible, to try to maintain relationships with the people I view as keepers of the legacies because I see my collection as serving the legacy of the designers. The pieces don’t belong to me. They belong to our wider community because they’re a part of our lived history.

The really cool thing is how many people I’ve met along this journey who have not only become cheerleaders but actually reach out to me about pieces. Writer Jill Kargman has become a friend and she gifted me a vintage Stephen Burrows dress. She said, “It belongs in your collection,” which is just the coolest thing ever.

I’m from Houston, and we have the Houston Museum of African American Culture here. We’re living in a really interesting time where people are having conversations about the politics of diversity, but one of the things that’s cool about fashion is that people of different political persuasions were like, “I think this collection’s really cool. I read an article about your collection, I ended up ordering a book about Elizabeth Keckley, or I ended up going down a rabbit hole about Ann Lowe.” At this moment, to be able to do an exhibition like this in my hometown felt particularly poignant.

Melanie Lawson has been a legendary anchor in Houston for decades. She was asked what she’s going to do in retirement, and she said that her husband, John Guess Jr., was the CEO of the Houston Museum of African American Culture. I sent him a note about how much Melanie inspired me and then mentioned my collection.

It was just kismet. They’ve actually never done a fashion exhibition. It was a real gamble. But what I so appreciated is that they had the same vision that, in a time in which our world is so divided, it’s really beautiful when you can find pieces of art that people can come together around.

Something Was Hiding in This Ancient Roman Capital. It Took a Drought to Finally Reveal It.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Drones caught the ghostly outlines of a 2,000-year-old city—before returning greenery erased them again.

A heatwave wreaked havoc upon all facets of life in Europe to some degree this past summer, but it also provided archaeologists in Switzerland with an interesting opportunity: the chance to view ancient Roman history in an entirely fresh way. With vegetation stripped back and parched, a team researching Aventicum, a once-thriving Roman city, took to the air via drone for a bird’s-eye view of the entire site. From the new vantage point, they discovered things they simply hadn’t ever known existed.

The site in Switzerland’s state of Vaud, which is now known as Avenches, looked different in the unusually hot and dry months of July and August, allowing archaeologists to “observe remains usually invisible underground,” according to a statement from those working at the Aventicum archaeological site. The airborne exploration revealed previously unseen buried structures, redrawing the map of what was once the capital of Roman Helvetica.

“These are the richest discoveries made there in the last 50 years, at least over the last two generations of archaeologists,” said Jordan Anastassov, archaeologist for Vaud and the University of Geneva, according to Reuters.

The archaeologist said less vigorous plant growth occurs atop buried structures. Combine that with a drought-stricken region and wilting vegetation this past summer, and the archaeologists were able to make a number of “observations that complement the data from the excavations in an exceptional way and show the full potential of surface prospecting methods.”

Aerial images not only showed the locations of houses (making up a full neighborhood), expansive villas, and roads, but also highlighted thermal baths and a potential rectangular temple site running nearly 100 feet in length that was lost to history. Some of the buildings appear in sections of the historic city that haven’t been excavated, and the large building sits on what was once thought to be the town’s outskirts.

Founded originally after Julius Caesar conquered local tribes 2,000 years ago, Aventicum had a successful run under Roman rule, eventually succumbing to Germanic invasions, starting in the third century. “We are now asking really interesting questions we wouldn’t otherwise be asking,” said Hugo Amoroso, an archaeologist on the project.

The team hopes on-the-ground exploration of the newly discovered areas will provide fuller detail and a richer history.

The summer heat proved critical in exposing the areas. By September, as temperatures returned to normal and vegetation started to flourish again, many of the newly seen sites slipped from view, although the pools discovered at the bath location were still visible in a struggling alfalfa field. “We aren’t going to wish for droughts,” said Pierre Blanc, director of the site’s excavations, “but we’re going to make the most of this and keep looking.”

The use of droughts to reveal lost sites isn’t a new concept. Going aerial simply helps cover more ground quickly. A post from the tourism website About Switzerland that Anastassov shared on his LinkedIn page noted that a 1976 drought played a role in the discovery of a Roman villa in the nearby municipality of Orbe.

With droughts increasing throughout the world, archaeologists are turning to aerial monitoring as a planned tactic, scheduling surveys systematically to better understand potential excavation sites and more accurately redraw ancient maps, just like at Aventicum.

A partner’s views on abortion are a dealbreaker for many singles. The Kinsey Institute’s 2025 Singles in America research study found 52 percent of singles outright would not date someone who didn’t support abortion rights, considering it a deal-breaker. But in a world where state lines decide the level of healthcare available, simply sharing the same views on reproductive health with a partner is no longer enough.

Abortion is banned in 13 states, and you can date the most pro-choice person living in one of them without it changing what their local hospital is permitted to do. People in cross-state, long-distance relationships need to consider the laws when making life choices like moving or growing a family.

In a 2024 Institute for Women’s Policy Research poll, nearly 60 percent of parents and people planning to have kids in the next decade said they are worried about access to abortion, OB-GYN services, and prenatal care. Half of adults living in total-abortion ban states said they are concerned about access to fertility treatment. And a 2024 Redfin survey found about three-quarters of Democratic voters want to live somewhere abortion is legal.

The stakes go well beyond individual sentiment. Bans are changing who is even available to provide care at all. In Idaho, for example, after a near-total abortion ban took effect, the state lost more than a third of its practicing obstetricians. In Sandpoint, Idaho, all four of the local hospital's OB-GYNs left and its labor and delivery unit closed, sending pregnant patients more than 40 miles away to the nearest alternative. Nationally, more than 1.7 million women of reproductive age live in a "double desert" with neither abortion access nor maternity care nearby, per that same KFF Health News report.

Fertility care is also at risk. In 2024, after Alabama’s Supreme Court ruled that frozen embryos count as children, at least three IVF providers in the state paused treatment. Lawmakers passed a fix weeks later, but the conversation has continued beyond Alabama.

Where someone lives can shape the reproductive care available to them, which makes it access an essential part of the conversation for anyone dating across state lines, not just whose city has a better job market or cheaper rent. For long-distance couples, deciding where to build a life together needs to involve asking the harder questions: If I needed care for a miscarriage, an ectopic pregnancy, or another complication, could I get it? And will there even be a doctor available to treat me?

Today’s election results won’t answer every question long-distance couples face, but they could help determine what care is available in the states where they build their lives. And for anyone dating across state lines, that makes this Election Day personal.

Novee’s External Surface Testing checks which exposed assets attackers can actually exploit

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Artificial intelligence penetration testing startup Novee Cyber Security Ltd. today added External Surface Testing to its platform so its AI-driven attacks can reach a customer’s entire internet-facing footprint.

External Surface Testing is aimed at the scramble after a critical vulnerability is disclosed, when security teams have to work out which exposed assets are affected and whether any can actually be exploited. Novee argues that attackers see a target as a single environment with many ways in, including forgotten subdomains and staging servers someone left exposed.

Conventional external attack surface management products catalog a company’s exposed assets and score them by severity. Netta Rager Dan, vice president of product at Novee, said those products “show you what’s exposed, but don’t give you the full picture of real risk.” In the company’s telling, security staff are left to validate long lists of exposures on their own.

Novee’s version needs nothing more than a domain name to get started. From there the software works outward the way an outside attacker would, and it keeps track of what it learns about each asset and service as it goes. A newly disclosed vulnerability gets run against a customer’s live systems to see whether anyone could actually exploit it.

Findings exported from other vendors’ attack surface tools go through the same check. Novee says the testing also catches flaws nobody had logged, chained attack paths among them, and the customer ends up with a short, prioritized list of validated issues backed by evidence.

Until now Novee’s testing has stopped at the application layer. External Surface Testing runs on the same platform as the company’s Application Pentesting product, so a host found during external discovery can be promoted straight into a full gray-box or white-box pentest. Whatever the discovery phase learned carries over. Novee claims no other fully autonomous AI pentesting platform covers the external attack surface as well as web, mobile and AI applications.

Limits are built in to keep the testing safe. Customers approve the scope before anything runs, and the probes come from outside the perimeter without agents or customer-supplied credentials. Exploit attempts don’t use destructive payloads. Traffic is held under configured caps so testing doesn’t cause load spikes or bury the security operations center in noise.

The spool slots are numbered in a zigzag method, with 2,4,6 and 8 on the back row and 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 on the front. All the filament feeders are in the middle of the two rows, with the even-numbered spools feeding forward like always, and the odd spools feeding backward. It can get a bit complicated if you’re not using Bambu-branded filament with RFID tags. Being on the short side, I have to use a step stool to see the feeders.

The body of the Bambu Lab H3C looks similar to the rest of the H series except for the toolhead. The Flexfeed extruder can adjust automatically to push multiple filament widths and does well with some of the softest TPU I’ve tried.

The H3C has active chamber heating, up to 65°C, goes to 350°C at the hot end and 120°C on the bed, which makes printing PPA and PPS, nylons, ABS, and ASA easy. For the smellier filaments, it has active carbon and HEPA filtration.

The ventilation is sufficient to run lower-temperature filaments like PLA with the printer’s door closed.

The printer has an excellent camera that is used for AI detection on prints and can be monitored from the Bambu Handy app as well as the device page of Bambu Slicer. The camera can also capture time-lapses of prints when a USB stick is inserted in the slot behind the touch screen.

The Bambu Lab H3C has a footprint similar to the other H series printers but does require space on both sides to access the side panels of the AMS 3H. With the top-mounted AMS and the space required for the tool wiring and Bowden tubes, the Bambu Lab H3C requires a total of 1306 mm in height (over four feet) to operate comfortably. When resting on a standard-height counter or the 3ft tall racks we normally use for our printers, I needed a small ladder to reach the rear spools in the AMS 3H. Even my husband, who is 6’4,” had to stretch to reach the back. Shortly after I had the printer set up, I received the Bambu Station, which, at 23.5 inches tall on its wheels, is a great solution for keeping this printer in reach. If you don’t have a shelf or table the height of a coffee table, I recommend getting one.

Bambu is also introducing a companion E1 Air Purifier that syncs with the H3C and activates when you start a print job. We did not receive an E1 to test, and we’re not sure if the air cleaner will be offered as a standalone product. It is part of the premium $3,049 combo that includes the printer, rolling cart, and several spools of filament.

The Bambu Lab H3C arrives in two large assemblies: the main printer and all the things attached to the AMS 3H. After removing the packing material, zip ties holding the tool head in place, four screws holding the bed in place, and two retainers holding the rack in place, the AMS 3H can be hefted on top of the base unit. This is best done while the unit is on the floor or at least somewhere with a bunch of overhead clearance.

The touch screen attaches to the base unit without tools, and the ribbon cable is just long enough to reach the back of the screen. The nine tools have to be manually placed in the front rack, which takes some time until you learn how they attach. At least one needed to be attached once the printer was powered up.

The AMS 3H has its own power cord for the heater and a cable connecting it to the base. There are preinstalled screws to attach the Base unit to the AMS 3H, but I left these unused since removing the front panel requires tipping the AMS 3H back a bit.

The Bambu Lab H3C performs a bed level as part of its initial calibration suite and again before each print. This works flawlessly in practice.

When using non-TPU 1.75mm filament, the AMS 3H loads filament just like a regular AMS. Filament is inserted into the feeder, which automatically pulls it into the toolhead when that tool is selected. The RFID tag on Bambu Lab branded filament will automatically enter the filament type and color. Other filament brands need to be entered manually into the printer via the touchscreen.

The AMS 3H does not have powered rollers to retract the filament. You will need to select Unload from the filament screen, and the printer will cut the filament and retract it from the tool head. You will need to manually pull the filament out the rest of the way.

2.85mm filament and softer TPU will need to be manually fed through a port on the bottom of the AMS 3H. All filament slots except 2 and 4 have this port. The filament bypasses the tubing to feed directly into the tools. The standard Bowden tube must be removed from the tool and inserted into a holder on the side. For 1.75mm TPU, a small piece of Bowden tube is inserted into the top of the tool to help feed the filament correctly. 2.85mm filament can be inserted directly into the tool. Once inserted, filament using the bypass must be loaded manually.

Bambu Studio, based on PrusaSlicer and its forbear, is one of the best slicers out there. Working seamlessly with the printer for filament mapping, control, monitoring, and access to all of the files on Makerworld, it is a joy to use.

The H3C is a much faster machine than its predecessors because it does not need to reel filament back and forth from the AMS. However, it still uses a purge tower, which can waste a bit of time and filament. It only takes about six seconds to swap tools, which is quite remarkable.

The H3C did not come with filament samples, but Bambu Lab did send along several spools of PLA Palette and spools of 2.85 75A TPU to try. You’re going to need a bunch of filament for this beast, so check out our list of best filaments for 3D printing for suggestions.

To get the measure of the Bambu Lab H3C, I loaded up one of my favorite prints, a steampunk dragon by the designer Cinderwing 3D. I had previously printed it in eight PLA colors using two AMS modules on an Anycubic Kobra 3 Max, which is a bed slinger. That first print took 37 hours and 28 minutes and wasted 267g of filament in poop and purge.

Using a .2mm layer height and an average print speed of 140mm/s, the H3C cut that time down to a bit less than 24 hours, and only wasted 68g in its purge tower. The print quality is excellent, and all the colors came out clean. The main color is Prusa Pearl Mouse, Polymaker Panchroma Silver, and black, white, magenta, yellow, and cyan from Bambu’s new Palette PLA, which is actually designed for color mixing.

Eight colors were not enough. I swapped out the 2.85mm tool with the spare 1.75mm tool and printed a nine-color Princess Donut and Mongo from Dungeon Crawler Carl (my latest obsession). I have been using this print to test out other tool changers for a future comparison. The print came out fantastic, with super shiny silks and clean color transitions. I had to “hand paint” this in Bambu Slicer, which took a bit of time. The teeth on the print are a bit shaky, but that is due to the insane amount of support this model required.

This printed in 15 hours and 12 minutes, with a 152.6-gram prime tower, using a .2mm layer height and default settings. For comparison, the same print with the same settings but only 5 colors on the H2C took 24 hours and 44 minutes and used 111.4grams on its prime tower. The Prusa INDX took a similar amount of time (15 hours and 38 minutes) to run the same print in 8 colors, but only needed to waste 24grams of prime purge.

The Bambu Lab H3C is one of the best printers I’ve tested for printing TPU. The AMS 3H bypass ports require a tiny bit of set up, but they are much better than dealing with an external spool holder. With nine slots at my disposal, I can always have a spool of TPU ready to go.

My first TPU print was a strap hinge that combines normal 95A TPU with stiff PLA brackets. The designer wove the two filaments together manually, though you can make your own hinges flexible by using “beam interlocking” in the slicer.

I printed this in Polymaker red PLA and Polymaker red TPU 95A and the hinge is extremely strong. Printed with a .2mm layer height and running at an average speed of 115 mm/s the print completed in just over an hour.

I was excited to try the 2.85mm 75A TPU that Bambu Lab sent, and it did not disappoint. The 2.85mm nozzle comes preset to run filament directly from the bypass and just requires manual loading. This is the softest TPU I’ve ever run, and the FlexFeed extruder handled it perfectly.

I first ran the wrist rest from the printer’s memory using Bambu Lab’s green 75A TPU. The print took 50 minutes to run, used only 20g of filament, and is very squishy. I used the same filament and printed some tires for an RC project I’m working on. Using a .2mm layer height and an average print speed of 75mm/s the tires took 57 minutes per set of two. They look nice and are super squishy. They only have 2 walls and 7% concentric infill, so they are extremely lightweight.

For ABS, I ran a two-color Magic 8-ball-style D20. Dice Studio lets you print custom dice with numbers or text and export it as a 3mf file ready to print. This is printed in Ionized Cobalt Black Matte ABS and Natural Matte ABS from Fusion Filaments. The print was very nice, with just a little roughness on the overhangs on the very bottom of the die, and a little roughness on the text where it becomes very small.

To really test out the heated chamber, I used a filament I struggle with: PA6 GF. I printed a Twist Lock Container using grey PA6 GF from Sunlu. I ran it with 3 walls and a .2mm layer height at an average speed of 140mm/s. The print took nearly 6 hours to complete. The result is a nearly indestructible canister that looks good too.

The H3C is quite the multimaterial powerhouse and handles every material equally well. I don’t own any filament that it can’t print well, from soft TPU to PPS. Multiple nozzle sizes and multiple filament sizes can all be used together, and you’ve got nine places to put it all. Unless you need a print farm, I can’t really see the need for a different printer. This is a one-stop shop.

With a retail price of $2,499 for the H3C Tool Changer, this is a printer to definitely put on your wish list. In fact, I’m so impressed with the tool changer aspect, I keep forgetting that it’s also a helmet-class printer, which makes this machine capable of handling nearly any print you need to create.

If the Bambu Lab H3C seems out of reach, you can also check out last year’s Bambu Lab H2C, which is no slouch at multimaterial and has the same build volume. It’s currently on sale for $1,899 with a four-color AMS combo. The Snapmaker U1 is the most affordable tool changer we like on the market right now, and though it is smaller and only four-color, the $699 Black Friday price tag is very tempting. And of course, we have the Prusa CORE One INDX eight-color machine for those who want to keep their printers off the cloud, currently retailing at $1,949 as a kit.

The iPad mini 8 is a fantastic tablet thanks to its portable design and beautiful OLED panel. If that wasn’t enough, it delivers near iPad Air levels of performance and battery life.

The new iPad mini 8 features a thinner and lighter design than previous models, making it even more portable and easier to hold. Thanks to that, it was easy for me to read books and comics with one hand for much longer. The sturdy and smooth aluminum chassis also feels fantastic in hand. The iPad mini 8 comes in Midnight and Starlight colors, and both look great.

Another significant redesign is that the 12MP front camera is now positioned for landscape mode. This helps keep you centered during video calls or when taking selfies. The rear 12MP camera remains in the same position as before. The same is true for the power button / Touch ID sensor and volume buttons, which are located on the top when held in portrait orientation.

For the first time, the iPad mini features an IP67 rating that helps protect it against water and dust. So if you enjoy reading by the pool or want protection when working outside on a rainy day, you can do so with less stress.

The iPad mini 8’s upgraded display is a marvel to behold. Thanks to the smaller bezels, the screen is now 8.5 inches across instead of 8.3. It also gets a noticeable resolution bump to 2316 x 1512 pixels with 326 pixels-per-inch (ppi). These upgrades alone would have made this a better display, but Apple sweetened things up with an OLED panel.

Using the same tandem OLED technology as the iPad Pro, the iPad mini’s panel delivers excellent contrast between dark and light elements. As a result, colors practically pop off the screen. When watching a nature video featuring a lava flow, I was stunned by how vivid the reds and oranges were, while also marveling at the detail in the surrounding environment.

The review unit Apple sent features a nano-texture display, which does an excellent job of diminishing screen glare without meaningfully sacrificing an image’s vibrancy. A glossy screen would make everything pop a bit more, but I’d rather have less screen glare, so it’s a fair trade-off since you’re not losing much, at least to my eyes.

How did the iPad mini 8’s display fare in our lab testing? Apple said its small tablet can deliver 600 nits of SDR brightness and hit a peak of 1200 nits of HDR brightness. While the former is certainly true, HDR doesn’t quite make the claim. That said, getting a tad over 1000 nits of brightness is fantastic, and is more than double the brightness of the iPad mini. Trust me, this display gets extremely bright.

Color reproduction (sRGB and DCI-P3) is more saturated than before, which accounts for how rich colors appear. That said, hues don’t get too oversaturated and still maintain a lifelike quality. Color accuracy (Delta-E) is also quite strong.

This is the most powerful iPad mini ever, thanks to its powerful A20 Pro chip—the same 2nm processor found in the iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone 18 Pro Max. The Wi-Fi + Cellular model I tested has a 6-core CPU and 7-core GPU, which gives it a bit more oomph. Thanks to this chip, the iPad mini 8 comes close to matching the performance of the M4-powered iPad Air, which proves this tiny tablet punches above its weight.

Even with dozens of open Chrome tabs and a YouTube video running, the iPad mini 8 keeps going with no noticeable slowdown. If you want to get some work done on this tablet, it can handle it. The same goes for gaming: Resident Evil 4 Remake runs at a solid 30 frames per second.

Our benchmark results confirm my anecdotal testing. As you can see above, the iPad mini 8 blows away its predecessor on the Geekbench CPU benchmarks, turning in significantly higher scores. And while the iPad Air M4 outpaces it, the gap isn’t significantly large.

We see a similar story on the 3DMark benchmark, which tests a device’s GPU performance. The iPad mini 8 not only crushes the iPad mini 7, but it also scores better than the iPad Air M4! Granted, a device’s design plays a role, so it’s not an apples-to-apples comparison (pun intended), but the fact that the iPad mini 8 is even in the same conversation as an iPad Air performance-wise is impressive.

Apple says the iPad mini 8 has up to 10 hours of battery life while surfing the web on Wi-Fi or video playback. However, our testing reveals that the diminutive tablet actually lasts well past that claim.

In the Tom’s Guide battery test, which involves continuous web-surfing over Wi-Fi with the display set to 150 nits of brightness, the iPad mini 8 lasted for an astonishing 14 hours and 24 minutes! That’s nearly four hours longer than the previous model and Apple’s claims.

We typically perform our battery test three times to get an average, but we only had time for a single run for this review. I’ll update this piece after we’ve completed all three runs, but I suspect the final results will be close to what you see above. Basically, this is one of the longest-lasting iPads we’ve ever tested!

The iPad mini 8 ditches a traditional speaker system for what Apple calls an Acoustic Display. As the company explains, specially designed woofers are paired through the display to produce punchier bass and louder volume. Custom tweeters hidden beneath the display and enclosure also help with higher-pitched sounds.

Technical jargon aside, this iPad mini produces fantastic sound that always made my ears sing. Audio from podcasts always sounds sharp and clear, and the same is true for movie trailers. I was particularly impressed when listening to Tommy Johansson’s cover of Black Sabbath’s “Headless Cross,” where the powerful vocals and roaring guitars came through brilliantly.

Though the iPad mini 8 doesn’t get as loud as an iPad Pro, nor does it deliver similarly punchy bass, it’s still one of the best-sounding tablets I’ve ever tested.

Video calls and taking photos are more natural now that the front 12MP Center Stage camera is positioned for landscape orientation. Better yet, the camera now features a wider f/2.0 aperture (up from f/2.4), which helps improve photos taken in dim areas. As you can see above, the front camera does a great job of capturing all the details on my face, clothing, and in the background.

The rear 12MP camera keeps 4K video at up to 60 frames per second, but it also adds Adaptive True Tone flash for taking low-light shots and document scanning. Like the front camera, it does an excellent job of capturing details in photos.

The iPad mini 8 supports iPadOS 27 out of the box. The latest version of the operating system is more iterative than revolutionary, but it delivers strong system refinements and better AI integration, along with a more refined user interface that I quite enjoy.

Arguably the biggest change in iPadOS 27 is the smarter Siri, which now has a dedicated Siri app that works like a standard chatbot. Not only does it understand what you have on the screen, but it can also take actions on your behalf, like creating calendar events. The Liquid Glass UI isn’t as overbearing as before, and you can better fine-tune how it looks.

iPadOS 27 is very AI-centric, so your mileage may vary on how much you’ll use the new Apple Intelligence and Siri AI features. These aren’t things I typically use, but having small things like notification summaries is admittedly useful. Overall, iPadOS 27 is another smooth and stable operating system that works great on the iPad mini 8.

The iPad mini 8 brings some fantastic upgrades, but it’s curiously missing some crucial features.

Face ID is one of my favorite features on Apple, since it makes unlocking devices that support it so much easier. Unfortunately, this is a feature lacking on the iPad mini 8.

It’s a curious omission considering all the upgrades the tablet has already received. I won’t endeavor to guess why Face ID isn’t supported, as there could be any number of reasons. Thankfully, Touch ID is still supported and works as intended.

While the display has received a marvelous upgrade thanks to the OLED panel, larger size, and higher resolution, there is no ProMotion support. Because of that, the refresh rate tops out at 60Hz.

To be fair, that 60Hz refresh rate is extremely smooth, especially for games and videos. However, if you’re used to the 120Hz smoothness of the iPad Pro, then actions like swiping the screen and opening apps will appear choppy in comparison.

That aside, touch controls are as snappy and responsive as ever on the iPad mini. This applies when using your finger and when using accessories like the Apple Pencil Pro. Speaking of the latter, the stylus works marvelously on the iPad mini’s display for taking notes and doodling.

The iPad mini 8 is a phenomenal little tablet that I can’t get enough of using. And that’s saying a lot considering how the iPad Pro is my usual daily driver. I just love how vivid and sharp videos and books look on the tandem OLED panel. Holding the iPad is also nice thanks to its thinner, lighter design.

Even if the iPad mini 8 is now pricier than before, it’s a worthwhile purchase. You won’t find a tablet this small offering the same rich features and delightful experience. It also hints at what’s to come, as I’m sure Apple will incorporate features like Audio Display into future iPads. But for now, this is the most exciting iPad mini ever.
