'Blade Runner 2099' Creator Reveals the Biggest Challenge for Prime Video’s New Sci-Fi Series

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In just a few days, we will be going back to the dystopian future of replicants with a new limited series from Prime Video. Blade Runner 2099 jumps the story forward 50 years and places us in a world where the replicants have finally come out on top and humans have been turned into second-class citizens. The series follows Cora (Hunter Schafer), a human who has gone undercover as a replicant, who works as a blade runner alongside Olwen (Michelle Yeoh), a dying replicant in the last days of her life.

Collider got to speak with creator and showrunner Silka Luisa and director Jonathan van Tulleken about this brand new entry into the sci-fi franchise and how the series differentiates itself from the previous two films in the universe. Van Tulleken reveals the films he drew from as reference for the visual palette of the show, and Luisa reveals the process of what it takes to transfer a story into a new format. Luisa also talks about the big change between replicants and humans and how Cora and Olwen's story gives us insight into the new dynamic between the two groups. Finally, she gives some insight into the show's connection to both Blade Runner and Blade Runner 2049 and why the show decided to lean away from drawing stronger connections to the previous film.

Going into this, what's the most daunting task when it comes to formulating the world of Blade Runner 2099?

JONATHAN VAN TULLEKEN: I think Silka had written on the page this concept of the sunshine noir, and it was beautiful because Ridley [Scott] described his movie as a 40-year-old movie set 40 years in the future. And [Jordan] Cronenweth had looked to Citizen Kane, his cinematographer, and he described Harrison Ford as a Bogart detective story. And I think, for me, when reading what Silka had written, I was going, "Well, what is our touchstone then? What is our 40 years, 40 years ago?" So I was really thinking of those movies from the 70s and 60s, you know, The French Connection by [William] Friedkin and [Alan J.] Pakula's paranoia trilogy.

What makes those films so connected to what Ridley does as well is they've so much tactility; they're based in vérité. It was where cameras were small enough that you could take them out onto the streets. I think that really led into how we wanted to approach the work, which was something that felt very human, in a way that Ridley's feels so human; it's about what it is to be human, and so we wanted to make it feel human. So we built places that were real; we used original old lights; we used the original glass from the films, the lenses. I think that allowed us to build on what Ridley started and what Denis [Villeneuve] started.

Now the replicants are the ones doing the hunting.

SILKA LUISA: I think just from the narrative perspective, obviously, there's a shift from something that is so cinematic and theatrical. It's a really different experience going into a theater and being locked in a room and letting this world wash over you. We knew we were going to be watching this on TV, and so we really had to consider what is the pace of our Blade Runner? There is a certain tempo to both films that is very meditative; I love it, it puts you in a mindset that is that world, so we were always trying to calibrate both in the writing and in the shooting and ultimately in post when we were editing, how do we pace the show?

Can you have it be this much faster heartbeat? Do you also then need to create moments of breath so that you can actually do the score, which is so iconic? Early on in editing, we had cut the episodes really, really tight just to get that propulsion of Cora's story. But we realized we really needed those breaths because without them, it's not really Blade Runner, and so we were always balancing that.

Talking about the show and the specifics of the narrative, I really like that you flip the script on what we know before, making replicants into Blade Runners who hunt humans. But how did switching the narrative of the story change the approach to how you might structure the story with the focus being on the replicants more than on humans, or at least more than in previous stories?

LUISA: Well, I think the biggest shift was that it was an undercover story. I knew because we were doing a TV show, we would have more time, and that meant that there could be a relationship at the center of the show, not just a single character and their journey. And if you want to tell a relationship story, you know, there's been so many great undercover [stories], that Donnie Brasco dynamic felt like that was going to give you a human and a replicant side-by-side, both of them hiding secrets, that would be really rich terrain.

It's interesting that in this show, you're right, you do have more room to develop things, and you have dual protagonists in the form of Cora and Olwen. Can you talk a little bit about how these two characters have a meeting of the minds as the story progresses? Because Olwen is getting older, she's dying, whereas Cora is forced to go undercover and has to be forced to think more like a replicant.

LUISA: Yeah, at the beginning of the season, both of them are really lacking empathy. Olwen because she's a replicant, and that is just something; without childhoods, they don't develop it in the same way. It's one of the defining characteristics between humans and replicants that we know from the films. And Cora is just a human who is just been stripped of her soul, because sometimes you're in these circumstances where you just are, "Go, go, go," and you can't really take a breath to even think about other people because you just are trying to live the next day. [We] wanted to tell a story of two people who really have lost this fundamental part of what the human experience is, and how can they rediscover it together? Or can they? Can a human and a replicant being side-by-side... can that awaken empathy in both of them?

I'm curious because I know this story does have connections to the original film and 2049. So considering the reveal that we get from 2049 about replicants being able to have children, I was always thinking that in the back of my mind. Why didn't you guys lean into that? Are we going to get more of that, or are there hints trickled throughout the show? Because I have thoughts.

I've seen them all, but I don't want to get too deep into spoilers, but I'm curious about your thoughts on that.

LUISA: Well, I definitely wanted to leave 2049 with enough space to tell their story, because what I love about the end of that film is it felt like it left me wanting more. I could imagine different directions for how all those characters' lives continue, and what it meant for the city, what it meant for them individually, which I think is something that Blade Runner always does. In terms of is the focus on replicants being able to have babies? No, because I feel like that is connected to the 2049 story in such a fundamental way that it felt like touching it would be making decisions for how they were to finish that film.

Blade Runner 2099 premieres November 25 on Prime Video.

In a West Texas rock shelter, archaeologists found a hunting kit that was left behind 6,500 years

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Archaeologists in Texas uncovered a roughly 6,500-year-old hunting kit with darts, an atlatl and a rare preserved pronghorn hide.

Deep inside San Esteban Rockshelter, south of Marfa, Texas, archaeologists recovered parts of a hunter’s weapon kit that someone left behind thousands of years ago.

The Center for Big Bend Studies at Sul Ross State University presented the find in its spring 2025 newsletter. The center puts the kit’s age at about 6,500 years and calls it “potentially the oldest example of a full weapon system in North America.”

The kit as a whole has not yet been described in a peer-reviewed study. Only one of its pieces, a spear-thrower known as an atlatl, has been the subject of a journal paper.

The kit includes six stone-tipped dart foreshafts – the front section of a dart – and four self-tipped hardwood dart foreshafts

There are also four dart nock-ends, the rear tips that fit against the atlatl. A broken hunting boomerang and a partial atlatl complete the weapons.

The excavators also found a folded pronghorn hide. It had been tanned, and its hair was still intact.

Spaced holes ran around the hide’s perimeter so it could be tied to a frame, as Plains groups often did.

“We just sat there and stared at it in wonder,” said Bryon Schroeder, then director of the center. Someone had folded the hide and set it on top of a rock, he explained.

“And nobody touched it for 6,000 years,” Schroeder said.

An atlatl is essentially a wooden stick with a handle on one end and a hook or socket on the other. It acts as an extension of the human arm, increasing leverage against the dart, the National Park Service explains.

The San Esteban atlatl is described in a 2023 atlatl paper by Schroeder and Devin Pettigrew, an assistant professor at the center.

A radiocarbon date indicates that the atlatl is more than 6,000 years old. That made it the oldest directly dated atlatl in North America at the time, the authors reported in the Journal of Big Bend Studies.

In a paper published in 2026, Pettigrew and other archaeologists at the center measured how fast modern throwers launch darts.

That research shows that heavier darts, paired with strong and skilled throwers, become lethal to very large animals such as mammoths, the center said.

“In most archeological sites in Texas, only things made of stone survive,” notes Texas Beyond History, a project of the University of Texas at Austin. The dry Lower Pecos rock shelters are an exception.

Those shelters have yielded hardwood dart foreshafts and rabbit sticks, as well as bow fragments and arrow foreshafts from the bow-and-arrow era. Complete atlatls are very rare even there.

Unique depositional conditions favored the preservation of the Schöningen wooden spears in Germany. A 2025 dating study placed them at about 200,000 years old.

A mammoth-ivory boomerang from Poland is one of the oldest known in Europe, an Obłazowa Cave analysis concluded. A statistical model built on dated bones from the same layer places it between 42,290 and 39,280 years ago.

“We can use the wood to reconstruct the environment and learn more about the amount of time they spent working on tools,” Schroeder said.

Schroeder’s reading of the scene is that a person came to the back of the cave and sorted through the gear piece by piece. Some parts were judged good and others not, and then the person went on their way.

“But that one small act is going to have profound implications in understanding the environment,” he said.

The shelter sits in Presidio County along Alamito Creek, a tributary of the Rio Grande. A spring-fed rock pool, or tinaja, lies in front of it.

The center has worked there since 2019 with the Odyssey Archaeological Research Fund at the University of Kansas. The partners were looking for traces of the region’s earliest people.

The Odyssey fund was established in 2003 with an endowment from Joseph and Ruth Cramer. Its director is Rolfe Mandel, a distinguished professor of anthropology at the university.

In 2020, the excavators uncovered a hearth-like feature with burned and unburned bird eggshells dated to about 15,200 years ago.

“Though this site was pot-hunted in the past, the many large boulders that spalled from the ceiling preserved pockets of shelter deposits,” the Kansas team’s site summary states. Pot-hunting means digging for artifacts to collect.

The atlatl foreshafts and darts came from intact deposits in an area the excavators call the Sandal Shaft. The Kansas team dates them to about 6,200 years ago, a little younger than the center’s figure.

The pronghorn hide lay about 2 inches (5 centimeters) above them, capped with a metate, a grinding stone.

The hide yielded a raw radiocarbon age of 5,621 years, plus or minus 24, not yet converted to calendar years. That makes it about 600 years younger than some of the foreshafts and darts, the Kansas researchers report.

The information was obtained from the Center for Big Bend Studies.

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Right from the moment Peacock's Dig was announced, comparisons to Parks and Recreation felt inevitable.

The new sitcom reunites Parks and Recreation's co-creator Mike Schur with the show's leading lady, Amy Poehler. As co-creators on Dig, the two once more deliver a workplace comedy about a competent, passionate leader, played by Poehler, who assembles a motley team. In Dig's case, that's archaeologist Elise, who's overseeing a Grecian archaeological dig site.

Comparison to a prior project is territory Schur and Poehler know well, dating back to the first days of Parks and Recreation.

"When we started Parks and Rec, we were compared to The Office, because The Office was such a big deal at the time," Schur told Mashable over Zoom. "That was sort of our mini obstacle we had to get through."

Both shows were workplace-set mockumentaries on NBC. Critics and viewers alike were quick to call out the similarities.

While talking with Steve Carell for an episode of Good Hang earlier this year, Poehler recalled, "Parks and Rec had the worst launch ever. Everyone was like, 'This is not The Office. We don't like this.'"

However, Parks and Recreation ditched Office-like cynicism in favor of a warmer tone, and it carved out a niche of its own. Still, for Schur, comparisons to The Office prepared him for how people might react to his later work. That includes Dig.

"[With Dig,] we're being compared to a different show that we also made," Schur said. "We're experienced with the concept that people have expectations for something going in. And we're a little bit older and wiser in terms of how we manage that emotionally."

While some might say that comparison is the thief of joy, Schur has found the upside.

"I think it's a position of privilege to be in, to have a show that people are like, 'I hope it's like this other thing you did,'" Schur said. "That's great, because that means people watched the first one and cared about it. So I hope that people will watch this new show and judge it on its own merits."

I don't think the "Whoooaaaaaaa, dude!" award counts as a Game of the Year nod

Valve has opened nominations for the 2026 Steam awards, and for this 10th iteration of the annual event, players are being asked to choose a special winner. The Hall of Fame award will go to one of the ten previous Steam Game of the Year award winners, but there's one problem: Steam didn't have a GOTY category for the first two years of these awards.

That means that the "winners" in 2016 and 2017, Grand Theft Auto 5 and The Witcher 3, were never actually voted on by fans as GOTY-caliber stars. Certainly, both of those games were widely acclaimed when they launched – but The Witcher 3 and the PC version of GTA 5 were both released in 2015, before the Steam awards even existed.

Going back to the first round, the Steam awards offered an array of goofball categories with silly names that nonetheless honored great games. In 2016, GTA 5 did win an award titled "Whoooaaaaaaa, dude!" for games that "melt your face," and in 2017, The Witcher 3 won the "Choices Matter" award for games with a great deal of player agency.

As Valve explains in an FAQ alongside today's nomination announcement, the team used these two awards as a "proxy" to decide on the 2016 and 2017 GOTY picks. But I've gotta ask, why did 2017 not just use "Whoooaaaaaaa, Dude! 2.0," won by The Evil Within 2, for its GOTY? Or the "Even Better Than I Expected" award, explicitly built for games released in 2017 and won by Cuphead?

In fairness, it is fun seeing all-time heavy hitters like GTA 5 and Witcher 3 going up against similar heavyweights like Elden Ring, Baldur's Gate 3, and Hollow Knight: Silksong. And ultimately, none of this matters – as with any set of game awards, it's supposed to simply be an opportunity to celebrate excellent games. But, when did we ever let an award show go by without using it instead as a chance to get mad about the nominees?
