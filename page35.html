In a West Texas rock shelter, archaeologists found a hunting kit that was left behind 6,500 years

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Archaeologists in Texas uncovered a roughly 6,500-year-old hunting kit with darts, an atlatl and a rare preserved pronghorn hide.

Deep inside San Esteban Rockshelter, south of Marfa, Texas, archaeologists recovered parts of a hunter’s weapon kit that someone left behind thousands of years ago.

The Center for Big Bend Studies at Sul Ross State University presented the find in its spring 2025 newsletter. The center puts the kit’s age at about 6,500 years and calls it “potentially the oldest example of a full weapon system in North America.”

The kit as a whole has not yet been described in a peer-reviewed study. Only one of its pieces, a spear-thrower known as an atlatl, has been the subject of a journal paper.

The kit includes six stone-tipped dart foreshafts – the front section of a dart – and four self-tipped hardwood dart foreshafts

There are also four dart nock-ends, the rear tips that fit against the atlatl. A broken hunting boomerang and a partial atlatl complete the weapons.

The excavators also found a folded pronghorn hide. It had been tanned, and its hair was still intact.

Spaced holes ran around the hide’s perimeter so it could be tied to a frame, as Plains groups often did.

“We just sat there and stared at it in wonder,” said Bryon Schroeder, then director of the center. Someone had folded the hide and set it on top of a rock, he explained.

“And nobody touched it for 6,000 years,” Schroeder said.

An atlatl is essentially a wooden stick with a handle on one end and a hook or socket on the other. It acts as an extension of the human arm, increasing leverage against the dart, the National Park Service explains.

The San Esteban atlatl is described in a 2023 atlatl paper by Schroeder and Devin Pettigrew, an assistant professor at the center.

A radiocarbon date indicates that the atlatl is more than 6,000 years old. That made it the oldest directly dated atlatl in North America at the time, the authors reported in the Journal of Big Bend Studies.

In a paper published in 2026, Pettigrew and other archaeologists at the center measured how fast modern throwers launch darts.

That research shows that heavier darts, paired with strong and skilled throwers, become lethal to very large animals such as mammoths, the center said.

“In most archeological sites in Texas, only things made of stone survive,” notes Texas Beyond History, a project of the University of Texas at Austin. The dry Lower Pecos rock shelters are an exception.

Those shelters have yielded hardwood dart foreshafts and rabbit sticks, as well as bow fragments and arrow foreshafts from the bow-and-arrow era. Complete atlatls are very rare even there.

Unique depositional conditions favored the preservation of the Schöningen wooden spears in Germany. A 2025 dating study placed them at about 200,000 years old.

A mammoth-ivory boomerang from Poland is one of the oldest known in Europe, an Obłazowa Cave analysis concluded. A statistical model built on dated bones from the same layer places it between 42,290 and 39,280 years ago.

“We can use the wood to reconstruct the environment and learn more about the amount of time they spent working on tools,” Schroeder said.

Schroeder’s reading of the scene is that a person came to the back of the cave and sorted through the gear piece by piece. Some parts were judged good and others not, and then the person went on their way.

“But that one small act is going to have profound implications in understanding the environment,” he said.

The shelter sits in Presidio County along Alamito Creek, a tributary of the Rio Grande. A spring-fed rock pool, or tinaja, lies in front of it.

The center has worked there since 2019 with the Odyssey Archaeological Research Fund at the University of Kansas. The partners were looking for traces of the region’s earliest people.

The Odyssey fund was established in 2003 with an endowment from Joseph and Ruth Cramer. Its director is Rolfe Mandel, a distinguished professor of anthropology at the university.

In 2020, the excavators uncovered a hearth-like feature with burned and unburned bird eggshells dated to about 15,200 years ago.

“Though this site was pot-hunted in the past, the many large boulders that spalled from the ceiling preserved pockets of shelter deposits,” the Kansas team’s site summary states. Pot-hunting means digging for artifacts to collect.

The atlatl foreshafts and darts came from intact deposits in an area the excavators call the Sandal Shaft. The Kansas team dates them to about 6,200 years ago, a little younger than the center’s figure.

The pronghorn hide lay about 2 inches (5 centimeters) above them, capped with a metate, a grinding stone.

The hide yielded a raw radiocarbon age of 5,621 years, plus or minus 24, not yet converted to calendar years. That makes it about 600 years younger than some of the foreshafts and darts, the Kansas researchers report.

The information was obtained from the Center for Big Bend Studies.

Dig pulls off a perfect Parks and Rec reunion

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Peacock's Dig is just one Parks and Recreation reunion after another.

The archaeological comedy is co-created by Amy Poehler and Mike Schur, who both worked together on Parks and Recreation. Schur co-created that series with Greg Daniels, and Poehler led the show as waffle-loving civil servant Leslie Knope.

However, Poehler and Schur's collaboration isn't the only spark of Parks and Recreation magic you'll find in Dig. In a blink-and-you'll-miss-it moment, Dig also features a cameo from none other than Rashida Jones, who plays Leslie's best friend, nurse Ann Perkins, in Parks and Recreation.

Jones' appearance occurs in episode 5, when she pops up as a woman taking a photo of archaeologists Elise (Poehler) and Neville (Hugh Laurie) at a beachside restaurant. My Parks-loving self has never rewound a scene so fast to make sure my eyes weren't deceiving me.

According to Schur, the cameo came about due to proximity. Dig shot in Italy and Greece, and Jones is semi-permanently located in London. Schur, Poehler, and Jones, who have been friends since even before working together on Parks and Recreation, were communicating with one another during production.

"We were just texting with [Jones], and we were like, 'It's not that long a flight. What if you just hopped over here and hung out with us at this hotel we're staying at?'" Schur told Mashable over Zoom. "Then once she was there, it was a very quick jump to, 'You've got to just go into the show. You've got to pop in for a scene.'"

From there, Schur, Poehler, and Jones came up with the quick photo cameo.

"That's part of the fun of knowing people and working together for a really long time, is you do silly things like that," Schur recalled. "It was really fun, and she was totally game for it."

The highlight of the scene comes right after Jones exits, when Elise casually remarks, "She's very pretty."

For Parks and Recreation fans, the compliment instantly recalls Leslie's non-stop raves over Ann's beauty, whether it's calling her a "beautiful tropical fish" or a "poetic, noble land-mermaid." It's a sweet reminder of one of TV's most enduring female friendships, and it's right at home in Dig, which is full of sweet female friendships in its own right. Plus, according to Schur, the remark was completely improvised by Poehler.

"It was a very smart little mini gift to the folks who were fans of that show who will enjoy seeing them back together again," Schur said.

What does Dig co-creator Mike Schur think of Parks and Recreation comparisons?

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Right from the moment Peacock's Dig was announced, comparisons to Parks and Recreation felt inevitable.

The new sitcom reunites Parks and Recreation's co-creator Mike Schur with the show's leading lady, Amy Poehler. As co-creators on Dig, the two once more deliver a workplace comedy about a competent, passionate leader, played by Poehler, who assembles a motley team. In Dig's case, that's archaeologist Elise, who's overseeing a Grecian archaeological dig site.

Comparison to a prior project is territory Schur and Poehler know well, dating back to the first days of Parks and Recreation.

"When we started Parks and Rec, we were compared to The Office, because The Office was such a big deal at the time," Schur told Mashable over Zoom. "That was sort of our mini obstacle we had to get through."

Both shows were workplace-set mockumentaries on NBC. Critics and viewers alike were quick to call out the similarities.

While talking with Steve Carell for an episode of Good Hang earlier this year, Poehler recalled, "Parks and Rec had the worst launch ever. Everyone was like, 'This is not The Office. We don't like this.'"

However, Parks and Recreation ditched Office-like cynicism in favor of a warmer tone, and it carved out a niche of its own. Still, for Schur, comparisons to The Office prepared him for how people might react to his later work. That includes Dig.

"[With Dig,] we're being compared to a different show that we also made," Schur said. "We're experienced with the concept that people have expectations for something going in. And we're a little bit older and wiser in terms of how we manage that emotionally."

While some might say that comparison is the thief of joy, Schur has found the upside.

"I think it's a position of privilege to be in, to have a show that people are like, 'I hope it's like this other thing you did,'" Schur said. "That's great, because that means people watched the first one and cared about it. So I hope that people will watch this new show and judge it on its own merits."

I don't think the "Whoooaaaaaaa, dude!" award counts as a Game of the Year nod

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

Valve has opened nominations for the 2026 Steam awards, and for this 10th iteration of the annual event, players are being asked to choose a special winner. The Hall of Fame award will go to one of the ten previous Steam Game of the Year award winners, but there's one problem: Steam didn't have a GOTY category for the first two years of these awards.

That means that the "winners" in 2016 and 2017, Grand Theft Auto 5 and The Witcher 3, were never actually voted on by fans as GOTY-caliber stars. Certainly, both of those games were widely acclaimed when they launched – but The Witcher 3 and the PC version of GTA 5 were both released in 2015, before the Steam awards even existed.

Going back to the first round, the Steam awards offered an array of goofball categories with silly names that nonetheless honored great games. In 2016, GTA 5 did win an award titled "Whoooaaaaaaa, dude!" for games that "melt your face," and in 2017, The Witcher 3 won the "Choices Matter" award for games with a great deal of player agency.

As Valve explains in an FAQ alongside today's nomination announcement, the team used these two awards as a "proxy" to decide on the 2016 and 2017 GOTY picks. But I've gotta ask, why did 2017 not just use "Whoooaaaaaaa, Dude! 2.0," won by The Evil Within 2, for its GOTY? Or the "Even Better Than I Expected" award, explicitly built for games released in 2017 and won by Cuphead?

In fairness, it is fun seeing all-time heavy hitters like GTA 5 and Witcher 3 going up against similar heavyweights like Elden Ring, Baldur's Gate 3, and Hollow Knight: Silksong. And ultimately, none of this matters – as with any set of game awards, it's supposed to simply be an opportunity to celebrate excellent games. But, when did we ever let an award show go by without using it instead as a chance to get mad about the nominees?

The 5 Most Unwatchable James Bond Movies, Ranked

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The James Bond franchise has long stood as one of cinema’s most defining blockbuster titles. From the outset, way back when it started with Sean Connery in the lead role in 1962’s Dr. No, it has epitomized the suave spectacle and sophisticated charm of high-octane spy thrills. Ranging from Goldfinger to Skyfall, the saga has delivered some of the most exhilarating spy-action movies ever made, but any franchise that features 25 movies is bound to have a few misfires and, unfortunately, 007 is no different.

When the franchise is at its worst, it reduces its tropes to being crude caricatures of the genre. The gadgets become goofy, the Bond girl an affront to any form of progressive femininity, and the story often suffers most under the strain of forced stylistic choices, laughably cartoonish action sequences, and a sense of antagonism that is utterly ridiculous. Ranging from early errs that were painfully anachronistic even when they were released to more modern missteps, these blundering Bond movies represent cinema’s greatest franchise at its unwatchable worst.

Released 12 years after Connery's last Bond outing—1971’s Diamonds Are Forever—and marking just the second and latest non-Eon-produced Bond movie ever made, Never Say Never Again emerged amid a time of chaos and calamity for the franchise. Roger Moore’s ongoing tenure as the official 007 had begun to falter, and while his worst movies mark some of the franchise’s lowest points, they are made watchable through a sense of characterful campiness and, in some cases, a certain so-bad-it’s-good appeal. Neither of those attributes can be applied to Never Say Never Again, which sees a 53-year-old Connery return to the role to deliver what is effectively a tame rehash of Thunderball.

The lack of originality in the picture is only worsened by the fundamental Bond staples it was deprived of. Given it wasn’t an Eon Productions project, it never had access to the iconic Bond theme, the opening gun-barrel shot, or John Barry’s signature score. Without these qualities, the film lands with a certain flatness. It lacks authentic action-thriller energy, and its efforts to at least modernize the character by implementing ‘80s trends like arcade-style dueling only make it all the more outdated today. It isn’t the worst or most egregious Bond movie ever made, but it is among the most banal, and that pervasive sense of muted mediocrity makes it an unwatchable snoozefest in 2026.

Quantum of Solace is as bland and banal a Bond movie as has ever been made. The follow-up to Daniel Craig’s dashing debut in Casino Royale undoubtedly fell victim to the 2007/08 writer’s strike in Hollywood, making it indistinguishable from any run-of-the-mill, B-grade spy thriller. It lacks the ruthless hard-edged grunt of its predecessor, the snappy stylistic flourishes of its successor, and refuses to implement anything in the way of camp charm or thematic prowess. Even the villain’s environmental slant is ill-explored, making him an odd and forgettable concoction of timely ideals and traditional egomaniacal Bond villainy.

It is ultimately a Bond movie that lacks an identity. It doesn’t strive to be anything unique, refusing to stretch beyond the safe limitations of the franchise at its most middling and mediocre. To say it is an outright terrible film would perhaps be going too far, it just stands as a flick that has no aspiration of what it wants to be. Again, the writer’s strike of the time was definitely a limiting factor on what Quantum of Solace could achieve, but for a Bond movie to be plain boring is unforgivable. Even many of the worst installments of the franchise have their goofy eccentricities that at least give them something, but Quantum of Solace lacks anything memorable, and it derides viewers of any reason to want to rewatch it.

Most, if not all, of the original Connery Bond films have elements that haven’t aged all too well. In the best of them, it’s easily dismissed as a sign of the times, but in the lesser entries of his tenure as 007, it’s a bit more grating to watch. His fifth outing, You Only Live Twice, is the epitome of this notion. It lacks the pizzazz and pop of Connery’s earlier, more iconic movies, and it seems as though Connery himself knows it more than anyone. His performance is uncharacteristically wooden, a stilted cutout of a character where his charisma and quick-wit ought to be.

Bereft of Connery’s best work, the film struggles to apply wallpaper over the narrative cracks and biting insensitivities. Roald Dahl’s screenplay leans heavily on racial stereotypes—an idea that reaches an ugly crescendo with Bond’s infamous “yellowface” disguise as a Japanese man—and his endeavor to up the ante comes at an emotional cost. The movie is a big, drab blockbuster with no feeling, powered by senseless villainy and over-the-top theatrics. Upon release, You Only Live Once was a limp entry to the franchise, but today it is a bitterly hard-to-watch mishit that serves as a painstaking procession of every nasty trope and tendency the original Bond pictures had.

2006’s Casino Royale is a true masterpiece of action cinema, slick sophistication, and gripping high-stakes drama that finds the perfect balance between exhilarating set piece and character-charged intensity. The 1967 interpretation of the Bond story, however, is not. The unlicensed Bond adaptation follows a retired 007 (David Niven) as he is forced to re-enter the field to bring down the evil international crime faction SMERSH, which has been behind a recent series of deadly attacks on MI6 operatives. In an effort to mislead the criminal syndicate, several others also pose as agent 007, including baccarat master Evelyn Tremble (Peter Sellers) and Bond’s own former secretary, Miss Moneypenny (Barbara Bouchet).

The idea of lampooning the spy genre was good in theory, but the execution of 1967’s Casino Royale leaves much to be desired. Its story is a chaotic mess that is completely incomprehensible and never comes close to filling the film’s bloated 131-minute runtime, while on-set conflicts saw the movie pulled in several different directions, making the final product intangible. Even a supporting cast including Orson Welles, Woody Allan, and original Bond girl Ursula Andress are incapable of elevating the film beyond its pitiful nature. It was admonished by the masses upon release, and it has only gotten worse over the years.

The Bond franchise has had its fair share of mishits over the decades, but none have been quite so disastrous and agonizing as 2002’s Die Another Day. It marked an unfortunate end to Pierce Brosnan’s tenure as 007, which started out so well with GoldenEye giving a clear intent of suave style and gritty action. Three films later, the Bond saga had regressed to uninspiring uncertainty. Die Another Day is an awkward, disjointed conglomerate of the litany of Bond styles that came before it, and every tonal shift and plot twist it aspires for falls flat on its face.

The opening sequence has a certain gruffness to it, with Bond being tortured by the North Korean army over the course of 14 months, but whatever intrigue that hard-edged intro may have had immediately dissipates as the movie pivots to cringe-worthy campiness. Not only is Die Another Day’s narrative tempo off beat, its reliance on appalling CGI and ridiculous concepts to carry some of its action sequences is enough to leave viewers in hysterics. It marks an undisputed low-point for the franchise, a painful depiction of 007 at its absolute worst. The only saving grace of the movie is that it was so bad it forced producers to take an entirely new approach four years later when the saga was re-ignited with Casino Royale.

Graphite flagged 55% of the users who gave an opinion of an AEO tool across 54 subreddits this year as likely working for or promoting a marketing software product.

Among the users it didn’t flag, the accuracy of visibility scores drew more unfavorable opinions than any other product feature.

The growth agency picked subreddits where AEO comes up regularly and found 5,990 posts and 25,392 comments from January through September that mention a tool. OpenAI language models labeled the mentions, the opinions and the likely promoters.

The agency helps companies with AEO and SEO and says it doesn’t sell AEO tools or accept payment to be mentioned. Its CEO, Ethan Smith, is an investor in Peec AI, one of three tools tied for the highest score in the analysis.

Graphite flagged 1,298 of the 2,365 Reddit users who gave an opinion of an AEO tool or a marketing platform’s AEO features.

A user was flagged when the agency’s model put the chance at 50% or more that their posts promoted a marketing product they build, own, work for, sell or partner with. That includes SEO, AEO, sales and web analytics tools. An agency’s description of its own client work did not constitute promotion.

To test the model, the company manually inspected 43 of the users it had flagged. Ultimately, 12% of them disclosed being promoters, while 62% were found to have promoted a product but failed to identify themselves as such; the remaining ones were judged non-promoters. The write-up also says that some of the models’ labeling decisions could be incorrect.

Four types of likely promoter Reddit accounts were identified. Each type exhibited a pattern across several posts or comments.

One account was used to recommend the same lesser-known tracking service on eight posts and comments. In January this user claimed that they switched from a competitor, and 11 days later they commented about possibly purchasing the product.

Another account made reference to a little-known product in four comments with well-known tracking services over the course of eight days.

A third account posted three separate times for the same product within 12 minutes.

Over six weeks, 38 accounts recommended the same product in 52 comments, each using the phrase “from what I’ve read” in its recommendation. That phrase appears with any other tool no more than twice.

Of the opinions regarding the accuracy of AEO tools from users who weren’t flagged, 143 were negative while 27 had positive views. No other feature elicited as many negative opinions. These users provided opinions on 44 tools, for a total of 1,557 opinions.

Graphite’s chart compares praise and criticism across features after excluding opinions from users it flagged as likely promoters.

According to the write-up, users frequently view visibility scores as estimates due to variation in AI answers among different runs, locations and users. Several have advised fellow users to “treat them as trend, not truth” and to verify the information through manual review.

The citation data, or the citations used by AI systems as source material for their answers, was generally viewed positively by users. Price received more user opinions than any other attribute and most of those opinions were negative, at 182 negative versus 98 positive.

The agency also evaluated the 11 tools which generated sufficient opinions using a 0-100 point scale; a score of 50 represented neutral. Each of the 11 tools scored between 54 and 66 points.
