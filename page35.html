How Yoga Brings You Closer to Yourself and the Life You Want

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

If you buy through our links, we may earn an affiliate commission. This supports our mission to get more people active and outside.Learn about Outside Online's affiliate link policy

In Yoga Journal’s Archives series, we share a curated collection of articles originally published in past issues beginning in 1975. This article about yoga as a science and an art first appeared in the January-February 1977 issue of Yoga Journal. Find more of our Archives here.

Yoga is a science, an art, and a technique which spans the multilevel functioning of the individual. It is a science because of its empirical approach, presented most completely by the classic Yoga Sutras of Patanjali at least 1,000 years ago. Science is defined as a methodology which requires empirical proof of its theories; it requires that which proves a theory be easily repeatable by another experimenter if he/she follows the rules of the experiment.

Yoga, by this definition, is indeed a science. Its rules are given in sutra form and the presentation of Patanjali is a “how-to” book of the highest order. The practitioner need only follow the lead of Patanjali in order to have his own personal empirical results. (It is true that the directions are in parts esoteric and apparently illusive; this points out the necessity of having a teacher upon whom the practioner can rely for advice during certain intervals.) In addition to the work by Patanjali, the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and the Gheranda and Shiva Samhita are references which can explain the science of yoga.

But yoga is an art, too. Individuals have dedicated their lives to the perfection of this art. This does not necessarily imply the production of tangible output such as texts, although these have been produced in the history of traditional Indian thought. Rather, the use of the word “art” in this context implies the movement toward perfection within, the embodiment of beauty as it can be expressed through the human being. If one dedicates his/her life to the art of yoga, this implies a dedication which does not involve merely daily practice numbering a few hours. Instead, it implies a minute by minute practice which spans years, and some believe, lifetimes of existence.

The art of yoga is the practice of yoga on all levels of existence; this means every thought, action, dream, relationship, food intake, sexual activity, occupation, etc., are brought into the wider perspective of observation in light of the individual’s desire to understand the Self. This understanding does not imply intellectual understanding alone. It implies the “living” or the “being” in the Self rather than just mentally knowing about the Self. One can compare this to the experience of being in a hurricane with merely reading about the experience of a hurricane; the effect would be quite different.

One who lives in the Self has come into a wholeness of being, possibly for us all by virtue of our human existence, but available presently to only a handful due a complex interplay of events which, for whatever reasons, has brought them to a newer threshold of consciousness. Thus the art of yoga lies in bringing perfection into action through becoming one with oneself.

Part of the ability to do this lies with the powerful psychological technique of “acting as if.” If one wishes to meditate, one often begins by acting as if one were meditating. As the form of meditation is assumed, the content of meditation begins to be experienced. If one acts as if one loves another, and acts with sincerity and with honesty, often feelings of love toward that person do begin to flow. So with yoga. If one watches and practices as if yoga were an art and the Self were the artist, then this lends validity to the possibility of this being true.

This brings one to the question of technique. Yoga is also a concrete way of bringing about profound physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual changes within the individual. The student is instructed in yama and niyama (basic ethical precepts), in asanas (meditative postures), in pranayama (conscious voluntary control of the energy associated with breath), in pratyhara and dharana (watching and observing the mind), and finally, in dhyana, or meditation. The final experiencing of samadhi (union) cannot be taught; it is both the result of the foregoing disciplines and independent of them. The understanding of this apparent paradox is part of the process of learning about yoga.

Thus yoga can be seen from the angles of science, art or technique. It can be approached as a small practice of one’s life, or as the way that life is lived, a quality which is brought to live which liberates one from the strong bonds of ego.

Klopp settles Germany's goalkeeping debate ahead of November fixtures

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Germany head coach Jürgen Klopp named three goalkeepers in his squad for the upcoming UEFA Nations League fixtures against Serbia and the Netherlands, which was announced on Thursday: Marc-André ter Stegen (Ajax), Jonas Urbig (Bayern Munich) and Alexander Nübel (Besiktas).

After rotating between the three in his first four matches in charge last month, Klopp has now confirmed that ter Stegen remains Germany's clear first-choice goalkeeper.

“Marc is having a fantastic season in Amsterdam, and fortunately, the other two [Urbig and Nübel] are also having really good seasons. We're going into these matches with Marc as our number one,” Klopp said in an interview with DFB.tv (via iMiaSanMia).

"And this isn't about who happens to be making the better saves at any given moment, because goalkeepers need to provide a lot of stability and confidence. The number one requirement for a goalkeeper is to make sure the ball doesn't end up in the net. That's the most important thing. But beyond that, there are several other aspects that matter: How can he dictate our game, including when we're in possession? How does he handle these situations? And when we're defending with a high line, how early can he recognise those situations so that he can defend further up the pitch as a goalkeeper?

"It's a strange way of putting it, but essentially, it's about knowing when to come out and deal with the danger – not trying to dribble, but simply clearing the ball. All of these things are part of the job. Marc has the most experience in this area, and, yes, we're going into these matches with him as our number one."

Germany face Serbia away from home on November 13 before hosting the Netherlands three days later.

Bitcoin Mining Giant MARA Sparks Selling Concerns With $133 Million Transfer

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bitcoin mining behemoth MARA Holdings has again raised concerns over possible selling pressure on Bitcoin after it transferred around $133 million worth of BTC to multiple institutional crypto platforms.

According to data from blockchain analytics firm Lookonchain, MARA moved a total of 1,650 BTC (around $132.8 million) to platforms like Galaxy Digital, Copper, and others in the past 12 hours.

The latest transactions, which were first reported on Nov. 5, came a few weeks after a series of massive Bitcoin movements involving the publicly listed mining company.

It seems MARA is once again dumping Bitcoin, referencing recent transfers of massive amounts of BTC to trading platforms and custodians.

Nevertheless, it is possible that the transfers do not actually indicate that MARA has sold BTC.

It's worth noting that platforms like Galaxy Digital and Copper offer different services, including trading and custody, for institutional clients.

So it could be that MARA moved the assets for liquidity, collateral, or other purposes.

It's worth mentioning that the latest BTC movements come after a few similar transactions involving MARA throughout October.

On Oct. 9, MARA moved around 996 BTC ($81.13 million at that time) to a wallet linked to crypto platform Galaxy Digital.

The transaction prompted some speculation about possible BTC selling, though it didn't confirm whether Galaxy Digital actually liquidated the assets or not.

On Oct. 19, Lookonchain again identified some other transactions involving wallets linked to the Bitcoin miner.

As per the analytics firm's data, the wallets linked to the Bitcoin miner sent 1,000 BTC (approximately $82.8 million) to crypto exchanges like Binance.

Towards the end of October, concerns over possible selling increased.

Specifically, on Oct. 27, MARA moved another 1,000 BTC (around $80.19 million) to institutional crypto custody platform BitGo.

The following day, Lookonchain noted another transaction of 1,000 BTC, worth about $80.66 million, to BitGo.

As per its second-quarter financial disclosure, MARA sold around 23,093 BTC for $1.6 billion in the first half of 2026.

In the second quarter alone, MARA sold 2,213 BTC at an average price of $73,078.

Meanwhile, MARA has also made it clear that it is no longer interested in holding all the Bitcoin it mines.

Cristiano Ronaldo retires from international football

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ronaldo calls time on his career with Portugal as international football’s all-time top scorer after a dispute with his country’s federation

Want to bookmark your favourite articles and stories to read or reference later? Start your Independent Membership today.

Cristiano Ronaldo has retired from international football.

Ronaldo, 41, calls time on his career with Portugal after a legendary 23-year stint, scoring 146 goals in 234 games to become international football’s all-time top scorer and appearance maker.

He captained his nation to their first ever major title at Euro 2016, while also lifting two Nations League titles in 2019 and 2025.

“The day has come back for me to hand back the number 7 shirt of our national team,” he said in an announcement on social video. “I never felt it belonged to me alone. But for 23 years I had the privilege and the responsibility of wearing it, defending it, and carrying it with me into stadiums all around the world.

“Today, the time has come to pass it on to the future. It was not an easy decision. Because some decisions are not made with the mind alone. They are also made with the heart.

“A journey that began in August 2003 and changed my life forever has come to an end. When I look back at everything with the national team, I don’t just see the matches, goals, records or trophies.

“I see people. I see the Portuguese people living abroad who, for 90 minutes, felt a little closer to home. I see millions of Portuguese people I have never met but who became part of my life. Because at the end of the day, football is so much bigger than a game. Football is about people.”

The five-time Ballon d’Or winner’s retirement follows his controversial decision to quit the Portugal camp during the international break last month, where he accused new manager Jorge Jesus of twice breaking his word after remaining an unused substitute against Norway.

The Portuguese FA handed Ronaldo a 30-day suspension - which would be served before the November international break - as well as imposing a €255 (£214.88) fine, with Jesus having not closed the door on bringing the country’s captain back into the fold.

However, Ronaldo has now taken the decision to permanently step away from Portugal duty, a month after storied rival Lionel Messi played his last game for Argentina.

Ronaldo made his Portugal debut in August 2003 when he came on as a half-time substitute for fellow legend Rui Costa in a 1-0 friendly win over Kazakhstan, eight days after completing his move from Sporting CP to Manchester United.

He scored his first goal at Euro 2004, bagging in a 2-1 group-stage loss to Greece, who Portugal would eventually lose to again in the final. He was named in the team of the tournament at 19.

Ronaldo was involved in an infamous incident with club team-mate Wayne Rooney in the Euro 2006 quarter-finals, with Ronaldo rushing over to the referee to protest after the Englishman was involved in a tangle with Ricardo Carvalho. Rooney was subsequently shown red, with Ronaldo caught winking on camera to add further fuel to the fire.

Ronaldo went on to appear at a joint-most six World Cup finals and an outright-most six European Championships, with his crowning glory coming in France in 2016.

Ronaldo’s best World Cup finish saw Portugal beaten in the semi-finals by France in 2006, with Zinedine Zidane’s penalty proving the difference in a 1-0 win.

His final attempt to claim a World Cup winners’ medal ended in tearful failure this year when Portugal were knocked out by eventual champions Spain in the last-16, courtesy of Mikel Merino’s stoppage-time winner.

“Throughout my entire career with the national team I always pushed myself beyond my limits,” he continued in his statement.

“I wanted to win, I wanted to be better, I wanted to make history. I always demanded the most of myself. I always wanted more. I always believed we could go further. And I gave everything I had for this shirt.

“There were perfect afternoons and nights. There were victories I will never forget. There were defeats that still hurt to remember.

“I made mistakes. I suffered. There were also moments when I couldn’t give the Portuguese people what they so desperately wanted. But I never gave up. And above all, I never stopped feeling proud to represent Portugal”

He remains 15 strikes short of 1,000-goal target before he retires from football for good, with his only scoring avenue coming in Saudi Arabia with current club Al-Nassr.

Aniioki A9 Pro Max review: Basically an electric motorcycle that you can pedal

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

There are electric bikes, there are electric motorcycles, and then there are machines like the Aniioki A9 Pro Max that plant a flag somewhere in the increasingly large no-man’s-land between the two.

Technically, it has pedals. You can pedal it fairly effectively too, with the actually adjustable seat. You can even pedal it for fun, which is more than I can say for some of the increasingly motorcycle-shaped “e-bikes” I’ve tested lately.

But let’s get something out of the way right at the beginning: this isn’t a street-legal electric bicycle in the US. Not when we’re talking about dual motors producing up to 6,000W of peak power and speeds of around 45 mph.

And you know what? That’s fine. As long as we’re all honest about what this thing is.

The Aniioki A9 Pro Max is absolutely massive, and the spec sheet makes that abundantly clear.

The version I tested uses a pair of 2,500W direct-drive hub motors, one in each wheel, with Aniioki claiming 3,000W peak power from each motor. That gives the bike a combined peak figure of 6,000W and up to 250 Nm of combined torque. It’s hard to overstate how massive that is.

Then there’s the battery. You can choose between a 60V 70Ah pack with 4.2 kWh of capacity or an even larger 60V 80Ah pack packing 4.8 kWh. For comparison, many typical commuter e-bikes have somewhere around 500-700 Wh of battery. The smaller battery here is roughly six to eight times that size. That’s absolutely in the territory of legit light electric motorcycles.

Aniioki claims up to 230+ miles (370 km) of pedal-assist range, though as usual, you should take maximum e-bike range figures as best-case numbers. Using the throttle and enjoying those big motors is going to dramatically reduce that figure. Even so, with more than 4 kWh of battery underneath you, range anxiety isn’t exactly the first thing that comes to mind.

All that hardware comes with a predictable penalty: weight. The 70Ah model tips the scales at around 188 lb (85 kg), while the 80Ah version approaches 193 lb (87.5 kg). Those aren’t e-bike weights. Those are motorcycle weights. Again, you can see the direction this is going.

Here’s where the A9 Pro Max gets more interesting to me.

A lot of high-powered e-motos include pedals seemingly as a regulatory fig leaf. They’re technically there, but the geometry is so bad that nobody in their right mind would want to pedal the thing farther than across a parking lot. Even some smaller and presumably Class 2 legal e-bikes that have moto-style frames are nearly unpedalable for actual riding over any reasonable distance.

But the A9 here is actually pedalable thanks to having something that more closely resembles a bicycle seat, plus adjustability that lets riders dial in the correct seat height.

The A9 Pro Max feels more like a strange combination of an oversized fat-tire e-bike and a small electric motorcycle. There’s a Shimano 7-speed drivetrain and a huge 72-tooth chainring, meaning the pedals aren’t entirely ornamental.

I actually enjoyed pedaling it with the pedal assist engaged. You’re obviously not going to mistake a nearly 200-pound machine rolling on enormous 26 x 4.8-inch tires for a lightweight fitness bike, but there’s something fun about contributing some human power while two giant hub motors do most of the heavy lifting.

And when you don’t feel like pedaling, there’s a full-twist throttle waiting for you.

That combination is really the appeal here. You can ride it more like an e-bike when the mood strikes, or twist the throttle and treat it more like a lightweight e-moto.

And again, I don’t want to beat a dead horse here, but remember: the pedals don’t make this an electric bicycle. It’s still an e-moto, but it’s one that you can actually have fun pedaling. And it’s worth pointing out that at a starting price of US $2,899 (or more for the larger battery capacities), that’s a fairly reasonable price compared to most e-motos that can be more limiting in how and where you can ride them.

Fortunately, Aniioki seems to understand that putting this much power on two wheels requires more serious components than the average bicycle.

That brings us to the elephant in the room.

In the US, consumer-product rules generally define a low-speed electric bicycle around a motor of less than 750W and a maximum motor-powered speed below 20 mph (32 km/h), while individual states use their own operating laws that typically match the familiar Class 1/2/3 framework. A 6,000W machine capable of around 45 mph (72 km/h) blows straight through normal limits.

So please don’t see pedals and assume this belongs on your local bicycle path.

Where and how you can legally operate something like the A9 Pro Max depends on your local laws and whether it can qualify under another vehicle category. For many owners, the sensible place to enjoy its full performance is going to be private property or other areas where such vehicles are permitted.

That caveat doesn’t make the bike itself bad. It just means buyers need to understand what they’re buying.

And judged on what it actually is rather than what the word “e-bike” on the product page suggests, I had a lot of fun on it.

There are certainly riders who would look at a nearly 200-pound, 45 mph machine and say, “Why not just buy an electric motorcycle?” That’s a perfectly reasonable question.

But I also understand the appeal of this weird middle ground. The A9 Pro Max retains enough bicycle DNA that you can pedal it around and enjoy pedal assist, yet has enough battery and motor underneath you to deliver something much closer to small-motorcycle performance when you twist the throttle.

Imagine going off-road and getting to fly up hills while pedaling hard, but then being able to switch to throttle once your legs get worn out. Sure, it’s not everyone’s cup of tea, but it’s a cup of tea.

This bike is big. It’s heavy. It’s absurdly powerful by e-bike standards. And it absolutely isn’t the machine I’d recommend for someone looking for a normal street-legal electric bicycle.

But if what you actually want is a big, powerful electric two-wheeler that still lets you turn the pedals when the mood strikes, the Aniioki A9 Pro Max makes a surprisingly compelling argument for this increasingly bizarre category of pedal-equipped e-motos.
