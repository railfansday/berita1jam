La Coppa Italia potrebbe cambiare volto nelle prossime tre stagioni. Nel nuovo bando per i diritti audiovisivi pubblicato dalla Lega Serie A, con scadenza al 14 dicembre, compaiono infatti due diverse formule pensate per rilanciare un torneo spesso snobbato fino alle fasi finali. A scegliere quale adottare sarà il mercato televisivo: prevarrà il format legato all'offerta economica più alta ricevuta dai broadcaster, con il via libera dell'Assemblea.

Prevede solo 4 club di Serie C nel turno preliminare e l'ingresso delle prime otto classificate della passata Serie A direttamente dagli ottavi di finale con il vantaggio di giocare in casa. Sul piano economico, la Lega ha fissato una richiesta minima di 54 milioni di euro a stagione, pari a 162 milioni complessivi nel triennio. Nathan Fielder’s new Elizabeth Holmes documentary is three hours long. You’ll wish it went on for three more.

NATHAN FIELDER HAS become one of the greatest arbiters of what is—or isn’t—the truth. Fielder, whose mainstream career unofficially kicked off with his show Nathan for You, a dry, quasi-documentary, quasi-scripted, quasi-spoof of shows like Undercover Boss, has found a way to level up with each project he’s made over the course of his still relatively short career, each step of the way inviting audiences to not only question the reality of what he’s bringing to the screen, but their own stances and ways of thinking about things broadly.

The road for Fielder has included HBO’s The Rehearsal (another often surreal, often Lynchian project that presents as real, but on a subtextual level asks viewers to question whether any of what he’s depicting was really happening as it was shown) and The Curse, a Showtime/Paramount+ series that he starred in and co-created alongside Emma Stone and Benny Safdie. That show was, surprise, surprise, incredibly thoughtful, layered, and, ultimately, surreal when it comes to its underlying themes of sincerity and intention.

In a way, all of this has led Fielder to his latest project, the new A24 documentary You Can See Everything. The subject is a fitting one for Fielder: Elizabeth Holmes, the former Theranos CEO and convicted fraudster who was the subject of books (Bad Blood) and television shows (The Dropout) about her misdeeds. It makes total sense that someone famous for skirting the truth would be of interest for Fielder, a creator who has now spent so much time examining not only the truth, but what audiences might accept as the truth even given some reason not to.

In 2022, Holmes was sentenced to 11 years and 3 months in federal prison. That’s where You Can See Everything comes in: Holmes and her partner Billy Evans invited Fielder (and a film crew, led by co-director Lance Oppenheim) to join them in a home they were renting for complete access during the 34 days before she reported to federal prison in Bryan, Texas, in May 2023. And the film begins there.

And, quite frankly, if you’re considering seeing You Can See Everything, it’s best to leave it there. Fielder gets the access he was promised in the movie, but the joy of the film isn’t only in this exclusive vision of such a notable figure, but how an artist with such a relationship to the truth engages with someone who so notably has, seemingly, bent it. We also get to see Holmes and Evans shot as human beings, removed from any of the prior media circuses that have surrounded them. Fielder and Oppenheim put a fair camera to everything and let the camera do what was promised: See everything.

You Can See Everything has been one of the best-reviewed films of the year, and it lives up to the billing. Just when you think you understand what’s happening and what’s going to happen, you’ll be quickly proven wrong. Just when you think you have a handle on who someone is and what they’re all about… you’ll quickly be proven wrong. The movie is three hours long, which, of course, we know, seems daunting. But by the time it ends, you’ll wish it was going on for three hours more.

Below, we answer some of your questions about You Can See Everything—without spoiling any of the movie’s major twists or turns.

You Can See Everything is now available to watch at home. You can purchase it on Apple TV and rent it on Prime Video—and we suggest doing whichever of those you prefer.

You Can See Everything is, in essence, two movies in one. The first half of the movie is what the trailer and promotional materials for the movie billed: Nathan Fielder joins Elizabeth Holmes (and her new husband Billy Evans) in a home they rented for the 34 days before she reported to federal prison in 2023. This proves to be just as compelling as you’d imagine, as Holmes and Evans are both completely idiosyncratic characters. Moments like when Fielder shows Holmes Abbott and Costello’s “Who’s on First” bit are instant classics.

Eventually Holmes reports to prison in Bryan, Texas, and the movie is not even halfway over. It’s at this point that your eyebrow should raise, and you do the ‘leaning forward in seat’ meme—because this is when You Can See Everything gets truly wild. The timeline jumps forward two hours, and Nathan Fielder gets up to some of his mind-bending, Rehearsal-type business. At this point, we see some people’s true colors, and also meet some other unexpected guests and surprises in the movie. This second part of the movie is what will blow your mind, and leave you thinking long after the credits begin to roll.

You Can See Everything should be an absolute lock for a number of Oscar nominations, including Best Picture. And it should be a strong contender in one of the acting categories for one person whose appearance we will not be spoiling in this story.

Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim are the co-directors of You Can See Everything, and both have been key figures in American film and television culture for the last decade.

Fielder is a Canadian-born actor and director (and childhood friend of Seth Rogen) who rose to fame as the creator and star of Comedy Central’s Nathan for You. He’s dabbled in acting here and there—including a role as a limo driver in Rogen’s underrated holiday comedy The Night Before and in the sensational limited series The Curse—but his real calling has come in mind-bending documentary/comedy series like The Rehearsal. In season 2 of The Rehearsal, for example, Fielder actually got his pilot’s license while exploring the aviation industry. (And that was filmed between part 1 and part 2 of You Can See Everything, which is just bananas.)

Oppenheim, who is only 30, has also established himself as one of the best documentarians in the business. His movies Some Kind of Heaven and Spermworld along with his HBO series Ren Faire are deeply layered portrayals of very real people in very real situations; he shoots these documentaries in styles completely different from anyone else, though, lighting them like cinematic spectacles rather than what you probably think of as a documentary in your mind. (Some of the cinematography in You Can See Everything is unbelievably beautiful.)

Primetime, A24’s film about Chris Hansen and To Catch a Predator that came out last month and starred Robert Pattinson, Skyler Gisondo, and Merritt Wever, was Oppenheim’s scripted feature debut.

It probably helps to have information on Elizabeth Holmes’s case—which we recommend getting from any number of sources: The Dropout was the name of the Hulu series that starred Amanda Seyfried as Holmes, but it was also a well-researched and reported podcast that served as the show’s basis. There’s also an HBO documentary called The Inventor and a book called Bad Blood. Any of this would make for a good source for the crimes Holmes was accused of committing. The movie also includes a very brief recap of her case in the early going—not exhaustive, but enough to get the idea.

It does also make sense to have an idea of who Fielder is, what he does, and how he engages with his subjects. We recommend HBO’s The Rehearsal for that, but it’s not 100 percent necessary to enjoy and appreciate the film.

That’s the question, isn’t it? You Can See Everything explores the idea of truth, lies, perspective, and even documentary filmmaking itself. If it makes the most sense to you to take all of You Can See Everything at face value, then please go for it. If you feel the need to question everything, well, that’s okay too. That’s what makes Fielder’s work so unique and so immersive—you can think about it about 100 different ways, and no understanding or interpretation is wrong. It’s set up to feel like a kaleidoscope. You can understand something one way, but maybe something pops up, and suddenly you can’t think about it that way anymore. That’s the point—and that’s what you’ll think about long after the credits roll.

Gian Piero Gasperini says Roma’s trip to Paris Saint-Germain is “a great moment” to test themselves against the best, and admits the Lisbon defeat with Atalanta still leaves a bitter taste.

With quotes via TuttoMercatoWeb, the Roma coach was speaking at his pre-match press conference at the Parc des Princes, alongside Paulo Dybala, ahead of Wednesday’s Champions League clash. Kick-off is at 21:00 CET [20:00 GMT].

“It’s definitely a very, very good moment for us to be able to play this game against the strongest team,” Gasperini said. “What we’re thinking about most at the moment is putting in a high-level performance.

“It’s clear we have to measure ourselves against this team. It’s the first time we’ve faced them with Roma, so we’ll have to understand straight away on the pitch what their level is. It’s certainly very high, and we need to see how much we can counter their technique and their speed. Our aim is to reduce their effectiveness a little so that we can also play our own game.”

Roma go to Paris with four points from four games after dramatic defeats to Real Madrid and Manchester United. PSG, the holders, have won all four of theirs.

Gasperini insisted those losses shouldn’t affect his side’s confidence.

“This is what the Champions League is like. It’s the most important competition in the world, with the highest level,” he said. “You can put in very good performances, like we did against Real and Manchester, but then there are key moments, and the best players in the world are on the pitch and can decide games.

“We have to focus on producing that type of performance, because it gives you confidence, makes you grow and carries over into the league.”

This is not Gasperini’s first big night against PSG. In the 2020 quarter-final in Lisbon, his Atalanta side were leading going into the final minutes, but lost 2-1 to two late goals.

“That’s a game that’s still a bit hard to swallow, because of the ending,” he admitted. “We were down to 10 men in the final minutes because of Freuler’s injury and had no substitutions left. PSG brought on Mbappé, and he was decisive. But it’s still one of the highest moments in Atalanta’s history.

“This is a different type of game, because PSG are different. They’re much more of a team now, and that’s thanks to Luis Enrique. Roma are coming into this competition after several years away, which is different for us. It’s a league-phase game, and for us it’s a measure of what we’re doing.”

CBS News Editor-in-Chief Bari Weiss has made headlines for multiple reasons since being hired for the job in October 2025, but one specific aspect of the job she continues to receive backlash for is 60 Minutes. This past summer, Weiss and executive producer Nick Bilton cut ties with several high-profile employees and, more recently, the show has seen losses in viewership. Now, some fresh numbers regarding the news magazine show’s 59th season have arrived, and they mark yet another setback for Weiss and her staff.

Going into this latest season of 60 Minutes, which premiered in September amid the 2026 TV schedule, there was much speculation as to how the show would fare ratings-wise. This latest Nielsen data update from The Wall Street Journal (via The Independent) suggests that the show has indeed been underperforming with its broadcasts. Per the report, the series averaged 8.3 million viewers for the nine episodes that aired between September 9 and November 16. That marks a 16 percent drop from the previous season.

Months ago, Season 59 premiered to 6.5 million viewers on CBS, and that sum differed from the initial report of 7.7 million (which was not finalized at the time). One of the key commonalities between both reports, however, is that the news program has seen losses within the 25-54 demographic. In that demo, 60 has apparently seen a 25 percent decline compared to last season. This demographic is critical, especially when considering on-air advertising.

These ratings mark another less-than-desired situation for CBS News during what’s been a major year of change for 60 Minutes. In May, correspondents Cecilia Vega and Sharyn Alfonsi were fired along with executive producer Tanya Simon and several others. Just recently, Vega said she found it “disturbing” that she still didn’t know the exact reason she was let go. And, shortly after those moves were made, Scott Pelley was also fired from the show after allegedly accusing Weiss of “murdering” the series.

Another topic of discussion revolves around the claims of “journalistic interference” at 60. Series alums like Pelley and Vega have alleged that they were encouraged to insert what they considered to be news bias into their reporting. CBS veteran Dan Rather also chastised Weiss and co. for their reporting. The E-I-C has denied that employees’ editorial autonomy has been taken away from journalists at the company.

With the team at 60 Minutes being aware of the chatter that’s surrounded the show as of late, Bari Weiss recently addressed concerns. She recently spoke at a conference organized by The Information, where she said the show had been doing “tremendously well.” She also emphasized that her main objective for CBS News as a whole was to build a news media organization built on trust. In doing so, she’s made changes to several programs, with the aforementioned show really receiving a makeover.

Andrew Scott on Starring in ‘Elsinore’ and Feeling ‘Very Grateful’ to Be an Out Gay Actor

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Andrew Scott remembers first learning about HIV and AIDS when he was growing up in Dublin, Ireland.

“I would have been incredibly young, but I remember Oprah Winfrey talking about it,” he tells me. “She did quite a lot. She had Magic Johnson. That was a little bit later, but I remember she had Magic Johnson on her show and she spoke about the crisis. She had people who were living with HIV. It seemed kind of far away at the time.” Related Stories ‘PAW Patrol’ Spinoff Series ‘Skye’s Pup Squad’ Set for 2027 Release

Scott is now at the center of the conversation because of his starring role in “Elsinore.” He plays Ian Charleson, a star of the 1981 Oscar-winning “Chariots of Fire,” who starred in a 1989 production of “Hamlet” at the National Theatre in London as he was dying of AIDS.

Charleson never publicly revealed his sexuality or his illness before passing away just weeks after completing his run in “Hamlet.”

Scott came out in 2013 in an interview with “The Independent.”

“I relate to it deeply, and I relate to it as a professional who wants to just do my job and as somebody who feels very grateful to get those opportunities that Ian and his counterparts didn’t,” says Scott, who is considered a front-runner in the Oscar race for best actor. “I feel celebratory that I can look around and that there are out gay actors who are growing in number and not placing us in this insidious heaviness burden about separating actors in that way. It feels really wonderful that we have a lot of out gay actors in the movie in a movie about there not being a lot of out gay actors.”

Director Simon Stone says casting an openly gay actor as Charleson was “hugely important.”

“I think what message that sends to the world is that times have changed, and that we’re not going to apologize for the story that we’re telling here,” he says. “Andrew has had the huge benefit of the pioneering work of so many actors before him, that his career was not disadvantaged at all by being openly gay. He’s probably the first generation of actors who can say that.”

“Elsinore” also stars Billie Piper as Patsy, Charleson’s best friend who took care of him through his illness. Piper remembers Stone being mindful of how heavy that subject matter was. “It was a very sensitive set. Simon was very good at setting a very peaceful environment,” Piper says. “People were in their socks, sort of padding around. It felt like a womb. It felt like a nest. People could be extremely emotional. I think a lot of the crew were very upset watching takes. I think for the actors sometimes you’re so adrenalized that you’re sort of just in it. Reflection is something that sometimes comes later.”

The “Elsinore” cast also includes Olivia Colman as Charleson’s physician, Dr. Margaret Johnson, David Dawson as filmmaker Derek Jarman and Joe Locke as a fellow AIDS patient.

Screenwriter Stephen Beresford’s research included talking to the real “fantastically generous” Patsy as well as many other people who knew Charleson. “Some of the people I spoke to are not in great health now, who are older,” Beresford says. “But they made the effort to come and talk to me because they wanted to honor Ian, and that’s very special.”

As Charleson’s health deteriorates in the film, he develops Kaposi sarcoma lesions on his skin. Scott says he tried not to pay attention to the transfer tattoos that makeup department head Nadia Stacey applied to his skin to duplicate the dark cancerous growths.

“In some ways, a little bit like Ian, I had to try and think, ‘Well, they aren’t there.’ He did what he had to do, but he was determined with getting on with the business of living,” Scott says. “He threw himself into ‘Hamlet,’ and I threw myself into ‘Elsinore.’ It was very important to me to not fetishize Ian’s death, but to really think about his living. If everybody else was playing the tragedy around him, he was certainly looking toward the light and in some ways I tried to mirror that.”

Glassnode tracks realized losses by looking at coins that move on-chain at prices lower than when they previously moved. Measuring those losses against the capital that entered Bitcoin over the preceding year gives analysts a way to compare sell-offs across market cycles, even as the cryptocurrency grows in value.

The last time the indicator reached these levels, Sam Bankman-Fried’s crypto empire was collapsing and Bitcoin was trading below $16,000. This time, the selling pressure has coincided with a much broader retreat from risky assets, and even good news for the tech industry hasn’t been enough to keep Bitcoin afloat.

Bitcoin rallied 5% in about half an hour on Monday after Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang was returning as CEO. Less than six hours later, Bitcoin had given back the entire rally, while the Nasdaq 100 still held roughly three-quarters of its gains. In trading, this is known as a bull trap, and happens on strong bearish trends.

Bitcoin’s correlation with the Nasdaq 100 is now at its highest since September 2022, Glassnode said. Over the past 60 trading days, Bitcoin has tended to gain less when tech stocks rise and lose more when they fall.

U.S. investors have also been pulling money out of the market. Spot Bitcoin ETFs have lost $4.3 billion on net over the past 20 trading days, while Coinbase traders have been heavy net sellers over the past month. Buying and selling on Binance, meanwhile, have been roughly balanced.

“Spikes like this often came close to past lows, but some came early,” Glassnode said. The firm pointed to the sell-offs of early 2019, March 2020, and November 2022, when losses surged near market bottoms, but also to earlier episodes in 2018 and 2022 when prices had further to fall.

Long-term holders—investors who have held their coins for roughly five months or longer—accounted for most realized losses from mid-April to mid-November. Nearly 90% of the long futures positions vulnerable to liquidation between last week’s Bitcoin price and $53,200 are also gone, reducing the potential for another cascade of forced selling within that range.

Glassnode is watching Bitcoin’s realized price, the average price at which circulating coins last moved, which currently stands at $53,200. Bitcoin traded below that metric before bottoming in each of the previous three major bear markets.

Applying the discounts seen at the lows of 2022 and 2018 to today’s realized price would put Bitcoin at roughly $41,000 and $37,000, respectively.

For now, the more immediate concern is whether Bitcoin can close above its June closing low of $58,500. Glassnode said a recovery above $60,000 accompanied by renewed ETF inflows would help establish whether the summer lows are holding.

German Women Climb New Route on Sharphu VI

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last month, five women with the German Alpine Club established a new route on the northwest ridge of Sharphu VI in eastern Nepal. It was only the second overall ascent of the 6,076m peak near Kangchenjunga.

In October, Steffi Feistl, Anna Gomeringer, Kristin Hinkelmann, Kathi Huber, and Fenja Kochl set up their base camp at 4,000m. A huge amount of snow had fallen earlier that season, guaranteeing arduous slogging in some areas.

Their preliminary plan had been to attempt either Sharphu II or Sharphu VI, but Sharphu II’s condition looked too dangerous due to unstable rock, threatening seracs, and hazardous snow conditions.

Sharphu II and VI. Photo: Xtreme Climbers/labels by Gunter Seyfferth

On the other hand, the northwest ridge of Sharphu VI seemed feasible. The team acclimatized over several reconnaissance trips, overnighting up to 4,800m. They then set up their advanced base camp on a moraine ridge at that altitude. Then Kochl, Hinkelmann, and Huber set off over initially easy but deep snow-covered terrain.

They eventually reached a steep section at around 5,300m where powder snow overlying bare rock made placing protection difficult. “The mixed pitches had looked easy, but were steeper than expected,” recalled Huber. “Nasty, granular snow, and no hold on the rock underneath.”

Their work complete, the trio rappelled down, and the following day, Feistl and Gomeringer took their turn. They inched up a difficult mixed pitch followed by 200 vertical meters of burrowing through waist-deep powder.

The route up the northwest ridge of Sharphu VI. Photo: German Alpine Club

After a rest day, the five women set off for the summit. They reached a glacier plateau via a 5,800m, previously unclimbed sub-peak. Here, they bivvied. On October 21, they began the final two kilometers to the main summit. The recent sun had hardened the snow, so they sank at most knee-deep. Eventually, they reached the summit ridge. They had to take turns standing on the summit, because that final ridge was so narrow.

Gomeringer and Kochl on the summit of Sharphu VI. Photo: German Alpine Club

Sharphu VI was first climbed on March 25, 2025, by a Chilean-Nepalese team who thought they’d climbed a different mountain. They went up via the east flank. Before that, Sharphu VI had been attempted only once, in 2023.

The German women’s route featured 1,300m of elevation gain, graded M5, 55°. The expedition was the culmination of the German Alpine Club’s three-year women’s training program.
