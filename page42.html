Why ASML Holdings Fell in October

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A big earnings beat wasn't enough to offset broader fears that drove the AI stock sell-off last month and into November.

Shares of ASML Holdings (ASML +5.02%) fell 11.5% in October, according to data from S&P Global Market Intelligence.

ASML reported solid third-quarter results mid-month and gave a strong Q4 guide; alas, these strong earnings weren't enough to offset the extreme sell-off in AI-related stocks, especially semiconductor stocks, ahead of the November midterms.

ASML holds a monopoly on extreme ultraviolet (EUV) technology, which is required to produce all leading-edge logic and memory chips that power the AI revolution. As such, there isn't really a debate about whether ASML has strong near-term growth, but rather about how much growth there will be, and for how long.

In the third quarter, ASML revenue grew 55.2% over the prior year to 11.7 billion Euros, while adjusted earnings per share rose 135.9% to 12.53 Euros per share. Revenue actually slightly missed expectations, but ASML's bottom line beat on expanding margins. Moreover, ASML guided for fourth-quarter numbers well ahead of expectations, with revenue in the range of 14.2 billion to 15.2 billion Euros, while maintaining its higher-than-historical margins.

Not only that, but ASML also said that its customers' demand forecasts had increased so much that it was looking to expand capacity in 2028 by more than the 30% it was already planning.

Yet even all this good news was overwhelmed by anti-AI stock sentiment that took hold in October and continued into November. Fears over the sustainability of growth at leading AI labs, along with consternation over the impact of a Democratic "wave" election, appeared to overshadow ASML's strong results.

After the Democratic "wave" last week, AI-related semiconductor stocks sold off hard, and now appear to be pricing in the end of the current cycle next year or in 2028.

However, this may be an overblown reaction. After all, not even a month ago, ASML had conveyed that its customers were seeing stronger demand for longer. If the build-out continues with only mild modifications, then ASML is a strong buy on this dip.

A former teammate of Harry Kane and Luka Modric has this week been convicted of fare dodging in London, with his career taking a very different turn from that of his two Ballon d’Or-winning counterparts.

The news comes in the same week that Kane became the first Englishman in 25 years to win football’s most prestigious individual award, following in the footsteps of Michael Owen, who claimed the famous Golden Ball in 2001.

Kane’s journey began in humble surroundings, with the striker turning out for the likes of Fleetwood Town before climbing the footballing ladder at Premier League side Tottenham and, later, Champions League contenders Bayern Munich.

That rollercoaster ride through the football pyramid has seen him share the pitch with all sorts of characters from different backgrounds. One former teammate has now been found guilty of a rather unexpected offence - dodging a £7 train fare, despite having played a sport where lucrative contracts are not rare.

According to the Wimbledon Guardian, Sebastien Bassong, now 41, was caught following a friend through the ticket barriers at St Paul’s Station on May 2.

A subsequent investigation by a Transport for London (TfL) officer revealed that Bassong had been using a train driver’s nominee pass that did not belong to him.

In a statement to the court, TfL revenue control officer Adebukola Anifowose said she spotted Bassong at 1.08pm “pretend to tap an Oystercard enclosed in a white wallet before tailgating through the wide-aisle gate behind another male”.

Bassong appeared at Wimbledon Magistrates’ Court on Friday, October 30, where he was convicted of travelling without a valid ticket. He was fined £220 and ordered to pay the £7 fare, £250 in prosecution costs and an £88 victim surcharge, bringing the total to £565.

The friend who accompanied him later told the TfL officer that he had given Bassong the pass because he was “experiencing financial and personal hardship due to a divorce and unemployment”.

Bassong's career never really took off after he left Tottenham following just three seasons in north London. His only notable success thereafter came at Norwich City, where he made 123 appearances, won the club's Player of the Year award in 2013, and helped the Canaries secure promotion from the Championship via the playoffs in 2015.

He then spent a season at Peterborough United but managed just one appearance in the EFL Trophy as he continued to struggle with a recurring knee injury.

The same problem prevented him from making any lasting impact at Volos, where he made three appearances in the Greek Super League during the 2019/20 season before announcing his retirement. Before that decision, during lockdown, he featured in a pre-season friendly for Isthmian League side Haringey Borough.

These days, Bassong works as a strategic performance and leadership advisor, motivational coach and public speaker. He has also taken part in charity boxing events, including one organised by Pro Project Promotions, where he knocked out former Arsenal defender Armand Traore.

Here's How Much AAA Says It Costs To Own A Car In 2026

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

When we consider whether we can afford a car, so much of the focus tends to be on the sticker price that we often forget that the costs don't stop once we've made our purchase. Small, everyday expenses that come alongside, like fuel, servicing, finance interest, depreciation, insurance, and tax, aren't always obvious, but the true cost of car ownership adds up over time.

With that in mind, the car valuation experts at AAA performed a study to investigate car ownership costs in 2026. It turns out that drivers are facing ongoing price increases, with the AAA 2026 Your Driving Costs study revealing that the annual cost of owning and running a new car has soared to $12,863, or $1,071.92 per month this year. That's up from an average of $11,577 or $964.78 per month in 2025.

At that rate, ownership costs can add 32.67% on top of the $39,376 sales-weighted average MSRP of new vehicles. The larger bills have been linked to surging fuel prices, faster depreciation, and higher finance interest rates. Still, your specific costs can vary greatly, depending on your car's fuel type.

Owning any car at all is getting more expensive, but the AAA study revealed something more fundamental: Electric cars cost more to own than their traditional fuel-powered and hybrid counterparts. That's largely because depreciation on EVs is worse than gas or hybrid cars. On top of that, those who drive electric cars face higher fees and finance costs. Where EVs tend to do better is in fuel expenses, as they typically cost 66% to 70% less to run than gasoline cars.

Looking at a set of average consumption figures, AAA found that regular-grade gasoline costs $4.152 per gallon on average, while electric cars charged at home averaged as little as 18 cents per kilowatt hour. EVs have seen smaller electricity rate increases, too. The AAA data shows electricity prices have jumped 7.8% since 2025, while gas costs have increased a shocking 31.8%.

Even so, owning and running EVs costs more than gas-powered models, due to higher fees, finance costs, and depreciation, with certain EVs said to depreciate about twice as fast as their gas-powered counterparts. That contributed to pushing EV ownership costs above gas-powered models across the medium sedan, compact SUV, and pickup truck segments.

Nowhere is that gap more pronounced than in the medium sedan segment, where gas-powered medium sedans cost $3,080 less to own (at $10,582 per year) than comparable EV medium sedans ($13,662). Hybrids are even cheaper (at $10,204) and cost the least to own in every category, including the medium SUV class, where EVs win back some ground against gas-powered models, costing $14,332 versus $14,431 for gasoline SUVs.

As a car owner, every mile you drive costs money, but it is entirely possible to keep costs down. One way to do this is to shop around for cheaper insurance. Compare quotes from providers, or even consider buying a car from one of the cheapest brands to insure.

If you are team gas-powered cars, getting a model with great fuel efficiency is just as important. It should be easy enough, seeing as gasoline cars are now much more efficient than they used to be, with some of the most fuel-efficient models released over the years rated to return as high as 58 mpg.

You'll also want to consider depreciation. It is one of the single largest ownership costs and can add a weighted average of $4,422 per year to the cost of running some popular models, according to the AAA study. Toyota, Subaru, and Honda are some of the best brands for value retention, but if you're after something more premium, there's always Lexus. It's the luxury car brand with the best resale value, according to CarEdge.

However, maintenance and spare parts are usually more expensive for luxury marques than mainstream brands like Toyota or Honda. Speaking of which, staying on top maintenance is yet another important part of vehicle ownership that can help keep costs low. Extraction shooters need a PvE mode for casual players—so what’s the controversy?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This idea isn't half as controversial as half of you make it out to be.

Any major extraction shooter release inevitably summons the age-old debate of PvE vs. PvP. It’s been the case with ARC Raiders and DMZ, and that schism will resurface whenever the next one launches. You can always count on heated arguments stirring controversy about something that shouldn’t be controversial at all.

After all, how dare players enjoy PvE in a PvPvE game? The way the debate often goes, you’d assume it was a cardinal sin, like the PvE crowd was planning to personally tar and feather every PvP player in existence.

Instead, PvE players often just want a more forgiving, relaxed experience that does not involve getting ratted to death in Stella Montis or having to spend an entire match in Hajin with one’s butthole clenched so tightly that not even a Wi-Fi signal passes through it. And from the looks of it, this isn’t a small crowd, either.

Take ARC Raiders, for example. The demand for a PvE mode was so large that Embark tested a toggle that disabled damage between players last month. Before then, 37 percent of its fanbase (and growing) were focused on PvE cooperation, 34 were there purely for PvP (stable since launch), and 27 landed in the middle, Embark said. Over 51 percent of all surveyed users showed interest in a dedicated PvE mode, according to the studio.

If even the thought of discussing this makes you feel like Ben Affleck with a cigarette, you’re not the only one who’s exhausted. The PvE vs. PvP argument is the reheated leftovers from the skill-based matchmaking debate, annoyingly inescapable when battle royales were all the rage.

Often, the higher-skill players don’t want to match against others of similar ability because their games get too difficult and stop being fun. And yes, this is exactly what happens to lower-skill players without SBMM.

Activision ran a famous experiment with its matchmaking in Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 3 in 2024. The company loosened the skill constraints for half of its players during a month. At the end of the test, it noted that the 10 percent of affected players at the top of the skill pyramid returned more often, but the remaining 90 percent were considerably less likely to come back. And that’s with a diluted SBMM.

“If [SBMM] were completely removed, we would expect to see the player population erode rapidly in the span of a few months, resulting in a negative outcome for all our players,” the study said.

Read what’s behind the latest news in gaming (with a bit of snark) by subscribing to the Destructoid Checkpoint newsletter!

PvP is zero-sum by definition: for one to win, another must lose. Unsurprisingly, it doesn’t feel good to be on the losing side. Having the chance to opt out of that and into a different playstyle altogether shouldn’t cause this much uproar—especially not in a game where keeping gear plays a major role. And often, it doesn’t even relate exclusively to skill, but the type of experience one wants to have.

This time, it’s not even a matter of catering to one part of the audience in detriment of another, which would be an understandable concern if it made a modicum of sense. Embark isn’t going to make your fingers fall off every time you shoot another player. Having PvE in DMZ won’t make Activision cease content for Warzone or multiplayer, otherwise CoD Zombies wouldn’t exist.

Often, the people contemplated by PvE mode aren’t even in the game, but they’re yearning to be. The lack of full-on PvE in DMZ didn’t make me flock to multiplayer; it made me uninstall the game altogether. I only bought ARC Raiders because of the PvE test and the game’s aggression-based matchmaking. If that went away, I’d disappear with it. My friends did so the second the PvE test ended, and they’re eagerly waiting for the mode to return.

Extraction shooters being unapproachable made sense when they were niche. Now that every live-service seems to flirt with the genre, though, they should be more welcoming, especially if they want to draw a larger crowd.

As an avid frequenter of friendly lobbies in ARC Raiders, I’ve had more positive experiences with randoms than I can count, and that’s a big reason why I open the game. I’m having one hell of a time fighting Matriarchs, defibbing other raiders, and dropping duplicate blueprints. My archenemy was a cat bed, and I’m thrilled this was the case. It sucks when it’s tooth-and-nail all the time, as every big opponent of SBMM will tell you.

The difference is, unlike hero shooters or battle royales, extraction shooters already have a PvE element baked into them, no retrofitting required. Of course, you may have to reshuffle some things, like increasing the difficulty of the AI-controlled enemies to raise the threat and keep players on their toes. But it’s different from creating something entirely new, like if people wanted bots in Apex Legends or full-blooded PvP in the extraction-adjacent Helldivers 2. And it’s certainly not too much to ask.

There’s a reason extraction shooters were somewhat obscure before the gaming industry threw a borderline infinite amount of money at making the genre mainstream. Soulslikes aside, not everyone wants to get their teeth kicked in as a pastime. Sure, the cutthroat uncertainty from PvPvE shaped the identity of ARC Raiders, but there are ways to evoke that without fighting other players, as Helldivers 2 and GTFO have proved.

ARC Raiders has come a long way in the past year, in no small part because of the 37 percent of PvE players (and in bigger part because of the 63 percent of other raiders). Maybe the burgeoning PvE scene changes the vision of the game somewhat, and that’s fine. DMZ getting a fully PvE mode wouldn’t be entirely out of the realm of possibility, considering that’s basically what MWZ was. That’s the beauty and bane of live services: there’s always room for reinvention.
