These are some of the strongest 3D printer filaments for durable parts

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Stronger filament doesn't automatically mean a stronger print, and the toughest materials can require better hardware too.

If you're looking for one winner, it's not exactly convenient that a 3D print can be "strong" in a few different ways. A rigid bracket should resist bending, while a part that gets knocked around may last longer if it can flex and recover instead of breaking. In this article, we're going to hone in on rigidity and the types of materials that work well for structural parts.

Carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon (PA-CF) and polycarbonate (PC) are two of the strongest practical materials for the kind of filament-based 3D printers that are accessible to the average maker. Both materials can handle demanding functions, higher temperatures and considerably rougher treatment than everyday polylactic acid (PLA) or glycol-modified polyethylene terephthalate (PETG).

Sure, there are stronger, more expensive specialty materials that could vie for the title of heavyweight filament champ. But let's stick to the pros and cons of carbon-fiber nylon versus polycarbonate, so you can decide which material packs the most punch for your projects.

Carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon (PA-CF) combines nylon with short carbon fibers — hence the name. Nylon already handles demanding functional parts well, and the added fibers make the finished material stiffer and more resistant to bending under load. How resistant, you ask?

Bambu Lab's dry-state test puts its carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon 6 (PA6-CF) at 151 megapascals (MPa) of bending strength. Its heat-deflection temperature is 186 degrees Celsius, meaning it resisted deformation under a specified load until around that temperature. Those figures help explain why carbon-fiber nylon is used for brackets, fixtures and other working parts instead of for items that simply sit pretty on a shelf.

A strong filament, however, does not necessarily make a strong print. How you orient the object on the print bed, plus wall thickness, infill and part shape, can significantly change how much abuse the project can take. Bambu Lab does not claim the 151 MPa result applies to every finished print. Pricing is another factor to note, as Polymaker currently lists a 1kg spool of its Fiberon PA6-CF20 at $52.99 at time of writing

There is some hardware housekeeping to contemplate too. Carbon fibers are abrasive, so your old faithful brass nozzle may not last long. Bambu recommends a hardened steel nozzle, requires PA6-CF to be dried before use, and lists a nozzle temperature of 260 to 290 degrees Celsius with an enclosed printer when using this material.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) says fused-filament desktop printers can emit respiratory irritants and ultrafine particles, so good ventilation or filtration is important to consider.

Unlike carbon-fiber composites, polycarbonate (PC) produces strong parts without chopped fibers. It's a stiff engineering plastic with good heat resistance, making it suitable for housings, brackets and other parts that need to withstand more than light use.

Once again, you'll need to consider your equipment and workspace before deciding if PC is the best and strongest filament for you. Similar to PA-CF, Polymaker recommends a nozzle temperature of 250 to 270 degrees Celsius, a 90 to 105 degrees Celsius heated bed, and an enclosed printer. The company says large, dense parts require a heated chamber, which helps reduce uneven cooling and warping. Good workplace ventilation or exhaust is recommended.

When it comes to strength, print direction matters, too. Polymaker measured 69.1 MPa of tensile strength across the printed layers and 52.8 MPa between them. PC's bending strength clocked in at 106 MPa, a drop compared to the 151 MPa logged for PA-CF.

The big difference with PC versus PA-CF is the price. Polymaker lists PolyLite PC at $29.99 for a 1kg spool and PolyLite PLA at $19.99at time of writing. The bigger pain point could be your printer, since lower-cost models may struggle to maintain the temperatures PC prefers.

The ultimate adulting task: signing up for health insurance

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Not many 22-year-olds share DJ Mikunda’s enthusiasm for health insurance coverage.

“You know what sounds like a good Friday night? It’s looking at health insurance,” he joked.

But Mikunda has a reason to be eager. He has gone without coverage since he left home and started working in 2023. He’s now a manager at a gas station in St. Louis.

In years past, he has looked at plans on the health insurance marketplace at healthcare.gov — but the options proved overwhelming and a bit too expensive.

“I feel like I’m a well-informed individual,” Mikunda said. “I try to stay updated on what a deductible is and what the premiums are and copays. And I’m still finding myself a little bit lost.”

To sort through the basics, HealthQ introduced Mikunda to independent assister Jackie Shrago, who has been helping people navigate the insurance marketplace since the early days of the Affordable Care Act.

Search for a free “independent assister” on healthcare.gov to better understand your insurance options.

Shrago started by asking Mikunda about his income. How much money you expect to make in the coming year determines whether you’re eligible for reduced monthly payments. The system uses a sliding scale for households making less than 400% of the federal poverty rate — which means, for 2027 enrollees, an individual with no children can make up to $63,480 and still qualify for a discount.

A person in their mid-20s making $50,000, for instance, would get an average 23% discount, according to KFF’s subsidy calculator. For someone making $30,000, the tax credit would cover nearly three-quarters of the cost.

It’s important to estimate next year’s income as accurately as possible. If you end up making more than anticipated, you may owe money back to the government.

“The last thing you want is a surprise when you file your income taxes,” Shrago told Mikunda.

Short-Term Affordability vs. Long-Term Needs

Then, Shrago pivoted to the other considerations: How much can you afford to pay every month? And what kind of coverage do you really need?

It’s a personal decision about financial planning and risk tolerance, balancing up-front payments with out-of-pocket costs. Mikunda knows he’ll need regular doctor visits and prescription medication, because he has a chronic joint condition that has gone virtually untreated since he started working and lost Medicaid coverage.

So he might consider stretching his monthly budget for a plan that covers more of the medical cost.

Those financial trade-offs drive much of the decision, forcing you to find a “comfortable medium” between the level of coverage you want and the amount you’re willing to pay, Mikunda said.

There’s much to consider, especially for a first-timer. But one-on-one help is available at healthcare.gov.

If you click on the link to “Find local help,” you can filter specifically to search for “independent assisters” like Shrago in your ZIP code. Unlike insurance agents and brokers, assisters are forbidden from charging for their services or receiving compensation from insurance companies. (The brokers listed at healthcare.gov far outnumber the independent assisters, but the assisters are findable.)

Shrago also recommends starting the selection process early enough to step away and think.

“If you’re dealing with something that is uncertain enough and you feel really awkward about it, you don’t need to make a decision today,” she said. “Take your time and think about it.”

Assisters tend to have the least availability right before the federal marketplace’s Jan. 15 deadline, so reach out early.

The Trump administration cut funding for independent assisters and navigator nonprofits by roughly 90% in 2025. When making that announcement, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services said the program was inefficient, costing more than $1,000 per person enrolled. The reduction in funding means fewer people helping. In Ohio, for example, the number of paid navigators serving state residents dropped from 50 to five at the start of open enrollment in 2025.

Gen Z believes using AI is making their colleagues dumb and lazy—but may paradoxically see it as key to their own promotion, Wharton says

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last year, researchers from Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon University revealed startling evidence about the impact of using AI on how—and how hard—people think, finding that among more than 300 knowledge workers, leaning too much on AI tools like ChatGPT was associated with diminished critical thinking skills.

The study, mirrored by results from MIT-led research, suggested that even using AI for low-stakes tasks such as proofreading “can lead to significant negative outcomes in high-stakes contexts,” like writing legal documents, the study authors wrote.

For the young generation of digital natives navigating AI anxiety around keeping up with peers using the technology and AI displacing them from jobs, the fear of the technology making people dumber is dominant. But that hasn’t stopped them from using AI—even when they’re explicitly told not to.

A Wharton-led survey published earlier this year, conducted in partnership with Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation, found young people are ramping up their AI use, even as their concerns about it causing lazy thinking persist. A survey of nearly 2,500 U.S. adults between the ages of 18 and 28 completed in October 2025 found 79% of respondents believed AI makes people lazier, and 62% said they had concerns it makes people less smart.

“What we find is deep ambivalence on how Gen Z is thinking [about] using AI,” Benjamin Lira Luttges, a postdoctoral scholar at Wharton who led research for the report, told Fortune.

Despite these fears, Gen Zers have increased their AI usage. Pew Research Center data published in June found 66% of people ages 18 to 29 have ever used an AI chatbot, such as ChatGPT, up from 55% in 2024. However, 37% of those young adults said they believed the impact of AI on them over the next 20 years would be negative, the most pessimistic attitude of any age group surveyed.

The paradox of Gen Z’s willingness to use AI at the office, even amid persistent worries about the technology’s impact on critical thinking, lays bare the young generation’s complicated feelings toward AI, according to the report’s authors. After all, Gen Z’s fraught relationship with AI runs deep. Nearly one-fifth of the generation is worried about AI displacing them at work, yet they lead the workplace in AI adoption.

Though they require some decoding, Gen Z’s tangled attitudes toward AI can be critical in designing a path forward for the technology, more broadly, to be best integrated into the workplace, Lira Luttges suggested.

“Young people lead the adoption of new technologies, and a lot of things that are often seen as fringe, as not mainstream, are adopted by young people and eventually become part of the mainstream,” he said. “So in a sense … looking at Gen Z is a way of looking towards the future of work.”

Lira Luttges speculates the biggest mental factor informing Gen Z’s attitudes toward AI is simply a bias toward immediate gratification, a disposition more prominent in younger, developing minds.

“There’s a legitimate tradeoff between benefits and costs that you get from using AI,” he said. “Our brains are wired to prefer smaller, immediate rewards versus long-term, delayed rewards.”

As Gen Z grapples with finding or keeping jobs, as well as scaling their career ladders, job performance bolstered by an AI boost may hold more appeal than the less tangible threat of critical thinking skills loss. Similarly, even if an employer does not want an employee using AI for certain tasks, those workers, particularly if young, might consider getting their tasks done efficiently as more important, particularly if the risk of getting caught is slow, Lira Luttges noted.

Anyone—not just Gen Z—could also fall victim to the better-than-average effect, a statistically impossible phenomenon of most people generally believing they are above average at a certain task. Gen Z survey respondents, for example, may see themselves as AI power-users, Lira Luttges said. Sure, AI could atrophy critical thought capabilities and make other people lazy, but not those filling out the survey.

To maximize how AI is used in the workplace, employers should not ban AI, but rather embrace ambivalence toward it, the report authors argued. According to the survey, respondents who reported using AI more frequently worried less about its impact on intelligence and motivation, indicating AI anxiety may resolve over time.

But resolving AI anxiety doesn’t address the question of AI use impacting critical thought. Some future-of-work experts, including Mark Beasley, professor and director at North Carolina State University’s Poole College of Management, believe a critical thinking gap, not an AI skills gap, will pose a serious threat to organizational pipelines and business operations. Beasley told Fortune the threat AI poses to entry-level jobs could mean insufficient training and experience for middle- and eventually upper-tier positions in the near future.

“The biggest risk organizations face is just being stagnant,” he said.

But as long as workplaces are intentional about how they implement AI, Lira Luttges said the technology won’t have a significant impact on critical thinking.

“For every task, there are two kinds of efforts,” Lira Luttges said. “There is effort that is germane to the task, that is intrinsic to the thing that you’re doing, and that kind of effort is the effort that you put in, and gets translated into learning. But there’s a lot of effort that is just there, that’s just like friction, that doesn’t really teach you anything.”

“You should outsource the crap, not the craft,” he added.

Barack Obama says work-life balance isn’t real for successful people

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If finding success in your career is on your bucket list, you might need to get used to working lunches and staying past 5 p.m. That’s, at least, according to former President Barack Obama, who revealed that achieving proper work–life balance will not always be possible.

“If you want to be excellent at anything—sports, music, business, politics—there’s going to be times of your life when you’re out of balance, where you’re just working and you’re single-minded,” he admitted on The Pivot Podcast in 2024.

It’s a lesson he—and his family—learned the hard way.

The 65-year-old said when he first ran for president, he was on the constant grind for over a year and a half. While he said he would take some breaks during the weekends, his wife, Michelle, was largely left picking up the slack at home, like taking care of the kids, even while she had a full-time job of her own.

Though the time away from his family led to two terms in the White House, Obama admitted it wasn’t sustainable. As president, he set a rule where he had family dinner every night at 6:30 p.m.—even if he had to go back to work later. And while some leaders might think it takes away valuable work time, he said the opportunity to decompress with family was actually incredibly impactful.

“They ground you and give you perspective, and over time, that makes you better,” he said.

Even as a former president, Obama said his political accomplishments—what kept him away from his family—won’t be what he remembers at the end of life. Instead, it’ll be the love and memories he had with his children.

“I don’t think there’s a perfect formula,” he said at an Obama Foundation event in 2019. “I think it starts with recognizing that on my deathbed, I am confident that I will not remember any bill I passed, I will not remember any speech I gave, any big crowds.”

That’s not to say career aspirations are not important, he said, it just means the level of commitment someone can give to their work all depends on what life is like at home—and vice versa.

“There will be phases in your life where you have to prioritize different things,” Obama said. “There are times where it will be okay for you to just throw yourself into work because everything’s in a pretty good place. There are going to be times where you have to maybe make some sacrifices on the work side because things aren’t all okay at home.”

For couples, the same premise applies—sometimes sacrifice will be made to aid the other to focus on their dream, but the favor has to be returned.

“In both cases, [Michelle and I] try to say, you can have it all—but you can’t have it all at once,” he told The Pivot Podcast. “You have to recognize there may be times in your life that you are going to be busy, and the other person has to put up the slack, but you have to be willing to do that for the other person as well and try to create some equity inside the family.”

The debate over work-life balance isn’t just top of mind for political leaders—but business leaders, too.

LinkedIn’s cofounder, Reid Hoffman, has previously said that work–life balance is not much of an option for entrepreneurs who want to break through the competition.

“If I ever hear a founder talking about, ‘this is how I have a balanced life’—they’re not committed to winning,” Hoffman told Stanford University’s “How to Start a Startup” class in 2014. “The only really great founders are [the one’s who are] like, ‘I am going to put literally everything into doing this.’”

But like Obama, Hoffman had one exception to the grind: dinner with families.

“When we started LinkedIn, we started with people who had families. So we said, sure, go home have dinner with your family,” Hoffman said late last year on the Diary of a CEO podcast. “Then, after dinner with your family, open up your laptop and get back in the shared work experience and keep working.”

For other leaders, having structured work–life balance is non negotiable. In fact, Laxman Narasimhan, the former CEO of Starbucks, previously told Fortune he’s “very disciplined about balance.”

“It’s got to be a pretty high bar to keep me away from the family.”

Scientists Just Challenged a 50-Year-Old Theory About How Evolution Works

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In bacteria and yeast, a gene’s importance did not predict its mutation rate, challenging the idea that evolution gives vital DNA preferential treatment.

Not all genes are equally important. Some are essential for survival, controlling basic functions such as copying DNA and keeping cells alive. Others can be lost with little or no effect. But does that mean evolution has found a way to shield its most vital genes from potentially harmful mutations?

For decades, scientists have debated whether essential genes are naturally less likely to mutate. Now, researchers at the University of Bath have put that idea to the test, with results that challenge a long-standing evolutionary hypothesis.

Dr. Sofia Radrizzani and Professor Laurence D. Hurst, from the university’s Centre for Evolution, examined spontaneous mutations in the bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) and baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). Their study, published November 12 in PLOS Biology, found no evidence that essential genes receive extra protection and offers a new explanation for why earlier research may have suggested otherwise.

“We’d expect natural selection to rapidly remove mutations when they occur in more important genes, because these mutations are more likely to be harmful to the organism. This can create the illusion that important genes have fewer mutations when, in reality, some mutations have simply disappeared before we have a chance to analyze them – what you can think of as ‘ghost mutations’,” Hurst said.

To reduce that problem, the team used data from “mutation accumulation” experiments designed to minimize the effects of natural selection. Three separate tests found no evidence that selection had substantially skewed the results.

“This looks like the largest and cleanest sample of mutations assembled to date,” Radrizzani said.

The researchers compared mutation rates with experimental evidence showing what happens when individual genes are removed. Deleting an essential gene kills the cell, while losing other genes has little or no effect. This gave the team a way to test whether genes a cell depends on most were less likely to mutate.

“Analyze the data any which way and we found nothing. In neither species did these key genes have a lower mutation rate,” Hurst said.

The team also assessed importance through the amount of a gene’s protein the cell produces and the strength of natural selection acting on the gene over evolutionary time.

“We found that genes producing more abundant proteins don’t have lower mutation rates. Nor does the way genes evolve over thousands of years predict how frequently they mutate,” Radrizzani said.

These findings address a debate that began in 1972 and resurfaced as recently as 2023. At stake is the relationship between the two steps in the conventional account of evolution. Mutations first generate genetic variation. Natural selection and other forces then act on that variation, favoring some traits over others.

Darwin’s finches, which Charles Darwin encountered on the Galápagos Islands in 1835, offer a familiar example. Random mutations contribute to differences in beak size, allowing birds to use different food sources. When conditions favor particular foods, birds with beaks better suited to eating them are more likely to survive and reproduce. Their traits consequently become more common over generations.

The disputed proposal suggests that a gene’s importance also influences how often mutations arise in it. Vital genes would receive greater protection, while genes where change could help adaptation would mutate more frequently. Examples of the latter include genes in which mutations give bacteria antibiotic resistance or alter birds’ beak dimensions.

There is a theoretical difficulty with that proposal. Gene mutation rates are already so low that reducing them further for individual genes would offer very little benefit, making it difficult for natural selection to favor such a protective system.

“The claim that mutation is slower for important genes is a triumph of hype over substance,” said Radrizzani, who co-wrote the paper as part of her PhD. “It’s a really old suggestion and is reinvented in various forms routinely.”

Genes copied later during DNA replication did tend to mutate more frequently in the team’s analysis, showing that some features of the genome can help explain differences in mutation rates.

“While we see known causes of variation in mutation rates, we found no evidence for the effects that have been proposed as a challenge to the conventional model. None predicted which genes would have high or low mutation rates, and there are certainly no grounds for a shift in paradigm,” Hurst said.

Gen Z’s job crisis has reached the ultrarich. Wealth advisors say billionaire parents are now planning for kids who may struggle to land and keep jobs

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even the wealthiest parents in America are worried about their kids’ financial futures. The unemployment rate for recent college graduates has held at about 5.6%, and 42.5% of recent grads were underemployed at the end of 2025, the highest share since 2020. A college degree no longer guarantees a steady paycheck, and economists warn AI could upend the entry-level work that recent grads have historically done.

In a world where seemingly no one is immune to the impacts of AI on the workforce and an increasingly stiff job market, ultra-high net worth people are starting to have the same concerns as middle-class and low-income families: Will my kid be able to get a job and support themself?

“Billionaires have the financial resources to support their children, but they sometimes struggle to determine what else is needed for their children to succeed,” Tom Thiegs, managing director of leadership and legacy at Ascent Private Capital Management with U.S. Bank, told Fortune earlier this year.

Millionaires and billionaires “are recognizing this is not the same game they had to play,” wealth manager Patrick Dwyer told CNBC earlier this year. “Families have to rethink … what it means to support their children. And we’re not talking about spoiling your kids. We’re talking about: What if your kid needs retraining at 33?” Dwyer is managing director at Aligned by NewEdge Wealth, a boutique wealth management firm based in Miami. He works with clients with net worths between roughly $100 million and more than $1 billion.

Dwyer said his clients are concerned their children, typically between age 22 and 35, are struggling to secure and hang on to jobs that are historically associated with security and status, including technology, law, and health care. That means the wealthy will have to plan for a future in which they’re passing on more of their fortune to their kids.

“[They’re] realizing that if they don’t pass on more meaningful wealth to their children, or their children are not able to accumulate wealth … their kids could have [less] agency over their lives than they did,” he told CNBC.

While this may sound like an irrational fear from America’s wealthiest, it’s a reality more wealth managers recognize.

“This is a very real concern I’m hearing from ultra-affluent families right now,” Thiegs said. “On the surface it can sound irrational: ‘Why would a billionaire worry about their child getting a job?’ But realistically, no matter how much money you have, parents still want their children to succeed and lead fulfilled lives.”

The job market jitters Gen Zers face directly impacts how their parents can help them plan for their financial futures.

But the crux of the problem isn’t that parents fear they won’t financially be able to help their children, it’s more that they’re worried they won’t have the same career outcomes and sense of fulfillment as past generations.

“They’re not usually worried about the financial security of their children; rather they worry that the job market will impact their child’s sense of purpose, identity, and confidence,” Thiegs said. “They also worry that significant wealth will dampen their drive or desire to work.”

But that doesn’t mean Thiegs encourages his clients just to bankroll their kids for the foreseeable future. Instead, estate planning, investing, and other long-term financial planning is a must.

“When parents are worried about their children’s job security, we recommend creating a system that provides opportunities for growth and development rather than just a financial safety net,” he said.

It’s more important to implement plans that support a child’s self worth than just their net worth, he added.

Trent Von Ahsen, a certified financial planner and managing partner at Cedar Point Capital Partners, also says his ultra-high net worth families view the risks of today’s job market as less of a financial stability concern, but more about whether they’re setting their kids up to be indefinitely dependent on them.

“This cohort of parents seem more concerned about over-supporting their children, than under-supporting them,” Von Ahsen told Fortune earlier this year.

The shift away from historically high-paying, white-collar jobs is already evident in the choices Gen Z is making. Facing mass layoffs in white‑collar sectors and anxious about AI, many young workers are peeling away from traditional corporate routes in favor of jobs they think might offer more control or faster cash—from becoming content creators to opting for blue-collar jobs in manufacturing, electrical work, and other technical work.

In some cases, college‑educated Gen Zers are even competing for six‑figure nanny and tutor roles in elite households, chasing financial “freedom” outside of a traditional office career. A 2026 Deloitte global survey found just 6% of Gen Z and millennial respondents said reaching a leadership position is their primary career goal. Instead, many say they favor stability, skill-building, and well-being over climbing the ladder quickly.

This all means, though, billionaires and other high-net worth individuals have to financially plan differently from how they might have in the past. They have to design financial plans that “encourage growth and responsibility” instead of only making large inheritances all at once, Von Ahsen said.

“We see more emphasis on education funding flexibility, mentorship, and phased wealth transfers,” he said. It’s “really an attitude moving toward providing opportunity without removing initiative.”
