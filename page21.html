Trump Justice Department Drops Attempt to Block Certification of Alaska Senate Election Result in "Voter Confusion" Case

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

I previously wrote about the Trump Justice Department's lawsuit seeking to block certification of the Alaska Senate election results, and the district court ruling rejecting it.  The Trump DOJ argued that blocking the  results was necessary to prevent "voter confusion" caused by the presence of two candidates with very similar names on the ballot. As I  explained previously, Republican candidate Dan J. Sullivan can potentially be confused with Dan S. Sullivan (the incumbent GOP senator), and some voters might have accidentally chosen Dan J. even though they intended to back Dan S.

Today, as reported by prominent election law scholar Rick Hasen, DOJ filed a brief in the US Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit seeking to dismiss the case. They also want the district court ruling vacated for procedural reasons (I will not opine on whether that's warranted).

This brings to an end a lawsuit that was always a turkey of a case (though perhaps calling it that is an insult to turkeys!). As explained in my post on the district court ruling and in an excellent amicus brief filed by Rick Hasen, the case never had any merit, and a court decision ruling in its favor would have set a dangerous precedent.

In addition, the election results show that Democratic candidate Mary Peltola got an absolute majority of the vote (about 50.1%), more than both Dan Sullivans and all other candidates combined. Thus, even if all of the 1.7% of voters who chose Dan J. actually intended to back Dan S., it made no difference. Alaska's system of ranked choice voting (under which, if no candidate gets a majority in the first round, votes get reallocated) might have further reduced any problem. But no reallocation took place here, because Peltola got an absolute majority to begin with. This is likely what led DOJ to finally thrown in the towel.

As noted in one of my earlier posts about the case, Democrats may well deserve blame for seeking to deceive voters in this way, even though it was never likely to change the result (evidence indicates Dan J. may have gotten on the ballot as part of a Democratic effort to siphon off voters from Dan S.). But for reasons outlined in the same post, this kind of thing is far from the worst example of exploitation of voter ignorance.

Peltola's win was mostly just part of the broader "Blue tsunami" which saw the Democratis gain 9 Senate seats, including in several traditional red states. But perhaps Peltola got over the top because of the immense popularity of her pet lizard! We could use more lizards on Capitol Hill.

Ledger’s crypto wallet implant scare spreads to Berlin as Dubai cases come under investigation

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ledger offers X-ray inspections for suspect wallets as its hardware tampering investigation expands to Berlin, Dubai and Southeast Asia.

Ledger is offering limited-time X-ray inspections for suspect hardware wallets as its tampering investigation reaches Berlin.

In a Nov. 18 update, the wallet maker said its support team can arrange for customers to send a device to its factory in Vierzon, France, for inspection and return.

The offer lets owners have their device examined for physical alterations. For anyone who has already used a suspect device, Ledger advised moving funds to a wallet with a fresh recovery phrase. The company also said in its advisory that the hardware it found was designed to capture those words during setup.

Ledger noted that the German cases it had investigated were localized to Berlin. The company advised concerned customers who bought a Ledger wallet from a third party there from January 2026 onward to contact its support team.

It also asks concerned buyers who purchased from third parties in Southeast Asia or Dubai to get in touch.

Ledger revealed it is still investigating reports involving two devices in Dubai and is working with distribution partners to learn how they entered circulation. It reported one affected Nano Gen 5 as it continues its investigation into Southeast Asian resellers.

The advisory described the inspection as an option for devices that raise concerns, so customers can ask Ledger Support to arrange secure shipment through an official ticket.

Ledger recommended sending a device only through that channel, and that support will never ask for a recovery phrase. Its guidance for buyers in Berlin, Dubai, and Southeast Asia describes when to contact Ledger, while support handles the arrangements for individual devices sent to the factory.

The inspection can look inside the submitted device for added hardware, since Ledger’s Genuine Check verifies the secure chip's authenticity. Where that chip remains intact, the check can miss a physical addition.

Ledger reported that all the devices it confirmed in this incident carried the same physical modification and that the investigation found no flaw in its secure chip or operating system.

In its Oct. 15 disclosure, Ledger said genuine wallets had been altered after leaving its control and distributed through Southeast Asian resellers.

The company said the samples it examined from stores, warehouses, and its own stock at the time were clean. In November, it described the Berlin findings as part of the same incident and that it still had no confirmed case involving a device bought directly from ledger.com.

Ledger advised owners of an unused suspect device to leave it unopened, keep it out of use, and contact official support. They should avoid using it to set up a new wallet or restore an existing one.

For owners who have already used a suspect device, Ledger advised moving funds to a new wallet with a fresh recovery phrase, generated on a device bought directly from its website. It also recommended avoiding reusing any passphrase used on the suspect device.

6 Shows To Watch if You Love 'Sunrise on the Reaping'

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Just under two years after the release of the book, and after much anticipation, The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping is now officially in theaters. Sunrise on the Reaping is the second Hunger Games prequel, this time focused on the 50th Hunger Games and the Second Quarter Quell. It follows a young Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada), all the way through his games and the devastating aftermath.

After watching Sunrise on the Reaping, viewers will likely just want to go back and rewatch the five movies that came before it. After that, though, there are a number of excellent shows that fans of Sunrise on the Reaping should add to their lists. From other series that put young people in situations with impossibly high stakes, to shows featuring actors from the movie, these are TV shows to watch if you loved Sunrise on the Reaping.

The Wilds follows a group of eight teenage girls whose plane crashes on a deserted island on the way to an empowerment retreat for young women. Each of the girls has someone there whom they know, at least to some degree, from back home. They are all reluctant to accept that they're trapped there, but they soon have to figure out how to work together so that they can all survive and find a way out. The story is told through three timelines: the girls' time on the island, flashbacks to the events that led each of them there, and flash-forwards to their post-rescue interviews about what happened. There's just one twist that the girls don't know, though: they were brought there on purpose for an experiment, and even the plane crash was fake.

Like Sunrise on the Reaping, The Wilds follows a group of teenagers trying to survive in an environment that is not conducive to it, the main difference being that the girls on the island are working together instead of fighting each other. The Wilds also shows adults manipulating these teenagers' lives and orchestrating their suffering for their own purposes, not caring who dies or gets hurt in order to make their point. For Sunrise on the Reaping fans looking for another intense story that shows teens trying to survive in circumstances orchestrated by power-hungry adults, The Wilds is the perfect next watch.

Lenore Dove Baird (Whitney Peak) is instrumental to Haymitch's story. She believes in the possibility of a better future one day, and her hope and determination serve as an inspiration to Haymitch in even his darkest moments. For Sunrise on the Reaping fans who want to watch another Whitney Peak project next after seeing her play Lenore Dove, the Gossip Girl reboot is a great choice. In the series, Peak plays one of the main characters, a main character named Zoya Lott. Zoya is new to both New York City and Constance Billard, and she's there to get to know and have a relationship with her half-sister, Julien (Jordan Alexander).

Gossip Girl is much lower stakes than Sunrise on the Reaping, but it also centers on the complex emotions of teenagers whose lives are being manipulated by adults in power. The reboot has a compelling twist, in which Gossip Girl's identity is not kept a secret; the new account is controlled by a group of teachers who are looking for a new way to keep the students in line. As a result, the teachers cause a great deal of harm to Zoya, Julien, and their friends, whom most of the Gossip Girl posts are about.

The Society follows a group of high schoolers from the wealthy town of West Ham, Connecticut. One day, they all get on buses to go away for a school retreat, but they return that night because the road is blocked off. When they get back, though, everyone else is gone. They're the only ones left in their town, and they are unable to leave or contact anyone outside of it. Cut off from civilization, these teenagers decide to build their own society in the meantime. They call this new version of their town "New Ham," and they fight each other on how to best conserve resources and ensure their survival. They also try to investigate what happened to them and see if they can find a way to contact the outside world.

The Society, like Sunrise on the Reaping, is an intense story about teenagers trying to survive in incredibly dangerous and horrific conditions. Early on in this situation, the teens in The Society start enacting violence on one another, and multiple people die due to the disagreements caused by their conditions. The Society is an emotional and riveting series, and it is a great next watch for Sunrise on the Reaping fans looking for something fast-paced that will absolutely break their hearts.

Cady has spent every summer of her life on Beechwood Island, the private island owned by her family. The series is set over the course of two summers: the present one, where Cady is trying to get back her memories from last summer after a head injury, and last summer, where everything fell apart ahead of the mysterious incident where Cady got injured.

Cady's three best friends are the rest of a group that calls themselves "the Liars": her cousins, Johnny (Zada) and Mirren (Esther McGregor), and the nephew of Johnny's mother's boyfriend, Gat (Shubham Maheshwari). Squid Game is an intense and shocking series that keeps viewers on the edges of their seats from start to finish. Like Sunrise on the Reaping, Squid Game puts its protagonist in a dangerous game surrounded by people who are willing to kill both him and each other in order to win. Both Sunrise on the Reaping and Squid Game remind its viewers, though, that the real villains are the ones who set up this system in the first place.

Based on the series of novels of the same name by Kass Morgan, The 100 is a post-apocalyptic teen drama that takes place nearly a century after a nuclear apocalypse that destroyed Earth and killed most of the population. Now, society is thriving on a massive space station that orbits Earth – but it's not a perfect system, and their time left is limited. As an experiment to see if Earth is once again habitable, 100 juvenile delinquents are forcibly sent down to live there.

The 100, like Sunrise on the Reaping, follows teenagers who have been put into a dangerous situation due to circumstances outside of their control. They all just want to survive, but they disagree on how to best do that, and this often escalates to violence. Clashing that would otherwise be low-stakes costs a number of characters their lives because of these circumstances, in a way that will seem familiar to fans of Sunrise on the Reaping.

GM’s V2G pilot wants to pay California drivers for power from parked EVs

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

GM Energy and WeaveGrid are launching a vehicle-to-grid (V2G) pilot that will let select Southern California Edison (SCE) customers send electricity from their EV batteries back to the grid. Eligible drivers could get more than $16,000 in help with equipment and installation, and earn money when the grid needs their power.

GM says the pilot participants will be its first EV drivers in California to export power from their cars to their homes. Through the California Energy Commission, eligible customers in SCE’s Emergency Load Reduction Program can get up to $8,000 toward a GM Energy V2H system and up to $8,099 toward eligible installation costs, including connecting the system to the grid.

Chevy, GMC, and Cadillac EV drivers can use time-of-use energy management to take advantage of lower rates and may be paid for participating in eligible demand-response events. How much they save or earn depends on their rate plan, electricity use, and participation.

GM already offers managed charging through 13 utilities, and each program varies by utility.

An EV battery stores direct current (DC), and your house and the grid use alternating current (AC). GM’s current home backup system uses a wall-mounted inverter to convert the power. With AC bidirectional charging, the car’s onboard inverter does the job. Moving that work into the car could make the equipment on your garage wall simpler and cheaper to install.

GM is joining CharIN‘s North American AC V2G task force, which is working to align technical standards, equipment certification, and utility approval requirements. In Southern California, the pilot will give GM and its partners a look at how the process works for drivers, from signing up and getting the hardware installed to getting utility approval and sending power back.

Te Araroa day 22: Auckland here we come!

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

27.5km, Mangawhai trail host to Pakiri Paradise

Most of us (me, Lindsay, Sinead, Annabel and Anica) got going at around 7am again, although this time with good reason as we were expecting a bit of a longer day and needed to make a tidal river crossing later. The place we were staying was run by people who had previously done TA and provided everything hikers could need, at the very reasonable price of $10 per night. It was a great place to stay, and we walked out of the gate practically right back onto trail. We had been talking about coffee, but didn’t think that anything would be open at that time. But luck was on our side! Literally 50 metres down the street there was a coffee and smoothie van. What a great start to the day. While I was at university I was very much fuelled by coffee, but since then I’ve mostly switched to decaf. But I have been enjoying drinking it again while I’ve been hiking – it gives me a great energy boost, which puts me in a good mood, and I think the negative side effects I’ve experienced previously are reduced by having physical activity to funnel that energy into.

I found a friend when I went to pack away my tent

Coffee obtained, it was going to be a good morning!

Unfortunately, the day started with a decent road walk of about 7km, which I don’t think any of us had quite registered was coming. Eventually we made it to the beach we would be walking along for the greater part of the day. The signage was familiar to me and I didn’t quite realise why until I registered that it was Auckland Council signage. We had made it to Auckland region! The Northland/Auckland boundary is just south of Mangawhai so that morning, we had completed the first region of Te Araroa. Northland is one of the longest (perhaps the longest?) regions on TA, owing to its long and thin geography. So this was a significant milestone. It was also probably the region I was least looking forward to, yet I loved it. Not every minute, of course, but distance had made my heart grow fonder and I was able to appreciate the beauty of the place with fresh eyes.

The beach looked pleasingly short when we got onto it. That is, until I realised that the headland I could see was not the end of the beach section, but just a blip a few kilometres in. Still, the first section went pretty quickly and soon we were approaching the headland. There were hoards of surfers and foil board surfers, which looked pretty cool! There was also an interesting set up on the beach. Evidently a photoshoot was going on – a bed was set up, and a model posed on it in pajamas while a drone hovered and various involved people fluttered around. I’m sure the six dirty hikers walking through the background didn’t improve their shots!

The first part of the beach, looking towards Te Arai point

Up on Te Arai Point, there were awesome views along the coast. The tracks weren’t super well-marked though, and the route was a bit un-intuitive. I was with Lindsay and Sinead at this point, and eventually we reached a wooded section that popped out on a road back to the beach. Just before the road we stumbled (almost literally) upon Zoe, who was taking a break in what she pointed out might be the last shade for a while. We plopped down to join her, and collectively decided that the rest of the beach necessitated podcasts. I realised I had forgotten to download more episodes when I last had wifi, and scrambled around for something to listen to. I tried an audiobook but I wasn’t really getting into it, and stopped to switch to music and try to find the specific song I had in my head that morning (u + me = <3 by Olivia Rodrigo, in case you were wondering). Tunes acquired, I set off again.

This beach was actually perhaps the most annoying we have had to walk down so far. The tide was fully in at this point and the available area to walk on was soft, slopey, and narrow in places. There was one spot where the waves had created a bit of a cliff about 75cm tall, with very little space between the top of the cliff and the dunes behind. My feet were already wet from a stream crossing earlier, so I just decided to commit and walk in the water below the cliff. Apparently some of the others had taken their chances with the top of the cliff, with fairly unsuccessful results. It was a shame they were behind me, as the descriptions they gave of their falls were pretty amusing!

Walking over Te Arai point, with the surfers in the background

The other notable thing about the beach was the number of dead birds we came across. I really haven’t seen that many before. I hope it isn’t bird flu, which has recently arrived in NZ – perhaps it’s just something to do with the ocean currents that lots of dead stuff washes up there?

At about 11:45 I was hungry. I was going to grab a snack and keep going, but realised I might as well stop for lunch. Who says lunch can’t happen before 12, anyway? I was hungry so I might as well eat properly. I enjoyed my wraps with fresh salad, although I kept dropping things that then got covered in sand. Annabel had told me about these sun-dried tomatoes that come without oil, and I had bought some in Mangawhai. They were great, 10/10 reccommend those. While I was lunching, the others all passed me. After a few more kilometres of walking, I came across everyone sat on the beach eating their (late) lunch. We were nearly at the tidal estuary, which the trail notes said could be crossed 90 minutes either side of low tide. It was 2pm, and low tide wasn’t until nearly 5. But the others reckoned someone else had crossed outside of the reccommended window, and we decided to go and have a look.

The butterflies were having a good dance in my stomach as we approached the estuary. I find rivers quite scary, especially when I don’t know how deep they’ll be. We went to the mouth, as that’s where the smaller streams on the beach had been shallowest. But the water here looked quite fast-flowing, so we went further up to a wider, slower bend. It was impossible to tell how deep it was, but at least we wouldn’t get swept away. Sinéad offered to be the guinea pig. She went up to her ankles, then her knees – and then she was basically across. No problem! We all followed without difficulty. Sometimes the trail notes really overstate the difficulty of a thing. I guess it can be deeper sometimes – but we crossed 3 hours before low tide with no bother, when the notes suggested 90 minutes. One of the most valuable pieces of advice I got about TA, which I tell to everyone, came from a couple of NOBO hikers that I picked up near Nelson Lakes last season. They said that there is a lot of scare-mongering from other hikers, but you have to see for yourself and not put too much stock in what everyone else is saying. And apparently the same goes for the trail notes, too!

The crossing was so fine! Zoe wading across with me, Lindsay, Annabel and Anica waiting. Photo by Sinéad

Putting shoes and socks back on after the beach

We walked up the beach towards a holiday park. We were planning to go a bit further for the day but hoped they would sell us some cold drinks, and maybe fill some water bottles. It was a bit unclear where to go, but there was a man mowing the grass. ‘You lot look like an invading army!’ he called down to us. I suppose the six of us did look like we were plotting how to storm the castle! He invited us to come through the campground and rinse out our shoes and socks. After my sandpaper socks the other day, this was a very welcome invitation. He also said that the notes don’t give good advice about where to cross the river we had just been over. It’s much shallower inland, but the area is home to rare nesting birds, so DOC won’t allow the TA trust to tell people to cross further inland. Personally, I’m happy to wait a bit for the tides if it means not disturbing the birds. But I can see that it’s frustrating for the locals to see people being given less-good safety advice.

After washing our feet, we all headed over to the shop where we bought ice creams and cold drinks, and checked out the next bit of the route. Everyone kept saying it was really steep, but for some reason I didn’t think it looked too bad on the map. Then I decided to look at a different map app, the one I normally use, and the contour lines looked way denser! I realised that the contours on the TA app are really light and narrow compared to the ones on the map I’m used to, so things don’t look as steep. I guess that could be helpful, in some ways.

After a while, we headed off down the road. It was a fairly quiet road, and it wasn’t too long before we reached some track. The hill began immediately, and it was steep but not impossible. I ended up passing the others at the first flattish bit and continued on up. The views were pretty good and I felt happy about my fitness. I had a full 4 day resupply on my back but I found the hill ok, and I was able to keep plugging on without having to stop too often. Towards the top, there was a large fallen tree that I had to scramble over. Then I realised I was at the turn off for where we would be staying, and stopped to wait for the others. I watched them all clamber over the tree with varying levels of elegance. I would have taken photos but I didn’t want to walk back down the hill.

You can just see the others lower down the hill

The house we were staying at was a couple of hundred metres along a side-track. Annabel and I got there first, and the couple came out and introduced themselves, and asked where we were from. Annabel is also from southern England, to which the guy responded that we were both ‘bloody Southerners’; he’s from Yorkshire! The rest of the group arrived and they showed us where we could pitch our tents. It wasn’t as luxurious as the previous night, but we had everything we needed. And, they showed us the view of Auckland city from their garden! This was the first time we had seen the city, and although it still looked very far away, it was cool to know we were getting closer. Everyone was planning to take some time off or visit friends in Auckland, and it’s also the first major city on the route. So it seems to have become a big milestone in everyone’s minds.

You can’t see it, but trust me when I say that the Auckland city skyline was visible in this view

The weather was threatening rain so we pitched our tents, which were soaked from the morning dew still. I managed to snag a decent spot, but some of the others were less lucky and Annabel realised she had pitched her tent in a bit of a bog. Our hosts came out with a packet of scorched almonds, which was very nice of them! A little while later, Xavier showed up. He was at the campsite the previous night, but I didn’t end up speaking to him. Some of the others knew him from before, but I hadn’t met him so I took the chance to introduce myself. Then we carefully prepared dinner on the tables and chairs that kept sinking into the mud, and sat around talking for a while before calling it a night.

A Common Heart Failure Warning Sign May Actually Be Causing Dangerous Blood Clots

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A hormone used to detect heart trouble can also promote dangerous blood clots in experiments, pointing scientists toward a possible way to prevent blockages without disrupting normal healing.

Heart disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States, claiming a life nearly every 34 seconds. For people living with heart failure, a condition in which the heart struggles to pump enough blood throughout the body, doctors often rely on blood tests to detect signs of cardiac stress. Now, researchers have discovered that one of these familiar warning signals may also contribute to a dangerous complication: blood clots that can block circulation and trigger life-threatening events.

The substance is a hormone called B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP). Doctors routinely measure BNP and related protein fragments because elevated levels can indicate heart failure or other cardiovascular problems. Until now, BNP has largely been viewed as a marker of an overworked heart rather than a potential contributor to disease.

Researchers at the Texas A&M University Irma Lerma Rangel College of Pharmacy, part of Texas A&M Health, have found that elevated BNP can directly activate blood platelets and accelerate clot formation. The study, published in Blood, identifies a molecular pathway that could eventually offer a more targeted way to prevent dangerous blood clots.

“We thought that BNP might not only act as a passive marker but actively contributes to the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular disease,” said Dr. Zhenyu Li, a professor of pharmaceutical sciences who led the research.

Platelets normally protect the body from blood loss. When a blood vessel is damaged, these tiny blood components gather at the injury and stick together to help stop bleeding. Problems arise when platelets become overly active and form clots inside blood vessels where they are not needed. Those clots can obstruct circulation, depriving tissues and organs of oxygen, and contribute to emergencies such as heart attacks and pulmonary embolisms, which occur when a clot blocks blood flow in the lungs.

Li’s team traced BNP’s effects to a receptor on the surface of platelets called natriuretic peptide receptor-A (NPRA). Receptors allow cells to respond to specific chemical signals, and the researchers found that BNP activates platelets by binding directly to NPRA.

“It was previously unknown whether platelets even expressed this receptor,” Li explained. “We are the first team to investigate whether BNP directly activates platelets through the NPRA receptor.”

Once BNP attaches to NPRA, it increases the amount of a signaling molecule called cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) inside platelets. This sets off a chain of cellular responses that makes platelets more likely to stick together and promotes clot growth. In experimental studies involving blood-vessel injury and pulmonary embolism, elevated BNP markedly accelerated clot formation.

The researchers also examined what happened when NPRA was blocked or its gene was removed. Under those conditions, BNP could no longer increase cGMP levels or activate platelets, and the experiments showed protection against abnormal clotting.

“Knocking out the NPRA gene protected against these complications, proving the clotting process is mediated by the NPRA receptor,” Li said.

Blood samples from patients with heart rhythm disorders provided additional evidence. In these patients, higher circulating BNP levels were associated with greater platelet activity. The finding supports a possible connection between BNP and clotting risk in humans, although the association alone does not establish that BNP directly causes blood clots in patients.

People with elevated BNP are already known to face a higher risk of serious cardiovascular events, including heart attacks and pulmonary embolisms. The newly identified pathway offers a possible biological explanation for that relationship. Li emphasized that the discovery emerged from years of work examining platelet activity and cardiovascular disease.

“It has been an ongoing, gradual progression—not a sudden breakthrough, but a logical continuation of our research over the past two decades,” he said.

The findings could have implications for how dangerous blood clots are prevented. Existing antiplatelet medications reduce the ability of platelets to form clots, but their effects throughout the body can increase the risk of serious internal bleeding. By targeting the interaction between BNP and NPRA, researchers hope to develop treatments that interfere with harmful clot formation while preserving the normal clotting needed to heal injuries.

Such treatments remain experimental. Li suggested that, in the meantime, physicians may need to monitor patients with elevated BNP more closely and consider whether antiplatelet therapy could help reduce their risk of clot-related complications.

Several questions also remain about the mechanism. Although platelets appear central to BNP-driven clotting, the researchers still need to establish how much of the effect comes specifically from NPRA receptors on platelets rather than receptors on other cells. Future studies will selectively remove NPRA from different cell types to clarify their respective roles.

“Platelets appear to be major contributors, and to confirm this further, we need to develop tissue-specific models,” Li said.

The team is now working with Dr. Wenshe Liu, Regents Professor and Harry E. Bovay, Jr. Chair in Chemistry at the Texas A&M College of Arts and Sciences, to identify small molecules capable of blocking the BNP-NPRA interaction. Promising compounds will undergo further testing to determine whether they can prevent BNP-induced platelet activation and thrombosis without broadly disrupting healthy clotting.

“Because patients with high BNP levels face the greatest risk, targeting this pathway opens new opportunities to reduce thrombotic complications while minimizing standard side effects like severe bleeding,” Li said.

Regulators approve new agreement to ensure data centers pay for their own energy costs

SALEM, Oregon — The Oregon Public Utility Commission has approved an agreement between advocates and Pacific Power. Now, Pacific Power customers have a strong suite of protections against data centers’ impact on Oregonians’ energy bills.

Starting Jan. 1, 2027, new Pacific Power data centers will be held responsible for their own energy costs. These new rules will only apply to future data centers and existing ones making significant investments, which regulators identified as the most pressing challenge to address. Right now, the utility has very few existing data centers on its system.

“With a long line of data centers waiting at Pacific Power’s door, I’m confident that we have the right protections at the right time,” said Bob Jenks, Executive Director of Oregon Citizens’ Utility Board. “We were able to stop more extreme impacts on power bills just in time.”

Data centers over 100 MW will also have to pay a small surcharge to fund energy efficiency upgrades for low-income households. This includes funding for deep home retrofits, lowering energy use across the grid while saving customers on monthly bills.

“Many of the households we serve are already struggling to afford their energy bills and risk having their utilities shut off. Data centers will help fund weatherization, energy efficiency upgrades, and the critical home repairs that often prevent our clients from accessing these improvements in the first place,” said Charity Fain, Executive Director of Community Energy Project. “Requiring data centers to cover the costs of the energy infrastructure they need, rather than passing those costs on to residential customers, is an important protection for low-income households, seniors, and people with disabilities.”

Under this order, if there is a power emergency, Pacific Power can prioritize critical energy use for other customers, like renters and homeowners, by curtailing service to data centers. Increased electric demand by data centers strains the grid and increases the potential for blackouts during heat waves or hard freezes.

“This is a big win that puts Oregon at the forefront of managing data center growth the right way,” said Joshua Basofin, Oregon Clean Energy Program Director at Climate Solutions. “It protects Oregonians from rising costs and strengthens grid resilience. Data centers should not harm our ability to access essential electricity to power our lives and keep us safe all year.”

Regulators also ruled that new data centers can only be connected to Pacific Power’s grid if there is enough emissions-free electricity available to serve them. Under HB 2021, Pacific Power is required by law to serve all customers with 100% emissions-free electricity by 2040. This new rule will help manage costs for other customers outside of data centers during the transition to clean energy.

“We have long known that clean energy is the most affordable form of energy,” said Ben Brint, Senior Climate Program Director at Oregon Environmental Council. “This ruling is a win for data center accountability, and for Oregonians across the state — especially frontline communities already bearing the brunt of climate impacts and rising energy costs. It will save ratepayers money in the short and long run, allocating costs appropriately while advancing Oregon’s 100% clean energy goals.”

This change also comes at a time when many Oregonians are calling for broader changes to how the state handles data centers. Impacts on data centers are poised to be an issue again at the Oregon State Legislature in 2027.

“The POWER Act demonstrated the people’s power to fight back against the rising energy rates and ecosystem harms that large data centers inflict,” said Emily Bowes, Policy Strategist & Acting Director at Sierra Club Oregon. “In 2027, alongside new and returning climate champions, we will continue to fight for a clean energy transition in Salem and statewide.”

In 2025, the Oregon Legislature passed the POWER Act to help utilities rein in data centers’ added costs to Oregonians’ energy bills. Last fall, Portland General Electric began the work to roll out this new law in practice. In January, Pacific Power started a similar process for its customers. The next step is to address the few existing data centers on Pacific Power’s system, starting as early as summer 2027. It is unclear if the final phase will include rate adjustments.

Democracy Depends on Being Able to Forgive

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Jessica Schrader

In a 60 Minutes interview broadcast this month, President Trump was asked a profound question that rarely comes up in political interviews: Could he forgive his enemies? If entering heaven required him to feel love and forgiveness for James Comey or Nancy Pelosi, could he do it?

“The answer is no,” the president said. “I want to be honest, no.”

We should resist the tempting thought that only Donald Trump feels this way. We all need to look in the mirror: How willing are we to forgive the people we believe have done us wrong?

For most of us, grievance has a powerful pull—whether it’s rehearsing an argument or replaying a hurtful remark over and over in our heads. In psychology, we can understand forgiveness as a voluntary change in how we feel and act toward someone who’s hurt us in the past. It can loosen the hold of resentment on our attention and, in turn, help us choose how to respond in the future.

These days, the word forgiveness can sound like a concept out of a Sunday school class or a therapy session. But it’s also a prerequisite for a functioning democratic society.

A country needs ways to respond to wrongdoing and still leave open the possibility of a shared future. When every injury becomes a reason for retaliation, the work of governing turns into settling scores.

Here’s the thing about democracy: it’s a continuing relationship among fallible people. It depends on our ability to correct errors, acknowledge wrongdoing and work together again. Forgiveness is a vital civic skill because democratic societies have to do more than settle who was right in the last conflict. Forgiveness makes healthy debate and cooperation possible.

I once asked the legendary Archbishop Desmond Tutu what forgiveness meant. He simply said, “It means I no longer demand payback from you.”

When he was chair of South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, Tutu helped bring apartheid-era abuses to public attention. He underscored the principle that forgiveness isn’t blanket absolution. Truth, accountability and repair can coexist with refusing permanent vengeance. In our own lives, we can forgive someone while maintaining boundaries, including choosing not to resume a relationship.

When I interviewed former Colombian president and Nobel Peace laureate Juan Manuel Santos for my book On Belonging, he recalled advice to recognise an adversary’s humanity in war and peace. His 2016 agreement with the FARC opened a path for former guerrillas to enter into democratic politics.

This month, delivering the Nelson Mandela Annual Lecture in South Africa, Santos told the story of Pastora Mira. She cared for a wounded man who then confessed to torturing and killing her son. She embraced him, saying his acceptance of responsibility had freed her from the prison of lifelong hatred. It’s a breathtaking example. Such forgiveness remains a personal choice.

It’s important that we avoid thinking that the psychological grip of contempt is unique to any one country. Others—including my home country of Canada—shouldn’t think they’re immune to a politics of contempt. A 2025 Angus Reid Institute–Cardus survey found 87% of Conservative supporters regarded the Liberals as a threat to Canada’s well-being. And 84% of Liberal supporters said the same of Conservatives.

The philosopher Hannah Arendt understood forgiveness as a way to release people from being bound indefinitely to what they’ve done, allowing something new to begin. For her, forgiveness was inextricably linked to respect and to what I call belonging—that we honour people’s humanity, even if we disagree with their preferences.

Earlier this month, Texans elected James Talarico, a Presbyterian seminarian, to the U.S. Senate. He’s spoken eloquently about loving his enemies and seeing opponents as children of God. It’s a message that draws on a Christian tradition that asks us to extend the mercy we ourselves need—a tradition Martin Luther King Jr. brought into public life more than half a century ago.

Ultimately, forgiveness is a skill we can learn. In an international study of more than 4,500 adults, people given a short program to reflect on hurt and consider another person’s perspective reported greater forgiveness and fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety after two weeks than those waiting to begin. The study concerned personal relationships, but it offers reason to take forgiveness seriously as something we can actively cultivate.

We can all put this into practice. Name what happened and how it honestly affected your feelings. Try to consider the other person’s perspective, and think of a time you needed forgiveness for yourself. Give yourself time and space for your feelings to change. Choosing to forgive can precede feeling the emotion of forgiveness.

Let’s face it. In failing to forgive his enemies, Donald Trump isn’t so much of an outlier. But that needs to change. To build a healthy democratic society, we can’t have permanent enemies. We have to build a future together. That work can begin in our own relationships.
