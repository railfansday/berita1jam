Two-Thirds of Dementia Content On TikTok Is Misleading, Researchers Say

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A new survey suggests that about two-thirds of dementia advice videos on TikTok are misleading

Of the videos examined, the analysis shows that nearly half contained inaccurate and potentially damaging information.

The researchers say that approximately 44% of TikTok creators who share dementia content lack credibility and appropriate qualifications.

Although misleading dementia content may appear on other social media platforms, this research examined only TikTok content, warranting further investigation.

The survey was commissioned by Homewatch Caregivers and has not been published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.

A spokesperson for TikTok told Healthline that if health-related content is potentially harmful, it is flagged for removal. The spokesperson added that 99.6% of misinformation deemed violative was proactively removed from the platform in 2026.

The spokesperson added that TikTok relies on fact-checking and guidance from public health authorities to help assess the accuracy of content. The platform may also apply warning labels to content that cannot be verified as accurate.

Looking to social media for health information is not uncommon. A recent poll reports that 31% of adults in the United States use social media at least once a month to look for health information and advice.

Experts advise seeking information about dementia from trusted sources, such as a primary care doctor and organizations like the National Institute on Aging (NIA) and the Alzheimer’s Association, rather than social media, where content creators who share advice often lack expertise.

“It’s normal to want answers quickly when you’re caring for someone living with dementia, and a short video from someone who seems to know what they’re talking about can be really convincing,” said lead researcher Jennifer Winarski, senior manager of care operations and quality for Homewatch CareGivers.

“Despite there being some genuinely helpful information out there, you don’t always know whether it’s backed by medical evidence, so we wanted to bring our own expertise to uncover whether popular TikToks can be trusted,” she told Healthline. “We hope our report will encourage people to be a bit more careful about what they act on.”

For the survey, researchers selected a sample of 500 of the most-watched dementia-related videos on TikTok with hashtags such as:

Researchers then transcribed the video data to assess its accuracy.

The researchers found that two-thirds (66.4%) of the popular TikTok videos contained misleading information. Of those videos, about half (47.6%) contained potentially damaging misleading dementia information.

“It’s clear that many of the creators mean well, but much of what we saw was personal opinion rather than proven information, and in a few cases, general good health tips were presented as if they were specifically helpful for individuals living with dementia,” Winarski said.

“If families act on that, they could end up spending time and money on things that won’t help — and in the worst of cases, it could delay getting the support their loved one really needs,” she added.

Dung Trinh, MD, an internist for MemorialCare Medical Group and chief medical officer of the Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, said it’s important to be aware of the volume of misleading dementia information on social media. Trinh was not involved in this survey.

Some people may be making decisions about their health based on incomplete, misleading, or incorrect information, he said.

“For example, someone might see a video claiming that a particular supplement can reverse Alzheimer’s disease or that certain memory problems automatically mean someone has dementia,” Trinh said.

“These messages can lead to unnecessary fear, false reassurance, wasted money, or delays in getting a proper diagnosis.”

“We are entering an exciting era in Alzheimer’s care, with advances in blood-based biomarkers, earlier detection, and treatments that may slow disease progression in appropriately selected patients,” Trinh continued.

“Getting an accurate diagnosis and receiving timely medical guidance are becoming increasingly important.”

The researchers examined the content creators behind the TikTok videos included in their analysis. They found that 44% of video creators behind the misleading videos had no relevant qualifications.

Rehan Aziz, MD, vice chair of education in the Department of Psychiatry at Hackensack Meridian Jersey Shore University Medical Center and associate professor of psychiatry and neurology at the Hackensack Meridian School of Medicine in New Jersey, said that content creators offering dementia advice should have relevant qualifications. Aziz wasn’t involved in the survey.

“Start by looking at the creator’s profile for a full name and a specific credential, such as MD, DO, PhD, or RN, along with a specialty,” Aziz told Healthline.

“For dementia, the most relevant experts include neurologists, geriatric psychiatrists, geriatricians, neuropsychologists, and nurses, including advanced practice nurses, or social workers who specialize in dementia care.”

“You can confirm a doctor’s license through your state’s licensing board website and check board certification at certificationmatters.org,” Aziz said.

As of 2020, more than 55 million people worldwide were living with dementia — a neurological condition that negatively affects the brain. Researchers predict this number will hit 78 million by 2030.

The increase in the number of people seeking dementia content online is a reflection of this trend.

When looking for accurate dementia information and guidance, Nestor Beltre, MD, a neurologist with Baptist Health Kenneth C. Griffin Neuroscience Institute, part of Baptist Health South Florida, said a great first step is to speak with their primary care physician. Beltre wasn’t involved in the survey.

“Memory changes can occur for many different reasons, and some causes, including vitamin deficiencies, depression, and certain medical conditions, may be treatable,” he told Healthline.

“Early evaluation can help identify what may be contributing to these changes and determine the most appropriate next steps. Depending on the individual’s symptoms, a neurologist, psychiatrist, or neuropsychologist can provide further evaluation and guidance,” Belter continued.

Beltre advised turning to trusted resources such as the Alzheimer’s Association and the National Institute on Aging, which can increase public understanding of memory-related conditions and available treatment options.

“The most important message is that patients and families have resources available to help them better understand and manage cognitive health,” he said.

In the classic Thanksgiving comedy Planes, Trains and Automobiles, hapless hero Del Griffith (played by the late, great John Candy) delivers an emotional riposte to Steve Martin’s uptight ad man Neal Page that stands as a beautifully dignified defense of kindness, empathy and self-love in the face of his own perceived shortcomings.

“You want to hurt me? Go right ahead if it makes you feel any better…I could be a cold, hard cynic like you. But I don’t like to hurt people’s feelings. You think what you want about me, I’m not changing. I… I like me. My wife likes me…because I’m the real article. What you see is what you get.”

For many of us around the holidays, particularly after a night of excess, Del and Neal’s exchange could read as our internal monologue – your kinder inner voice battling the internal bully who judges your every overindulgence. The jury’s out on whether the kind voice is ever as potent as Del’s is in that moment. But it should be, because when it comes to your health around the holidays, the bully has usually got it wrong.

The truth is, for most of us, one day of immoderation during Thanksgiving is unlikely to matter in the grand scheme. As such, you would do well to avoid the litany of holiday season health myths out there, many of which seem hell-bent on punishing you for taking a day off:

Like all misinformation, these kinds of medical myths are loud and tend to die hard, but they almost always trade on quick fixes that target the wrong thing.

A 2020 review of 23 observational studies found that most showed significant weight gain during festive periods, averaging roughly a pound and a half. One of the original studies, a landmark NIH study from 2000, found that much of that weight tends to stick around. So the question is not what you ate on Thanksgiving Thursday and how quickly you can “undo the damage,” but rather whether the extended holiday season becomes a two-month period where your healthy routine goes out the window. Staying upright for a couple of hours to help with digestion, particularly if you’re prone to reflux, is also a great idea. If you’re on medication, remember to take it on time. Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

To evaluate distributed efficiency under realistic network bottlenecks, we benchmarked Qwen3-32B (64 decoder layers, partitioned into P = 65 fragments with H = 65) across 2× TPU v5p-32 slices (dcn_diloco_parallelism=2) using built-in Linux traffic-control (tc) bandwidth shaping to limit inter-slice bandwidth across three network regimes: Unthrottled (Line Rate), 30 Gbit/s aggregate (Moderate Congestion), and 10 Gbit/s aggregate (Severe Congestion).

A common question with low-communication training is whether allowing learners to train independently between synchronizations degrades model quality—especially on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where independent updates could cause expert routers to drift apart.

MaxText provides a native JAX and Flax NNX implementation of both Vanilla and Streaming DiLoCo—requiring no external central parameter servers.

You can enable Streaming DiLoCo in your MaxText YAML configuration or CLI overrides by setting enable_diloco: true and enable_streaming_diloco: true. As a best practice, set both num_diloco_fragments (P) and diloco_sync_period (H) to N_layers + 1 so that exactly one fragment is synchronized per training step (Δh = 1):

In MaxText, diloco.py splits each global batch across the M learners so each learner processes GBS / M tokens per step. To overlap cross-slice communication with TPU computation in Streaming DiLoCo, weight deltas for the scheduled fragment are offloaded to M-way sharded syncer workers running on the learner host CPUs. Rather than routing through a single central coordinator, the host-CPU syncer shards execute a distributed all-reduce across the network in the background to average the fragment deltas while the TPUs continue running inner-loop training steps. Each syncer shard then applies the outer Nesterov momentum update locally and writes the updated fragment weights back to its colocated TPU learner. To dive deeper or launch your first run:

Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText decouples large-scale LLM pre-training from the requirement of a single, monolithic, high-bandwidth fabric. By combining use of an inner and outer optimizer with fine-grained parameter streaming, engineering teams can pool TPU slices across congested datacenter networks or geographically distributed metros—sustaining high hardware utilization without compromising model convergence. Streaming DiLoCo is available today in MaxText v0.2.5 on PyPI, the corresponding Docker images and on GitHub main. Try it today for experimenting with distributed training workloads.

Multi-Turn Agentic Reinforcement Learning with Tunix and Gemma 4 on Google TPUs

In our previous posts - Easy FunctionGemma finetuning with Tunix on Google TPUs and Autonomous LLM post-training with Tunix on TPUs - we showed how Google Tunix streamlines Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and single-turn Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on Cloud TPUs. Real-world AI agents, however, rarely finish their job in a single turn. They have to reason over partial observations, invoke tools, observe environment feedback, and adapt their strategy across multiple steps. So in this post, we will walk through how to train a multi-turn, tool-using LLM agent using Tunix's agentic reinforcement learning pipeline on Cloud TPU v6e-8. You can find the complete code in this GitHub repository.

We will revisit the Plane Strike board game from an earlier TensorFlow/JAX reinforcement learning blog, replacing the custom MLP policy with Gemma 4 E2B in thinking mode on a single Cloud TPU v6e-8 host. Plane Strike is played on a 6x6 grid (A1 to F6) where one 8-cell plane is hidden in one of 4 orientations. Each turn, the agent sees the result of its previous strike and the raw visible board (. untried, X hit, O miss), thinks through candidate placements, and calls the strike(cell="...") tool to strike one cell. The game ends when all 8 plane cells are hit, or after 23 strikes.
