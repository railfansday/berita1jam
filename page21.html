With just a few weeks remaining, lawmakers are trying to pass new protections for victims of online harm before the end of 2026, targeting deepfake creators and online platforms that block users from filing lawsuits.

On Tuesday, members of a Senate Judiciary subcommittee hosted a hearing calling for the end of forced arbitration for online harms. Their immediate target was Roblox, which they accused of evading legal complaints about children allegedly groomed on the platform by enforcing its own forced arbitration clause against users who agreed to the terms as minors, in some cases even under the age of 10. Separately, a few blocks away, Reps. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) and Laurel Lee (R-FL) brought hotel heiress Paris Hilton to Capitol Hill to advocate for the Defiance Act, which would let victims of nonconsensual intimate deepfakes sue their creators.

“The best thing we can do in terms of enforcement is we can open up the courthouse doors,” Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO), who chairs the Judiciary subcommittee, told reporters after the Roblox hearing. “The more I see these companies evade regulations, the more I think there is nothing as powerful as a jury.” Hawley introduced a new bill with ranking member Dick Durbin (D-IL) to render arbitration agreements unenforceable for disputes over alleged child sexual exploitation or “child cyber injury,” which could include “reputational harm, mental suffering,” or death.

“The best thing we can do in terms of enforcement is we can open up the courthouse doors”

Roblox has sought to enforce its arbitration clause in lawsuits it’s facing over allegations that users were groomed on the gaming platform as minors, despite the company’s claims of protections. Roblox is facing about 180 lawsuits in a consolidated case over its alleged role in failing to protect children. Under the arbitration process, the public would not get to see legal discovery like the kind that’s come to light under recent cases against Meta. Hawley called the jury system “corruption-proof” and “an incredibly big hammer.”

Roblox has denied that it’s trying to dodge accountability. “Safety has been central to Roblox since we launched. We require age checks for chat, place users under 16 in age-based accounts with protective defaults, and block images and video in chat,” Roblox’s global head of public policy Nicky Jackson Colaço said in a statement. “A legal position about arbitration is not a measure of a company’s safety record, and a hearing built around it fails to advance safety.”

Congress has only a little more than three more weeks left in session before the end of the year, and a new Democratic majority will be sworn in come January. Republicans are expected to do their best to push through President Donald Trump’s nominees for judgeships and executive branch posts like FCC commissioner in the lame-duck session, as well as try to pass year-end bills like the National Defense Authorization Act, which often includes a range of other policy items. But some lawmakers are holding out hope that they can open up courthouses to new litigation over deepfake abuse and child predation.

Litigation has become one of the more successful ways for survivors of online harm to seek change. Congress has failed to pass many prominent child safety, privacy, and consumer protection bills, though it has begun to tackle the deepfake issue through the Take It Down Act. At the same time, attorneys general and individual plaintiffs have collected significant settlements and legal wins despite the broad scope of tech’s legal shield, Section 230. If bills like the Defiance Act or those seeking to end forced arbitration were to pass, tech companies and perpetrators of online harms could become vulnerable to a new wave of legal challenges.

“You will be amazed by how quickly the company will change its behavior once it is sued in public court”

In addition to Hawley and Durbin’s bill about forced arbitration, Sens. Richard Blumenthal (D-CT), Katie Britt (R-AL), and Cory Booker (D-NJ) introduced a similar bill with a slightly different approach, including explicitly covering data privacy violations. At the Roblox hearing, Northwestern University law professor Myriam Gilles, an expert on forced arbitration, said she’s testified before Congress many times on this topic, but “never expected to come before this body to talk about its use against 9 year olds.” Passing either proposal, she said, would have a profound impact. “You will be amazed by how quickly the company will change its behavior once it is sued in public court.”

The Defiance Act would let victims of nonconsensual intimate deepfakes sue their creators for damages, in an attempt to raise the cost of creating these kinds of images. Hilton visited Washington earlier this year to share how an intimate non-deepfake video of her at age 19 shared without her consent impacted her life, and she used her story to advocate for the Defiance Act. At Tuesday’s press conference, she said she would come back to Congress as many times as necessary for the bill to become law. “Some people might have thought that was the end of my fight,” Hilton said. “But when I make a promise to survivors, I keep it.”

Hilton said over 50 million people have been victimized by sexually explicit deepfakes. “For me personally, strangers have created over 100,000 AI-generated explicit images and videos portraying me in the most graphic and disturbing ways imaginable,” Hilton said. “If this can happen to someone with my resources and my platform, imagine how powerless a young girl might feel facing this completely alone.”

Hilton’s comments were followed by Elliston Berry, an advocate who said a classmate made an explicit deepfake image of her friend group “from an innocent social media post” when she was 14, and distributed it throughout her school. “In a matter of seconds, my sense of security disappeared,” she said. “For me, the Defiance Act means recognition that victims deserve to be heard, that their experience matters, and that their right to seek justice should not depend on how quickly technology advances.”

Hims & Hers is launching five compounded peptide treatments not approved by the FDA

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Hims & Hers customers may be prescribed Sermorelin, NAD+, Glutathione, Vitamin B12 and L-Carnitine after a provider visit

Hims & Hers on Wednesday launched Peptides+, a specialty offering five compounded treatments that the Food and Drug Administration has not approved, according to CNBC.

The company said customers who see a licensed provider for a clinical appointment may receive a personalized plan that includes sermorelin, NAD+, glutathione, vitamin B12 and L-carnitine. The treatments are prepared domestically, and licensed U.S. pharmacies that answer to state pharmacy regulators dispense them, the company said. Hims & Hers said its Hims brand is offering the treatments to men and its Hers brand to women.

Hims & Hers plans to add BPC-157 and TB-500 "consistent with regulatory standards," the company said. Neither peptide is approved, and the bulk of the studies so far involved animal subjects instead of people, according to CNBC.

Peptides+ is available with the company's Care+ program. Care+ covers biannual lab work, unlimited help and regular check-ins with a team of dedicated clinicians, plus around-the-clock messaging with providers who have training in peptide treatment, the company said.

The launch follows an October policy from the Department of Health and Human Services. Under that policy, the FDA has said it will hold off on enforcement against licensed pharmacies, federal facilities and physicians who compound and dispense six peptides that lack approval, for as long as formal rulemaking is being reviewed, according to CNBC. The policy does not signal that regulators have cleared those individual peptides.

"Anyone investing in their health deserves to know exactly what they're putting in their body, and to have dedicated support along the way," Anant Vinjamoori, chief medical officer of Hims, said in a statement.

Vinjamoori also acknowledged gaps in the evidence. "We don't have all of this data that we're used to having with compounds like this," he told CNBC. He said patients with serious conditions such as cardiovascular, liver or kidney disease won't qualify for the compounds at this stage.

"There is a difference between this and FDA-approved therapies," Vinjamoori told Fierce Healthcare.

Outside experts have questioned the approach. "Without actual testing, all you have is anecdotal information, and that's just not enough information to put a substance in your body," Joshua Kritzer, a chemistry professor at Tufts University, told CNBC.

Office light may throw off the body’s daily rhythm

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

In a study of day-active rats, an hour of simulated daylight at noon helped restore activity rhythms weakened by fluorescent office lighting.

People spend about 90% of their time indoors, under ceiling lights that are a small fraction as bright as the sky outside. The body reads that light as a biological signal.

In a rodent that keeps human hours, living under standard fluorescent office light weakened the animal’s daily rhythm of activity.

An hour of simulated daylight at noon each day, roughly analogous to a lunchtime walk outdoors, partly restored the rats’ daily activity rhythm.

In a second experiment, more of the male rats that spent six weeks under fluorescent light showed diabetic-range blood sugar than rats under daylight-strength light.

A smaller share of sperm were moving in the fluorescent-light group, about 19% compared with 45% under simulated daylight.

The study is small, with eight animals in the first experiment and 20 in the second, and all of them were male.

Lily Yan, a professor in the Department of Psychology at Michigan State University, led the work. The lead author is Antony Bum Kim of the Department of Architecture at the University of California, Berkeley.

“It is a biological signal that influences circadian organization and, potentially, a range of downstream health outcomes,” Yan said of light.

The body’s internal clock helps regulate daily patterns, known as circadian rhythms, in sleep, appetite, and much else.

Nearly every lab rodent is awake at night, which makes it a poor stand-in for a person whose body clock runs on daylight.

The Nile grass rat, a small African rodent, is active in the daytime, and Yan’s lab has used it for years to study how light affects the brain.

Over each cage, the team installed a programmable light that can imitate the color mix of many sources. Its daylight setting ran at about 5,600 lux, roughly the level that building designers treat as true daylight.

The office setting imitated a standard fluorescent tube at about 150 lux, the brightness of a typical indoor workspace.

Eight male grass rats lived under four lighting schedules in turn, two weeks each. Sensors logged their movement day and night.

The first schedule was steady daylight from dawn to dusk. The second was daylight that brightened to the same midday peak and faded again, which delivered about half the total light.

The third was fluorescent office light all day. The fourth was office light with one hour of full daylight at noon.

The rats were most active, and most sharply divided between a busy day and a quiet night, under steady daylight.

Under fluorescent light their activity was lower and their rhythm flatter, with its peak shifted later in the day.

The ramped daylight did not match the steady version even though it reached the same peak.

The authors wrote that the total amount of light over the day, more than its brightest moment, seems to set the strength of the rhythm.

Adding the noon hour of daylight to the office schedule raised the strength of the rhythm again, though only partly.

All eight animals showed a burst of activity during that hour. The authors called it a partial rescue.

A separate group of 20 male grass rats lived for six weeks under either steady daylight or fluorescent light, 10 in each group.

The rats’ body weight, belly fat, and average blood sugar did not differ between the groups.

But five of the 20 animals had blood sugar in a range the lab treats as diabetic, and four of the five had lived under fluorescent light.

In a follow-up with a different way of drawing blood, the pattern held. Four of eight rats under fluorescent light crossed that line, compared with one of seven under daylight.

The two groups produced the same number of sperm. Under daylight, about 45% of those sperm were moving. Under fluorescent light, about 19% were.

“Factors affecting health are not limited to drugs, diet and exercise,” Kim said. “The light environments we create in our homes, schools and workplaces are also biological exposures.”

Ten animals per group is a small sample. More rats under fluorescent light crossed the study’s threshold for diabetic-range blood sugar, while average blood sugar was similar in the two groups.

The authors also wrote that blood drawn after euthanasia can read differently, which is why they ran the follow-up.

Every animal was male, so the study says nothing about females. And a grass rat under a programmable lamp is not an office worker with windows, a commute, and a phone.

The authors wrote that the findings support daylight deficiency as a risk factor for diabetes in this animal.

The sperm result is new, they wrote, since earlier work on light and sperm looked at day length rather than light quality.

What remains untested is whether the same shifts happen in people. The authors wrote that the next step is for other groups to repeat the work in grass rats or other day-active animals.

The next question is whether an hour of brighter light at midday could help people who work far from a window maintain a stronger daily rhythm.

The full study was published in the journal Journal of Biological Rhythms.

American to Renovate, Expand Phoenix Lounges

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

American Airlines plans to refresh and expand its three Admirals Club lounges at Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport.

The carrier said Tuesday that it will add seating, upgrade amenities, and improve food and beverage service across all three spaces in Terminal 4.

In the near term, the Admirals Club lounge in Concourse A between gates A19 and A21 will get a new servery area, infrastructure enhancements to support expanded food and beverage offerings, and facility updates to improve the overall customer experience.

Longer-term plans call for nearly tripling the combined footprint of the Concourse A lounges, the other being above gates A7-A9. New furniture and finishings will be brought in, and seating capacity will expand by 150%.

The lounge in Concourse B, between gates B5 and B7, will get a new self-service food and beverage area and have its seating capacity increased by 20%.

“Phoenix continues to be an important hub in our network, and these investments reflect our commitment to giving customers more space, comfort, and convenience when they travel through PHX,” American Chief Customer Officer Heather Garboden said in a news release. “While we look forward to the development of the new West Terminal, these enhancements will help create an even more seamless travel experience for the millions of customers who pass through Phoenix each year.”

The planned West Terminal will be built on the west side of the airport between Terminal 3 and Taxiway U. American is expected to serve as the terminal’s primary carrier.

According to the airport’s timeline, the project should be finished by 2033.

From acrylics and tips to builder gel and dip powder, nothing has ever worked for me. But one simple option has never let me down: press-ons. They provide a natural-looking manicure without damaging your nails, take just minutes to apply, and can be done from the comfort of your own home. There’s no drying, intense filing, or pricey appointments required. What’s not to love?

Recently, I fell down a glue-filled rabbit hole in search of the best press-on nails out there. Every week, I’d apply a new set in the name of research, meticulously assessing each for quality, durability, and longevity. I have very small nail beds, so finding a size, shape, and length that fit my fingertips was essential. After about a year of testing (and an absurd amount of nail glue), there was a clear winner: Glamnetic.

We praised the product last year, and that praise still holds true. The application is not only simple, but I actually find it quite therapeutic. I start by pushing back my cuticles with the included wooden stick and filing the entire surface of the nails, not just the edges. Doing this helps the press-ons adhere better and last longer. Next, I match each of my fingers to the best-fitting press-on. Finally, I remove any oils from my nails using the alcohol pad, and glue on the set. I start with my pinkies and move toward my thumbs, placing a dot of glue at the base of each press-on and holding it down for about 30 to 45 seconds. Before I know it, I’m left with a perfect manicure that’s affordable, natural-looking, and long-lasting.

While it’s always fun to experiment with different shapes and lengths, my personal favorite is the brand’s “Short Oval” collection. For under $20, each set comes with 30 nails in varying sizes, and all of the above materials. They are incredibly durable without looking or feeling thick, and each set lasts about two weeks.

Whether you prefer a milky pink shade like Strawberry Icing or a more festive set like Snowfall, Glamnetic is known for its impressive designs. From magnetic cat-eye polish to 3-D chrome appliqués, there’s a set for every style, season, and mood. For a nail biter who has destroyed just about every expensive manicure imaginable, finding a budget-friendly option that can actually keep up with me feels like a small miracle.

We're very careful about line extensions because this is a creative company, and our goal is to make the best entertainment on Earth.  Entertainment we’re always proud of. 📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Fifty years after the Huerta de Otero site in Mérida, Spain, was first excavated in 1976, archaeologists are still making new discoveries. According to Hoy, a local daily newspaper, excavations have been intermittent, only picking up steam in 2019.

The latest discovery at the approx. 1.2-acre site, of which only 15 percent has been excavated so far, involves mural paintings from around the mid-1st to mid-2nd centuries CE, depicting animals like lions, deer, and various types of birds, along with mythological figures like a satyr. Archaeologists also uncovered a new, well-preserved mosaic to go along with one of Huerta de Otera’s most well-known discoveries, a large, multicolor Medusa mosaic floor uncovered in 2023. Ancient Romans established a colonia, known as Augusta Emerita, in the area around 25 BCE. The site is made up of remains of a sprawling Roman-era residence, baths, and roads. The latest discovery involved excavating more of the remains of an opulent home belonging to a wealthy family. “We’ve encountered a tremendous surprise: extraordinary mural paintings,” Pepe Vargas, who oversees archaeological work at Huerta de Otero, told Hoy. Vargas also noted a courtyard peristyle covered in mosaics.

Excavation work at the site was possible due to a vocational training program, the Barraeca Professional School, overseen by the Mérida City Council. The current cohort included 12 local students trained as archaeological laborers for three months and then worked in the field at Huerta de Otero for nine months. A new group of trainees will likely start in January, according to Hoy.
