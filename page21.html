Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: What a Missed Deadline Taught Me About Switching Off

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

I missed a deadline last week. The piece you’re reading was supposed to be filed before I headed out on a long weekend to Italy for my wife’s birthday.

The main problem was I simply ran out of time to write the piece. An urgent stakeholder ask out of the blue, a couple of unanticipated work fire drills, and a long-standing evening social commitment earlier in the week made for a perfect storm, and something had to give.

The other problem and consequence was that I carried a chunk of guilt into the break.

It caught up with me at 6 am on the first morning, when I found myself drafting this column in my head. I should have been dozing peacefully. I continued to ruminate on my lengthy to-do list at work deep into day one as we headed from Venice into the Dolomites. My body was in Italy, but my brain was clearly still at my desk.

Switching off, particularly from work, is hard for most of us, and especially in an age of ubiquitous screens and wall-to-wall information competing for our attention. For me, that always-on reality plays a major role in the difficulties I have disconnecting from the daily grind.

I’m fortunate to love my job, and if I’m candid, I probably take a little too much of my identity from it. I hold on tight, and the phone in my pocket is an ever-present enabler.

A meme that has stuck with me over the years goes something along the lines of being an adult is staring at medium screens all day only to come home and stare at a little screen while a big screen is on in the background. The irony is not lost on me that I came across this rather withering take on modern life while scrolling on said little screen.

Regardless of the role the screen plays, psychologists who study employee well-being describe what I was missing for the early part of the weekend as “psychological detachment.” A state where you are genuinely able to not think about work while away from it. The science suggests it’s an important thing to work on.

A 2025 meta-analysis of 32 studies found that vacationing has a strong effect on well-being, and that the effect lasts longer than earlier research suggested. A second, smaller analysis of eight studies points to detaching from work, along with physical activity, as among the most helpful things to do while you’re off. In another study that tracked teachers during a two-week break, exhaustion, anxiety, and low mood all declined, while those who kept ruminating about work recovered less.

Whatever the science says, I know that for me the act of stepping away from work and being disciplined about not checking back in while I’m away is critical for my mental health and for staving off burnout. Getting out in nature and hiking or cycling or swimming keeps me present. It resets and recharges my batteries, and it tops up my creativity in a way that I find benefits what I am then able to bring back to the office.

Sure enough, as we drove deeper into the mountains on a crisp, blue-sky day, I could feel the golden autumnal hues and majestic rock formations drawing me in, closer to an elemental feeling of calm and happiness. That sense of equilibrium stayed with me all weekend, and while I’d be lying if I said my mind didn’t occasionally drift to my desk, it was fleeting, with the knowledge that all the things would be waiting for me when I got back. Just like this piece was.

With Thanksgiving on the horizon, and the holidays coming thick and fast thereafter, I’ll leave you with this bit of hard-earned advice: if your job is deeply entwined with your life and part of who you are, as mine is, time away can sometimes feel like a small act of disloyalty. It isn’t. In fact, it’s probably the most important thing you can do to remain faithful to your work ethic, and to yourself.

And if you’ll be working over the holidays, or you’re not working right now, or you don’t have the benefit of someone on hand to carry some of the load for you (shout out to all the Grannies out there), or your vacation looks very different from mine, that’s all okay. Find the pockets where and when you can: an evening, a walk, an hour in front of a screen, or better yet a book, and let work wait. Above all else, be kind to yourself. That’s how you really give yourself a break.

Robin Hough is the Editor-in-Chief of Healthline, and in addition to this regular Letter from the Editor column, he can be found every other Wednesday in our Healthline Wellness Wire newsletter.

pnpm: One Copy of Every Package, Shared Across Projects

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 13 • Originally published at practicaldevblog.blogspot.com

Picture a laptop that has hosted a dozen JavaScript projects. The same version of Express sits in a dozen node_modules folders. It works, and on a fast connection nobody notices, until the disk fills up or a CI runner installs everything from scratch on every push. The waste is not dramatic, it is steady: a few minutes here, a few gigabytes there, and a node_modules folder that becomes the biggest thing on the machine. pnpm goes after that waste. It keeps one copy of each package version in a shared store and links it into every project that asks for it. Is that saving worth changing how installs work?

My first check is how many projects share packages on the same machine. One project with a handful of dependencies gains almost nothing from it. The saving shows up with several projects, a monorepo, or a CI runner that installs fresh each build. If that is your setup, the store earns its place; if not, stay with npm.

My second check is whether the code uses packages it never declared. npm lays dependencies out flat, so a project can require a package that arrived only as a side effect of something else, and it works by accident. pnpm keeps node_modules strict: only packages listed in package.json are visible at the top level. That strictness catches hidden assumptions before they break a deploy, but the first switch can surface missing declarations to fix, so allow time for cleanup.

My third check is peer dependencies. pnpm is stricter about them too, and warns or fails where npm silently picked a version. That forces version conflicts into the open, but a tangled peer tree will need them resolved instead of ignored.

My fourth check is the team and its tooling. pnpm reads the same registry and package.json, and produces a lockfile, pnpm-lock.yaml, that plays the same role as package-lock.json. Most tools work fine with it, but anything assuming a flat node_modules layout, or a script that runs npm install directly, needs updating. Check the CI config first.

My verdict, as judgement: use pnpm when several projects share dependencies, or when you want dependency lists you can trust. Skip it for a single small project where npm works. The recommendation flips if the team leans on undeclared packages with no time to clean them up, or if a pipeline tool cannot handle pnpm's layout.

The setup is two small projects on the same machine, site-a and site-b, each a plain Express app with exactly one dependency in package.json:

Both were installed with pnpm 10.14.0 on Node v24.22.1. The first install in site-a downloaded Express and its 68 transitive dependencies, 69 packages in total, and finished in under four seconds:

The second install, in site-b with the identical dependency, also finished in 3.8 seconds, and it did not need a second download, because the packages were already in the store. The store lives at a single path on this machine, /home/hatch/.local/share/pnpm/store/v10, and it held one 4.0M copy of everything both projects needed. Inside each project, node_modules/express is not a copy at all, it is a symlink:

A plain du comparison looks confusing at first: du reported 4.5M for each pnpm node_modules folder and 3.9M for an npm install of the same Express version, with an apparent size of 2.2M in all three cases. The numbers are noisy because pnpm links files from the store with hard links, and du counts a hard-linked file once per folder it appears in. The honest measure is the store itself: one 4.0M copy served both projects, while npm copied the files into each project separately. The saving grows with every extra project that shares the same versions.

A curl request to the running server returned the JSON exactly as written:

The thing that went wrong came from that strict node_modules layout. Express itself depends on a package called debug, so debug is present on disk inside node_modules/.pnpm, but it is not listed in site-a's package.json. Requiring it directly failed:

The same require in the npm comparison project loaded debug without complaint, version 2.6.9, because npm's flat layout leaves every transitive package within reach. That is the accident pnpm refuses to repeat: code that depends on debug should say so. The fix is to declare it, then install again:

After pnpm install reported "Packages: +1", the same require worked and reported version 4.3.7, the version actually declared, rather than the older 2.6.9 that Express happens to use internally. The error felt annoying in the moment, and it was doing exactly what the tool promises: the dependency list now says what the code really needs.

First, the phantom dependency above. The symptom is Error: Cannot find module 'debug' with code MODULE_NOT_FOUND, for a package sitting under node_modules. The fix is to declare the package in package.json and run pnpm install, never reach into .pnpm by hand.

Second, a lockfile mismatch in CI. The symptom is a failed install saying "specifiers in the lockfile don't match specifiers in package.json" and a line like "express (lockfile: 4.21.2, manifest: 4.21.1)". The fix is to run pnpm install locally so the lockfile updates, then commit it; never hand-edit a version without reinstalling.

Third, mixing npm and pnpm in one project. The symptom is two lockfiles, package-lock.json beside pnpm-lock.yaml, and installs that behave differently on different machines. The fix is to delete package-lock.json and node_modules, run pnpm install, and keep only one lockfile in the repo.

Fourth, judging the store by du on node_modules. The symptom is a node_modules folder that still reports several megabytes, as if the saving never happened. The fix is to measure the store path with du instead, since hard links make per-project folders look bigger than the disk they use.

pnpm earns its place on a machine with several JavaScript projects, or a team that wants dependency lists it can trust. The shared store is a real saving, and the strict layout turns silent accidents into loud, fixable errors at install time instead of on a deploy. For one small project with npm already working, switching buys little and adds a new tool to learn. If your setup looks like the two projects above, try it on one of them first: install, run the server, and see whether anything in the code was quietly depending on a package it never declared. If the list comes back clean, keep going.

Why my downloader has two PHP endpoints instead of one

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When I first built the downloader on ramrom.io, I had one PHP file doing everything: take the pasted link, call the provider API, fetch the video file, and hand it to the visitor. It worked. It was also a mess. Every bug lived in the same place, every fix touched the same file, and when something broke I had no idea which half had failed.

So I split it in two. Now resolve.php does the talking and the proxy does the fetching, and they never swap jobs. Here is why that split has held up.

resolve.php takes one thing from the browser: the link the visitor pasted. It checks that the link is a real URL on a platform I support — TikTok, Instagram, Facebook or YouTube — and nothing else. If the link fails that check, no API call is spent. Then it calls the provider's API, gets back a list of formats, and returns that list as JSON.

That is all it does. It never touches a video file. It never streams a single byte of media.

Because it is a small, boring endpoint, it is easy to protect. The per-IP rate limit lives here, not in the proxy: 30 requests a minute per visitor, counted in a flat file, because shared hosting has no Redis. The retry loop lives here too — if the provider answers "Service is busy", it tries again up to three times before telling the visitor honestly that the provider is down. And because the response is small JSON, I can log failures without logging anything about the visitor.

Once the visitor picks a quality, the browser calls the second endpoint, the download proxy, with the file URL that resolve.php returned. The proxy does not know or care which platform the link came from. Its job is to fetch bytes and pass them through.

Streaming matters here. A video can be hundreds of megabytes, and PHP on shared hosting will die if you try to load that into memory. So the proxy reads the remote file in chunks and echoes each chunk straight to the visitor, keeping memory use flat. It also sets the headers that make the browser download instead of playing: Content-Disposition: attachment with a cleaned-up filename, and the right Content-Type for MP4 or M4A.

The proxy also has its own guard rails, different from the resolver's. It checks the file really is a video before sending it — an HTML error page wearing a .mp4 name gets rejected. It follows at most five redirects, and it refuses private IP ranges, so nobody can point it at something on my own server.

The main win is that each endpoint fails in its own way, and I can tell which one it was. If resolve.php returns an error, the provider or the pasted link is the problem. If the proxy fails, the file itself is the problem — deleted, private, or the provider's link expired. The error message the visitor sees can say exactly that, instead of a generic "something went wrong".

Testing is simpler too. I can curl resolve.php with a link and check the JSON without downloading anything. I can curl the proxy with a known file URL and check the headers and the first bytes without involving the provider API at all. Two small checks, each testing one thing.

There is one honest exception. YouTube's provider tunnels sometimes return empty responses to datacenter IPs, which includes my server. For those formats the proxy cannot help, so resolve.php flags them as "direct" and the visitor's browser fetches the file straight from the tunnel instead. Same two endpoints, same split — just a flag that says "this one, skip the middleman".

If you are building a tool on plain shared hosting, resist the one-file version. Two small endpoints, each with one job and its own guard rails, are easier to debug, easier to protect, and much easier to explain to yourself six months later when something breaks at midnight.

Flutter + Node.js: how I plan a mobile MVP

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 13 • Originally published at ahmer-najam.net

A mobile MVP has two jobs: reach real users fast, and stay cheap to change. Flutter helps with the first, because one Dart codebase gives you both an iOS and an Android app. A small Node.js API helps with the second, because your business rules live in one place that a web dashboard or a second app can reuse later.

I am a freelance full stack developer with more than 20 years in corporate IT. This is the order I work in when a founder asks me to turn an idea into a working app.

Who is the user, and what is the one thing they must be able to do? "A customer places an order and sees its status" is enough. That sentence decides your screens and your tables. Then sketch the data: for an order flow, users, products, orders and order items. Getting this right early saves the most rework.

Keep it plain REST with JSON: POST /auth/login, GET /orders, POST /orders, PATCH /orders/:id/status. In Node.js I use Express or Fastify with PostgreSQL or MongoDB, validation on every route and one consistent error format. Document the endpoints from day one so the app, the dashboard and later integrations share one contract.

Three layers are enough: a data layer that calls the API, a layer that holds state, and the widgets. Riverpod is lighter to start with, Bloc is stricter for larger teams; either works if you use it consistently. Generate Dart models from your API shapes so a backend change shows up as a compile error instead of a crash on a user's phone.

A button, an input, a list tile and a loading state, used everywhere, make the app look consistent without a long design phase. Handle empty lists, slow networks and expired sessions early. Users meet those on day one.

A few API tests for login, the core workflow and permissions, unit tests for the logic layer and widget tests for the main screens are enough for an MVP. Install weekly builds on real devices; simulators hide keyboard, notch and slow-network problems.

GitHub Actions can run tests and build the app on every push. The API runs in a Docker container behind HTTPS with secrets in environment variables and a daily database backup. Create the store accounts in your own name (Apple Developer $99 a year, Google Play a one-time $25) and leave time for review.

For a focused app with a small admin view: week one for scope, data model and API design; weeks two to four for core screens and endpoints, with an installable build each week; week five for payments, notifications and polish; week six for testing and store release. More user types or integrations add weeks.

Both can ship a good MVP. I lean to Flutter when you want a very consistent interface on both platforms and have no React mobile team. React Native makes sense if you already have React web developers to share.

Better U.S. Bank ETF: State Street's KBE vs. First Trust's FTXO

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

KBE carries a lower fee and higher yield, yet FTXO's concentrated 50-stock portfolio tells a different story about risk and reward.

The State Street SPDR S&P Bank ETF (KBE -0.68%) offers a more diversified, lower-cost entry into the banking sector compared to the concentrated and higher-fee First Trust Nasdaq Bank ETF (FTXO -0.43%).

Both ETFs target the U.S. financial landscape, yet they take different structural paths for investors seeking exposure to domestic banks. While FTXO focuses on "smart" indexing with fewer positions selected by factor criteria, KBE provides broader exposure across the industry, including regional players and diversified financial services.

Beta measures price volatility relative to the S&P 500; beta is calculated from monthly returns over the available fund history (up to five years). The 1-year return represents total return over the trailing 12 months. Dividend yield is the trailing-12-month distribution yield.

Investors may find the State Street fund more affordable, as its 0.35% expense ratio is lower than the 0.6% charged by the First Trust fund. Additionally, State Street SPDR S&P Bank ETF offers a higher payout for income-seeking portfolios.

The State Street SPDR S&P Bank ETF focuses 100% on financial services, providing exposure to large, mid, and small-cap companies across the banking landscape. Its largest positions include Central Bancompany (CBC -0.73%) at 1.14%, Customers Bancorp (CUBI -0.06%) at 1.03%, and First Citizens BancShares (FCNCA +0.10%) at 1.03%. The fund uses a modified equal-weighted approach to ensure the portfolio is not overly concentrated in a few giants. It launched in 2005, and has paid $1.47 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$66.8 share price works out to a 2.2% yield.

First Trust Nasdaq Bank ETF is also 100% concentrated in financial services but maintains a tighter portfolio of 50 positions based on liquidity and fundamental factors. Its largest positions include Wells Fargo (WFC -0.05%) at 8.07%, U.S. Bancorp (USB -0.38%) at 8.02%, and JPMorgan Chase (JPM -0.17%) at 7.81%. This structure creates a more top-heavy profile compared to the State Street fund. It launched in 2016, and has paid $0.77 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$40.2 share price works out to a 1.9% yield.

For more guidance on ETF investing, check out the full guide at this link.

Investors looking for exposure to the U.S. banking sector have two distinct choices in the State Street SPDR S&P Bank ETF (KBE) and the First Trust Nasdaq Bank ETF (FTXO). Choosing between them depends on whether you want to target a handful of the largest U.S. banks through FTXO or prefer KBE's broader, more regional bank-focused holdings.

Over a five-year time horizon, both funds deliver about the same return. For cost-conscious investors, however, KBE's lower expense ratio and higher dividend yield can be compelling factors.

That said, a key consideration is the current interest rate environment. Major Wall Street banks and economists increasingly expect the Federal Reserve to continue raising interest rates into early 2027.

If that happens, KBE is more likely to outperform in a controlled, moderate rate-hike cycle where overall U.S. economic growth stays steady. That's because of the fund's focus on regional banks, which rely heavily on traditional lending and deposit-taking versus the big banks' greater fee-based businesses. When rates rise, regional banks can reprice loans quickly, and expand their net interest margin.

However, if the Fed is more aggressive with rate hikes, regional banks could suffer as loan volume declines, making FXTO the fund more likely to outperform in this scenario.

Reeling Oregon Ducks remain positive, committed to the grind

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

EUGENE — The 2026 season is on the verge of completely unraveling for the Oregon Ducks. One more loss, and any shred of postseason hopes would be extinguished.

Yet even after two upset losses and falling out of the rankings — despite starting the season at No. 2 — the Ducks (3-2, 1-1 Big Ten) remain focused, together and committed to salvaging the season.

"One thing I really love is the way the people in the locker room have been responding to this adversity," sophomore cornerback Brandon Finney Jr. said.

After his team's 17-15 loss to No. 12 UCLA on Saturday, Oregon coach Dan Lanning said the team would be measured by how it responds moving forward.

On Wednesday, he said the team has elevated its work ethic and embraced accountability.

"I've been proud of the way that they've worked," Lanning said. "I don't see a lot of hanging heads, right? I think that's challenging, too, especially when you're young, to not lose a little bit of gusto and not lose a little bit of belief."

Oregon linebacker Devon Jackson (#9) drops UCLA running back Wayne Knight (#3) for no gain as the No. 13 Ducks face the No. 21 Bruins in a Big Ten college football game at Autzen Stadium in Eugene on Saturday, Oct. 10, 2026. Sean Meagher/The Oregonian

The Ducks, who host Nebraska (5-1, 2-1) at 8 p.m. Saturday, responded well to adversity following a 39-31 loss at Oklahoma State on Sept. 12. Oregon won its next two games, including a 41-27 win at USC on Sept. 26.

That victory seemed to give the Ducks a fresh start. But two weeks later, they lost to the Bruins and dropped out of the rankings.

Now, Oregon must run the table to have a legitimate shot at the College Football Playoff, which seemed like a given for a team that returned most of its starters from last year's semifinal squad.

RELATED: Can the Ducks bounce back from UCLA loss? Experts release new Oregon vs. Nebraska predictions

It's not uncommon for teams with grand expectations to unravel in tough times. So far, that hasn't happened.

"The team is very connected and maintains its composure whether we're winning or losing," punter Bailey Ettridge said. "We haven't let the losses last week and against Oklahoma State bring us down. I think we've handled them well."

Wide receiver Dakorien Moore said the team is filled with players who came to Oregon to win and be great. He added that the team's goals remain attainable, and team leaders have worked to encourage togetherness and mutual trust.

"If you can't trust your teammate, then everything ain't going to form in the way that it's supposed to," he said.

This also means trusting the process, the plan and the team's goal to get better instead of blaming one another.

Oregon head coach Dan Lanning talks to the defense during a timeout as the No. 13 Oregon Ducks face the No. 21 UCLA Bruins in a Big Ten college football game at Autzen Stadium in Eugene on Saturday, Oct. 10, 2026. Sean Meagher/The Oregonian

"Everybody knows what we want to attack this next week, being that Nebraska is a good team," he said.

The outside noise has been deafening. To say that many Oregon fans have melted down on social media would be an understatement.

Virtually all of the players have social media accounts. Moore said he is active on social media but ignores negativity.

Finney said he isn't very active on social media and doesn't care what people say outside the program.

"Why would I let your opinion inflict me or my thoughts on my performance or my thoughts on our performance," he said. "Because people are going to be there when you're doing good, when you're bad; when you're doing bad, they're going to leave you."

For that reason, the team lives by the motto F.E.B.U., which stands for "Forget Everyone But Us".

"F.E.B.U. is a true statement that we all follow," Finney said. "… Just back to work. We got to figure out what we did wrong, go to the doctor, and just get back to work."

Fifth-year linebacker Devon Jackson said before the season that his main goal was team-driven — winning the national title.

"At the end of the year, what happens, happens, but I promise if we focus on the day and focus on one game at a time, we will be okay," Jackson said.

On Saturday, the Ducks' defense played one of its best games of the season, while the offense played its worst without starting quarterback Dante Moore, who remains in concussion protocol.

On teams that lack closeness, such a contrast can cause issues. But that is not the case with Oregon.

"I mean, we're all in this together," Jackson said. "It's not like, the defense won the game and offense lost, or special teams won or lost. We all lost together and we all win together. So, we got each other's back no matter what."

Oregon offensive players celebrate a touchdown run by running back Jordon Davison (#0) as the No. 13 Ducks face the No. 21 UCLA Bruins in a Big Ten college football game at Autzen Stadium in Eugene on Saturday, Oct. 10, 2026. Sean Meagher/The OregonianSean Meagher/The Oregonian

Jackson said that after Saturday's loss, he and senior center Iapani Laloulu talked for 20 minutes about the team. He told Laloulu that he didn't care what was going on with the offense; the defense had its back.

"I wouldn't trade that group with anybody else in the country despite what other people may think," Jackson said. "And we're going to continue to lean on each other because it's we all we got and we all we need."

Realistically, Oregon can't expect much sympathy from outsiders. The Ducks have top-notch facilities, infinite uniform options, a roster with an estimated payroll of $50 million, and a coach who isn't afraid to speak his mind, as Lanning did after beating the Trojans.

Many in the football world certainly wouldn't mind seeing Oregon fall.

"Ain't nobody going to feel sorry for us," linebacker Matayo Uiagalelei said. "So just came back in the next day with the mindset ready to work."
