Russell Brand Documentary From ‘Jimmy Savile: A British Horror Story’ Producers Set at Netflix Days After Rape Sentencing

Netflix has unveiled a documentary on Russell Brand, days after the comedian and former actor received a prison term of 10 years and six months for rape and sexual assault.

“Russell Brand: Open Secret” is directed by Olivia Isaacs and produced by Fremantle-owned 72 Films, the company behind “Jimmy Savile: A British Horror Story.” The film follows Brand from his rapid ascent in comedy to his sharp fall after allegations of sexual offenses, through to his criminal trial. No premiere date was announced. It draws on insiders and archive material to examine Brand’s life. It also tracks the investigation carried out by The Times and The Sunday Times, led by the papers’ chief news correspondent Rosamund Urwin and investigations correspondent Charlotte Wace.

A London jury found Brand guilty of one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault. He was acquitted of two other rape charges and one charge of attempted indecent assault. Judge Bennathan handed down the sentence on Monday and ruled that Brand will not be eligible for release until he has completed two thirds of the term.

“About 20 years ago, Russell Brand, you were becoming famous,” the judge said in his sentencing remarks. “You were clever and, in many people’s eyes, funny and charismatic. You had also become a sexual predator.”

After the trial ended, the Metropolitan Police confirmed that Brand is under investigation over further rape allegations from two other women.

The allegations against Brand first came to light in 2023 in a docuseries made by The Sunday Times with Channel 4’s “Dispatches.” Until then, Brand was known chiefly for his comedy and for film roles in “Forgetting Sarah Marshall” and “Get Him to the Greek.”

Clare Cameron, David Glover and Mark Raphael are executive producers on “Open Secret.” Kate Yandle, Laura Spence and Felicity Cross produce.

Glover and Raphael founded 72 Films as an independent outfit focused on high-end documentaries, and the company is part of the Fremantle group. Its credits include “9/11: One Day in America,” which won an Emmy, and “John Lennon: Murder Without a Trial,” a Critics Choice Award winner. Tesla’s Cheapest Cybertruck Ever Is An RC Racer That Goes 37 MPH

The Tesla Cybertruck was never as affordable as Elon Musk envisioned back in 2019, when the angular electric pickup truck was unveiled as a concept. Now, though, the American EV maker is selling a sub-$1,000 miniature version of the controversial model, right on the verge of its discontinuation.

The Tesla Cybertruck RC Racer was added to the company’s web shop today, as spotted by Tesla enthusiast Sawyer Merritt on X. The 1:10-scale remote-controlled EV features all-wheel drive, a rechargeable lithium-polymer battery, and independent suspension with plenty of travel.

The company claims its RC Racer’s 2.4-gigaherz radio system can maintain high-speed stability at distances of up to 326 feet (100 meters), while an optional Gyro feature adds counter-steering to help prevent spinouts.

A single brushless motor sends power to both axles through a set of drive shafts, enabling a top speed of 37 miles per hour (60 kilometers per hour). That’s very impressive, and it makes the small-scale Cybertruck faster than all low-speed vehicles (LSVs) in the United States, which are capped at just 25 mph.

Tesla bundles a USB-C charger in the box, which is a welcome addition in a world where smartphone makers are no longer including charging cables, let alone charging bricks. The Cybertruck RC Racer has an advertised run time of up to 20 minutes, and judging from the promotional video posted on the company’s website, it looks like a ton of fun.

To charge the battery, the polycarbonate body needs to be removed from the chassis via the quick-release clips. Then, the battery needs to be disconnected from the motor and connected to the on-board charger, and charging can begin via the USB-C port on the side of the chassis. According to Tesla, a full charge may take up to three hours, which can be a bit of a buzzkill, but the company said it will soon start selling spare parts for the RC Racer, so buyers will probably be able to get a spare battery or two to keep the fun going for longer.

The included transmitter has a side wheel for steering and a trigger for acceleration, braking, and reverse. Separate controls for the throttle trim, steering alignment trim, steering reverse, and gyro stability assist are also included. The transmitter is powered by four AA batteries, which are not included in the box.

Tesla’s Cybertryck RC Racer is available now for $350 on the company’s online shop, with the first shipments expected to arrive at customers’ doors starting next week. According to the company, the RC car is not suitable for children under 14 years old.

‘I Play Rocky’ Opens 50th Anniversary Edition of Cairo Film Festival as Chief Hussein Fahmy Looks to the Future

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As the Cairo Intl. Film Festival celebrates 50 years since its founding, president Hussein Fahmy wants younger Egyptians to rediscover their country’s rich cinematic heritage. For the veteran actor, introducing new audiences to the films that established Egypt as the Arab world’s cinematic capital is central to the festival’s future.

Speaking to Variety ahead of the 47th edition, which runs Nov. 11–20, Fahmy emphasized the importance of preserving that legacy. “This remains my dream, to have this young generation aware of our history, of how Egyptian cinema was actually dominating the whole Middle East.” He sees the festival as an important platform for bringing that history to new audiences, particularly through screenings of restored Egyptian classics. Fahmy also wants Cairo to stand alongside the world’s major film festivals, an ambition he believes depends on building audiences’ confidence in its programming. “The more credibility the Cairo Film Festival has for the spectator, the more they trust us with the movies that we bring, with the jury that we select,” he said.

Cairo’s credibility was shaken somewhat following news that one of the films in Critics’ Week competition, “Dancing Angels,” had been pulled from the lineup, before the invitation was eventually restored, although the filmmaking team then declined the opportunity to premiere the film there. (See full story here.)

But attracting audiences, Fahmy acknowledges, is an increasingly unpredictable undertaking. “The situation, whether it be the social situation, whether it be the international situation, is affecting the taste of the spectator. It’s affecting the mood of the spectator,” he said.

This year’s edition brings together 145 films from 64 countries, including 17 world premieres, five international premieres and 87 Middle East and North Africa premieres. The lineup spans acclaimed international directors, emerging Arab filmmakers and restored Egyptian classics, alongside new programs aimed at younger audiences.

The festival opens with “I Play Rocky,” directed by Peter Farrelly (“Green Book,” 2019 Academy Awards best picture winner), which chronicles Sylvester Stallone’s struggle to bring his screenplay for “Rocky” to the screen while insisting on playing the lead role himself. The selection carries particular significance for the anniversary edition as the original “Rocky” was released in 1976, the same year CIFF was founded.

The international competition includes two Egyptian productions: Ashgan El-Hamus’ “Al Baraneya,” about a family living among jasmine fields outside Cairo whose livelihood is threatened by a landowner’s persistent offer to purchase their property, and the world premiere of Ahmad Abdalla’s “BerleeN_,” which follows three Arab strangers confronting their pasts over the course of a day in the German capital. The feature is produced by Film Clinic, the Cairo-based production outfit headed by veteran producer and former CIFF director Mohamed Hefzy.

Other regional contenders include Lebanese filmmaker Nadim Tabet’s “In the Darkness I See You,” a supernatural drama set around a construction site where tensions rise between Syrian workers and Lebanese villagers, and Moroccan director Laïla Marrakchi’s “Strawberries,” which follows Moroccan seasonal workers facing exploitation on farms in southern Spain. The latter premiered in Cannes’ Un Certain Regard section and is Morocco’s submission for the international feature Oscar.

The 12-film competition also features “Heysel 85,” from Romanian-Belgian director Teodora Ana Mihai, which revisits the deadly 1985 football stadium disaster in Brussels that left 39 people dead; Austrian director Adrian Goiginger’s “Four Minus Three,” about a professional clown coping with the loss of her family; and Iranian filmmaker Morteza Alizadeh’s “Inherited,” which follows a woman on death row who learns that pregnancy is her only way to delay her execution. “Inherited” will have its world premiere in Cairo.

Moroccan filmmaker Nabil Ayouch heads the international competition jury, whose members include Chinese actor Fan Bingbing and Belgian director Laura Wandel.

The Horizons of Arab Cinema competition includes seven films, among them the world premiere of Egyptian director Sara Shazli’s documentary “Your Daughter.” Shazli explores her relationship with her mother, filmmaker and producer Marianne Khoury, niece of legendary Egyptian director Youssef Chahine. As she prepares to become a mother herself, Shazli reflects on how Khoury’s lifelong devotion to cinema shaped her childhood.

Also making its world premiere is Iraqi director Ali Toffan Al-Fatlawi’s “Al Baseer – The Blind Ferryman,” which follows a blind ferryman living in the marshes of southern Iraq who falls in love with a mysterious woman whose existence no one else believes.

Other Horizons selections include Algerian filmmaker Mia Bendrimia’s documentary “Magma,” which delves into the effects of colonialism on her family across Algeria and France, and Lebanese director Rania Rafei’s “The Day of Wrath: Tales from Tripoli,” tracing decades of political and social upheaval in the northern Lebanese city.

The out-of-competition lineup features internationally acclaimed films, including Andrey Zvyagintsev’s Cannes Grand Prix winner “Minotaur,” Emin Alper’s Berlin Silver Bear winner “Salvation” and Valeska Grisebach’s Cannes Jury Prize winner “The Dreamed Adventure.” Japanese filmmaker Hirokazu Kore-eda is represented by two films, “Look Back” and 2026 Palme d’Or nominee “Sheep in the Box,” while other selections include Radu Jude’s “The Diary of a Chambermaid,” Mahamat-Saleh Haroun’s “Soumsoum, the Night of the Stars” and Rodrigo Sorogoyen’s “The Beloved.”

The festival’s commitment to Egyptian film history is particularly visible in its Restored Egyptian Classics program, launched last year and featuring 27 titles in this edition. Among them are Salah Abu Seif’s “Cairo 30” and “The Second Wife,” Henry Barakat’s “The Sin,” Youssef Chahine’s “The People and the Nile” and Hassan el-Imam’s adaptations of Naguib Mahfouz’s “Palace Walk” and “Palace of Desire.”

“I find that the festival is a very good platform to screen all the restored movies of Egyptian cinema, of its black and white cinema,” Fahmy emphasized. “It’s a wonderful history and I would love everyone to be aware of it as they go into the future.”

The anniversary edition will also honor filmmakers Matteo Garrone and Abderrahmane Sissako with Golden Pyramid Honorary Awards for Lifetime Achievement, alongside Egyptian screenwriter Medhat El Adl and cinematographer Saeed Shimi. Egyptian actor Asser Yassin will receive the Faten Hamama Award for Excellence.

The festival is also expanding its efforts to reach younger audiences through its New Wave program, a new Cairo Kids section and an extended reality program. Its industry arm, Cairo Industry Days, running Nov. 14–19, will host masterclasses and conversations with filmmakers and industry figures, alongside financing, training and networking opportunities, particularly for filmmakers from the Arab world.

For Fahmy, the festival’s future ultimately depends on building on Egypt’s cinematic legacy while attracting a new generation of filmgoers. “As the festival gets older, I want it to become younger,” Fahmy reflected.

The cryptocurrency market is struggling to find support as Bitcoin, Ethereum and major altcoins extend their declines. Bitcoin traded near $73,400 on November 11, while Ethereum slipped toward $2,050 and Solana fell below $85. The broader market has come under pressure from institutional withdrawals, leveraged liquidations and growing macroeconomic uncertainty.

With several major cryptocurrencies approaching important support levels, investors are increasingly questioning whether the latest correction has further room to run. Is the crypto market approaching a bottom, or could another wave of selling push prices lower?

The sell-off has extended beyond Bitcoin, with major altcoins recording steeper losses as investors reduce exposure to riskier digital assets. Bitcoin has struggled to recover above $75,000 after falling to its lowest levels since August. Ethereum has faced heavier selling, dropping toward $2,050, while Solana has slipped below $85.

The pressure is also visible across the wider market. XRP and other large-cap altcoins also faced significant selling pressure, highlighting the breadth of the correction.

Leveraged trading has amplified the market decline. Crypto futures liquidations reached approximately $1.1 billion in the 24 hours reported on November 9, with long positions accounting for a substantial share.

Ethereum recorded particularly heavy liquidations, reflecting the pressure on traders who had positioned for a price recovery. Meanwhile, futures trading volume surged above $180 billion as open interest declined to approximately $127 billion.

This combination points to significant position unwinding. As leveraged bullish bets are closed automatically, additional selling can push prices lower and trigger further liquidations, creating a cycle of downward pressure.

Institutional demand has also weakened as investors withdraw capital from spot crypto investment products. US spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded two consecutive weeks of net outflows, while Ethereum and Solana investment products also experienced withdrawals. Earlier in November, combined outflows across major US crypto ETFs exceeded $1.1 billion in a single week.

These withdrawals have become an important headwind because ETF demand has been a significant source of institutional buying. Continued redemptions could make it harder for Bitcoin and other major cryptocurrencies to establish a sustained recovery.

The broader economic environment is adding another layer of pressure. Stronger-than-expected US employment data and renewed expectations of tighter Federal Reserve policy have reduced appetite for speculative assets.

Political uncertainty following the US midterm elections has also raised questions about the future direction of cryptocurrency regulation. Meanwhile, concerns surrounding the reported $6 billion Binance security breach have added to industry-wide uncertainty. Together, these developments have weakened investor confidence, leaving digital assets vulnerable to further volatility.

The immediate outlook depends on whether Bitcoin can regain support and whether ETF flows begin to stabilize. Ethereum and Solana also need to attract fresh buying interest to prevent the correction from spreading further across altcoins

Manchester City's Jeremy Doku is undoubtedly one of the most exciting wingers in the Premier League.

Yet, his performances still leave you wanting a lot more from the 24-year-old.

His ability to dribble with the ball, beat opponents and carry his team up the pitch is almost unmatched by any winger currently in the Premier League.

However, despite his impressive dribbling, which the stats truly show is impeccable, there is plenty to be frustrated with.

And Enzo Maresca will undoubtedly be feeling the same way.

For all his impressive statistics, there is one thing Doku still needs to do to be regarded as one of the very best.

Doku's dribbling for Man City will count for little if he cannot consistently turn that quality into quantifiable outcomes for his side.

According to Data.MB, Doku is leading Premier League wingers in 10 stats on a per-90 basis.

He has the most accurate crosses (2.11), the highest number of deep completions (3.79), the most dribbles attempted (14.96) and the leading number of successful dribbles (10.32).

Additionally, the Belgian international also gets involved in plenty of offensive duels (27.4) and comes out on top in duels won (16.23).

His other chart-topping stats include progressive carries (8.64), successful attacking actions (13.7), touches in the opposition box (9.7) and progressive actions (14.12).

However, despite those impressive numbers, Doku still manages to frustrate, with his end product not matching up to his offensive qualities.

This season, the Belgian has only managed to pick up two goal contributions, while his 0.45 shots on target per 90 place him in the 34th percentile.

Doku is in the 100th percentile for big chances created per 90, so perhaps it is not entirely on him.

But Man City need to start seeing him increase both his goals and assists, as that is ultimately what attacking players will be judged on.

Doku may be easy on the eye to watch, but with a lack of goal contributions, that is a frustrating watch.

Relying on Erling Haaland is not a bad strategy for goals, but as the last two games showed, when the Norwegian struggles, there is little danger elsewhere for the opposition.

City have scored just once in their last two, losing both of those fixtures.

If the likes of Doku and even Iliman Ndiaye can circumvent the need for Haaland, Maresca's squad will be in a healthier place.

Young duo at heart of Nations League's meanest defence

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Northern Ireland play Ukraine in Belfast on Saturday before travelling to Budapest to face Hungary on Tuesday

Across his two spells as Northern Ireland boss, Michael O'Neill has been blessed with no shortage of centre-back talent.

Jonny Evans, Aaron Hughes, Gareth McAuley and Craig Cathcart were mainstays of his first stint in charge - so much so that, in some of the biggest games of O'Neill's managerial career at Euro 2016, the quartet all started across a flat back-four.

Since returning to the job four years ago, O'Neill has switched to three at the back, with Sunderland duo Trai Hume and Dan Ballard providing an injection of Premier League quality in what remains a side largely drawn from the Championship and League One.

But with Hume, something of a Swiss army knife in the squad, covering the injured Conor Bradley on the right flank and even starting in centre midfield against Ukraine last month, and Ballard presently nursing an injured hamstring, change has been required through this Nations League campaign.

Despite those forced alterations in defence, the side go into their concluding Group B2 fixtures against Ukraine and Hungary top of their pool and the only side at any level of the competition yet to concede a goal.

At the heart of their defensive efforts have been Ruairi McConville and Tom Atcheson, two young centre-backs already described by their international manager as having "no ceiling".

At 21-years-old, Norwich City's McConville is the more senior of the pair.

Having been given his debut off the bench at home to Belarus two years ago this week, among the former Brighton man's early international assignments was trying to keep a rampant Alexander Isak in check during a 5-1 defeat by Sweden.

In the short time since he has looked an increasingly commanding figure, leading this Nations League group in clearances made with both head and boot.

"It would be unbelievable to keep that record for these last two games and go the whole of the campaign," he said of what is Northern Ireland's longest run without conceding a goal since 2017.

McConville notes that the streak is a team effort and product of collective work-rate, rather than just the defensive three alone.

"We're a team that works hard, a team that doesn't want to give anything up.

"We're confident in ourselves and we know that we're a good side."

It is McConville who has taken on the Ballard role in the middle of the defence for the past three games, with Atcheson on the right and Oxford United's Ciaron Brown, himself superb through the campaign so far, playing from the left.

On the developing understanding between an emerging unit, McConville added: "We're enjoying playing together.

"It's still early days together and we have to keep taking it game by game and doing what the gaffer tells us and the work on the training pitch.

"We don't have much time together when we're here [in international camp] so it's important to do all we can."

After 16 caps, McConville is a relative veteran compared to Atcheson, who made his international bow in a friendly against Wales in March at a time when he had only four senior club starts to his name at Blackburn Rovers.

Better Tech ETF for 2027: State Street's XLK vs. Fidelity's FTEC

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Both funds charge just 0.08%, but their holdings counts differ sharply, and long-term returns reveal a narrow gap.

The State Street Technology Select Sector SPDR ETF (XLK +3.23%) and Fidelity MSCI Information Technology Index ETF (FTEC +2.91%) both provide ultra-low-cost technology exposure, differing primarily in their breadth of holdings and long-term risk-adjusted returns.

Selecting a technology fund often comes down to a choice between large-cap concentration and total-market breadth. While both funds are anchored by the same massive software and hardware leaders, their portfolio construction creates different outcomes for investors looking for mid-cap participation within the tech sector.

Beta measures price volatility relative to the S&P 500; beta is calculated from monthly returns over the available fund history (up to five years). The 1-year return represents total return over the trailing 12 months. Dividend yield is the trailing-12-month distribution yield.

Both funds carry an identical expense ratio of 0.08%, making them two of the most affordable options in the category. The yield difference is minimal, with the State Street fund providing a slightly higher payout than its Fidelity peer.

The State Street Technology Select Sector SPDR ETF focuses on the technology portion of the S&P 500, offering exposure to the largest and most established companies in the industry. The portfolio is strictly 100% tech and holds 76 stocks, meaning it skips over the small-cap segment to favor stability and size. Its largest positions include Apple (AAPL +2.04%) at 16.41%, Nvidia (NVDA +2.87%) at 13.19%, and Microsoft (MSFT -2.26%) at 12.77%. The fund was launched in 1998, and has paid $0.83 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$168.9 share price works out to a 0.5% yield.

The Fidelity MSCI Information Technology Index ETF tracks a much broader index, including 294 holdings that reach further down the market-capitalization scale. Its sector makeup is 99% technology, with trace amounts in industrials and financial services. Its largest positions include Apple at 18.96%, Nvidia at 14.80%, and Microsoft at 13.21%. This fund was launched in 2013 and manages $20.8 billion in assets under management (AUM), while paying $1.11 per share over the trailing 12 months, which on its recent ~$263.8 share price works out to a 0.4% yield.

For more guidance on ETF investing, check out the full guide at this link.

Investors seeking exposure to the hot technology industry have two distinct choices in the State Street Technology Select Sector SPDR ETF (XLK) and the Fidelity MSCI Information Technology Index ETF (FTEC). Both share similarities, such as their expense ratios, betas, and dividend yields.

The key factors setting XLK and FTEC apart are their AUMs and the size of their holdings. XLK's far larger AUM gives it greater liquidity, making it an ideal fund for active traders. It also focuses only on companies within the S&P 500, granting you exposure to the biggest names in tech. However, this concentration means you miss out on the growth potential of smaller companies.

FTEC's key strength is its superior diversification compared to XLK. With nearly 300 holdings, this ETF provides broader exposure across the technology sector. This also helps to cushion against a single stock impacting overall performance.

Whether FTEC or XLK is right for you depends on your goals. If you want greater liquidity and a focus on the big tech companies, XLK is the better choice. If you seek broad exposure to tech businesses to capture the greater upside potential of mid- and small-cap stocks, FTEC is the way to go.

The Golden Joystick Awards have been running for 44 years now. That's a staggering amount of time, especially since video games haven't been commercially available for much longer than that. It also means many of the ceremony's records are pretty well established.

During last night's show, however, one two-person indie shattered all expectations, scooping four awards and breaking one of those 44-year-old records in the process.

Meccha Chameleon was developed by two people: Lemorion and Haganeiro. It took roughly five months to develop, and it quickly became one of 2026's biggest hits, selling over 20 million copies (more than Pokemon Legends Z-A, God of War Ragnarok, and Resident Evil Village's lifetime sales).

Last night, it scooped four awards at the Golden Joysticks, sharing the most wins on the night with eventual Game of the Year winner Resident Evil Requiem. It topped every category it was nominated in, except Best PC Game, which was won by Crimson Desert, and those four wins meant it took home more awards at a single Golden Joysticks ceremony than any other indie game.

It beat Marathon, Valheim, and Arc Raiders to win Best Multiplayer; Mewgenics and Cairn to win Best Self-Published Indie; and Mortal Shell 2 and Orbitals to win Developers' Choice. It also won the Streamers' Choice award.

The previous record for most wins by an indie in a single night was shared by multiple games, at 2. That list contains some behemoths, including Hollow Knight: Silksong, Balatro, Cult of the Lamb, Hades, and Outer Wilds.

If you include Baldur's Gate 3 and Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 as indie, then Meccha Chameleon didn't quite grab the record, as both won seven in a single night; however, because their status is disputed, many won't include them.

Meccha Chameleon wasn't the only record breaker at last night's Golden Joysticks. With its win in the Best Remake/Remaster category, The Witcher 3 became the game with the most outright wins in the ceremony's history, with 10.

Capcom also had a stellar evening. Three of its games won awards: Pragmata for Best Supporting Actor, Onimusha: Way of the Sword for Best Sound Design, and Resident Evil Requiem picked up four wins, netting the show's overall Game of the Year for the studio. It also won Studio of the Year, beating Nintendo, HouseHouse, and Square Enix, winning that award with the highest vote percentage of the entire evening (57%). Spurs may be weighing up Oliver Irow's loan at Plymouth

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Mid-November remains a transfer window free zone and, most of the time, a speculation-free zone – but it is a time where clubs begin to really look towards the January transfer window and Plymouth Argyle may well have a situation developing involving Oliver Irow.

Having narrowly missed out on the top six and the play-off places with some excellent form in the second half of last season, the expectation was that Plymouth would again challenge for promotion back to the Championship this season.

The Pilgrims lost key striker Lorent Tolaj to Bristol City over the summer but they signed well themselves, most notably with the addition of Will Evans to partner Bim Pepple up-front.

Plymouth sit fifth in League One with seven victories and two draws in their opening 14 matches, which is in stark contrast to their start last year in which they collected just 13 points from their opening 14 games of last season and found themselves in the bottom four and the relegation places as late as the New Year.

Another signing that contributed to the bright start but has began to fade away from an individual perspective is Tottenham Hotspur loanee Oliver Irow – and it could well be that Spurs seek to find him a new home away from Home Park this winter.

Having spent time on loan at Mansfield Town in League One last season, Irow had shown flashes of quality for The Stags that suggested to Argyle that he could be a worthwhile contributor to their promotion challenge this season.

Irow scored a brace on debut for Mansfield in a 3-0 victory against eventually relegated Port Vale in January before some inconsistency. He did finish with a couple of assists in his final couple of appearances at the back end of the campaign, though.

Plymouth have been told to demand up to £2m for Bim Pepple, who had been on the radar of Sheffield United, Bolton and Portsmouth in the summer.

Set to kick on at Home Park, the 20-year-old former England U17 international made a bright start to life at Argyle with four goals by mid-September, including one against Premier League opposition Coventry City in the second round of the EFL Cup.

Irow notched in a 3-0 win against Barnsley with a superb thunderbolt of a strike inside the opening minute before a brace in a 4-2 victory against Blackpool a week later.

He started seven of Argyle’s opening eight games of the campaign in League One and, up until mid-October, appeared to be one of the first names on the teamsheet.

However, after a third successive start without a direct goal involvement since that 4-2 win away at Blackpool, Irow was dropped from Plymouth’s eleven after a 2-1 win against AFC Wimbledon in Devon.

In that game, Irow was hooked after 65 minutes with Plymouth having fought back from a goal down against the South Londoners with his replacement, Xavier Amaechi, going on to score their 95th minute winner.

Subsequently, Irow was dropped to the bench for the next two games – two games that Argyle lost, 2-0 against Huddersfield Town and then 3-0 away at Stevenage.

Failing to make an impact against either side, Irow was then an unused substitute as Argyle got back on the winning track with a 3-2 victory over Cambridge United before he again was given an even short cameo off the bench, of only ten minutes, in a goalless draw against Reading.

Struggling for form since a bright enough start, Irow played an hour against seventh-tier outfit Alvechurch in the first round of the FA Cup in a game that took Plymouth 50 minutes to eventually break their non-league opponents down and run in three after a triple substitution that saw Irow hooked alongside Malachi Boateng and Amaechi.

In midweek, perhaps a sign of Irow falling down the pecking order was further given by manager Tom Cleverley as he played the full game against League Two side Swindon Town in the group stages of the EFL Trophy with Irow again failing to get a goal involvement as Argyle snatched a last gasp victory.

That appearance against The Robins means that the Spurs loanee has now featured 15 times for the club this season but he has only contributed a goal involvement in just three of those matches.

At the start of his loan, it may have appeared as though Plymouth had picked up a gem ready to kick on and really assert himself at League One level but as each game goes by and confidence dwindles, there must be a growing feeling of regret from all parties.

Plymouth would want a player to be more regularly effective than scoring a goal in every five appearances on average, Spurs would want one of their highly-rated young talents to be trusted enough to be stuck by despite a dip and Irow himself can’t be happy with just cup starts and a bit-part role off the bench.

It has not been a prolonged dropping if Irow just yet, given he was starting league games a month ago, but the trend is not a good one.

It does not help Irow that six other players have matched or surpassed his goal involvement record in League One for Argyle this season and three of those being players of a similar ilk and in a similar position in Amaechi, Caleb Watts and Ronan Curtis.

Irow has shown his quality at both Mansfield and Plymouth but it has already shown a pattern that it tends to be bottled for short bursts, rather than sustained, and Spurs may well be weighing up whether to recall him and send him to a different side where extra time would be granted in League One or the level is lower to prove consistency at the top end of League Two.

Like Oreos, the heist genre is a malleable philosophical concept.

If you think about it, the best seasons of Mad Men, The Americans and Better Call Saul all were fundamentally structured as heist stories.

But television has been using its opportunities for longform storytelling to futz around with heist narratives — “Heist Double Stuf,” if you will — for decades, with mostly forgettable results.

You might remember FX’s Thief because Andre Braugher won an Emmy for it or Netflix’s Kaleidoscope for its “watch in any order you want” anarchy, but most recent entries in the genre are more like Hulu’s Culprits, which you probably haven’t even heard of.

Ultimately, it’s very possible that you’ll hear of Apple’s 12 12 12 (especially since if you’re reading this review, you just have). With Anthony Mackie and Jamie Dornan, it boasts a pair of reasonably well-known stars and, when I get to the part of this review that explains the premise, you’ll probably think the premise has some promise.

The chances of it remaining in any conversation about genre highlights, however, is close to nil. I’ve watched all eight episodes, and the first two illustrate the premise’s potential, the next four illustrate the premise’s limitations, and the last two scrap the premise entirely to deliver an increasingly irritating series of unexciting twists leading to an unsatisfying conclusion.

Mackie’s Jack Strickland, an FBI agent out to avenge the death of a partner he believes was murdered by master thief Paul Galvin (Dornan), explains the premise at the top of each episode.

“A high-asset robbery, professionally conceived, is 12 months in planning, 12 hours in execution. A bank robber who is not caught within 12 days is not caught at all,” he says, reciting from some FBI handbook.

See? 12, 12 and, also, 12. Get it? A bit like And And And, the original name of the band in The Commitments. Only worse.

So the plot, as created by Jim Keeble and Dudi Appleton, unfolds in three interlocking narratives that go something like this: There’s “The Heist,” an hour-by-hour breakdown as Paul, generically hot-headed brother Bobby (Jack Kesy), and a team of familiar specialists — including train-loving Kira (Kali Reis), master tunneler Serge (Jose Garcia), contortionist Alberich (Julius Feldmeier), inside woman Gunda (Hildegard Schroedter) and several others — break into a highly secure bank in Zurich. Their target? Roughly a billion dollars in cash newly deposited by some vicious Russian oligarchs.

There’s “The Planning,” a month-by-month breakdown, as we see the events that conspire to bring Paul out of a comfortable retirement with the lovely (and pregnant) Severine (Agathe Rousselle) and move pieces around the board in anticipation of the heist.

And there’s “The Takedown,” a day-by-day breakdown, as Strickland and Swiss cop Lena (Sallie Harmsen) are trying to catch the thieves, testing the meticulousness of Paul’s plan and the depths of Strickland’s obsession.

The first two episodes, directed by prestige streaming favorite Kari Skogland, are derivative but generally proficient. The opening shot, in which Alberich unfolds his body from a safe deposit box to emerge in a locked vault, is genuinely evocative even if it’s not the last time you’ll think of Ocean’s 11, and think that Ocean’s 11 did it better and more efficiently. The stakes are established simply, a few of the characters are introduced competently, and there’s a dual chase scene — intercutting foot and car pursuits through whichever European city is playing Zurich — that’s edited slickly.

Yes, I know all that praise is damningly faint, but nothing in 12 12 12 is inspired and I’m a sucker for even the most generic of heist pieces if production is clean and professional. This is all of those things, to a point.

Really, the “point” comes in the fourth episode, when a character gets buried in a man-made avalanche that was capable of burying a cabin, yet he’s just fine one scene later; and we discover that the avalanche was part of the getaway plan that’s never mentioned again in the planning segment, because it’s stupid and reliant on the sort of CG effects that the show never uses any other time and probably shouldn’t have used this time. It’s here that it first became clear to me that just because Paul is one of those preternaturally prognosticative protagonists capable of seeing the chess board a hundred moves in advance doesn’t mean the creators of 12 12 12 have similar gifts.

What started as a steady and balanced piece of storytelling begins to lurch along with no real destination in sight. Then the last two episodes are pretty much nonsense, which is disappointing because they’re directed by Skogland, who ditches the confidence of the first two episodes to stage a ludicrous nighttime chase/animal encounter that produced gales of unintentional laughter.

I’m not sure if the middle of the season meanders because of how uninteresting Jack and Paul are as adversaries, and that’s why Mackie and Dornan’s performances become less engaging; or if Mackie and Dornan’s performances become less engaging, and the show meanders as a result of their lack of intensity. Maybe it’s both.

Mackie is an actor who simmers well at a low level, but he isn’t the man for deep, all-consuming compulsion, which makes him a bad pick for a character who is a nondescript Ahab on the trail of a nondescript Moby Dick. Jack makes one or two references to his race being a driving force for his mania to avenge his deceased partner and set things right, but that has the feeling of a late-in-production note to dimensionalize the character. It fails. I don’t know if the series would have been better if Harmsen’s Lena had been as important to Jack and the series in the middle as she is at the beginning and end, but she’s forgotten for whole episodes at a stretch.

Dornan’s best performances have come either when he was able to go fully dark (The Fall) or fully quirky (Barb and Star Go to Vista Del Mar), but Paul is humorless and bland and his rough edges have been sanded away entirely.

If the three-timeline structure, giving us the two main characters in three very different contexts, fails to yield anything like a compelling set of foils, it has served no purpose at all.

The supporting characters rarely stand out either. Kesy’s interpretation of Bobby’s scheming volatility is used as a consistent and never convincing red herring, without the character ever really feeling dangerous or like a person. I always like Olafur Darri Olafsson, but I have no idea what his character’s role is in the heist. At least I know his name is “Blasius,” when there are three or four people who help with the heist in unspecified ways and might not even have names. Reis, so good in True Detective: Night Country, is barely used at all. Patrick Fabian has one or two good scenes as one of Jack’s FBI bosses, who resents him for multiple good reasons.

In Oreo terms, this “special edition” isn’t as good as the BTS Brown Sugar Pancake, nor is it as icky as the Apple Pie a la Mode, which managed to simulate neither apple pie nor ice cream in any recognizable way. But if the question is “Has this heist show done anything to improve on the original formula, or even to justify permanence in the heist genre firmament?” the answer is an unequivocal “Nope.”
