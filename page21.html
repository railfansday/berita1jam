One Familiar Item Of Clothing Scored Lowest for Intelligence — Can You Guess Which?

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

One everyday garment came last for both perceived intelligence and trustworthiness.

One everyday garment came last for both perceived intelligence and trustworthiness.

Track pants, also known as tracksuit bottoms, receive the lower ratings for perceived intelligence compared with blouses, ordinary trousers or long skirts in a study of women’s clothing.

Of the six types of clothing the researchers compared, track pants also received the lowest average ratings for trustworthiness.

Blouses topped the rankings for intelligence and trustworthiness but scored near the bottom for confidence.

Short skirts showed almost the reverse pattern, scoring near the top for confidence and lower than long skirts on both intelligence and trustworthiness.

Short skirts also gave the strongest impression of sexual interest, showing how much people can read into a hemline.

Long skirts fell between short skirts and blouses on confidence, while ordinary trousers scored higher than tracksuit bottoms on both intelligence and trustworthiness.

Onesies gave the strongest impression of happiness and scored about as high as short skirts for confidence.

White clothes suggested a happier wearer than grey clothes did, with black clothes coming last.

These examples show how the impression changes with the question being asked: a blouse came out ahead for intelligence, while a onesie came out ahead for happiness.

The researchers began by asking about 200 people what they thought clothes could tell them about someone and comparing their answers with traits examined in previous research.

They then gathered ratings on 5,000 photographs from online shops, covering five kinds of impressions: intelligence, confidence, trustworthiness, happiness and sexual interest.

Participants formed these impressions from photographs of the garments alone, without seeing anyone wearing them.

The results showed that who was doing the judging explained more of the variation in ratings than which garment they saw.

For example, one person might associate tracksuit bottoms with training for a race, while another might picture someone spending a Sunday on the sofa.

The researchers draw a parallel between judging people by their clothes and judging them by their faces:

“That is, much like inferences from faces, the inferred attributes often lie in the eye of the beholder.”

The study was published in the Journal of Computational Social Science (Rosenbusch et al., 2021).

This 80-Year-Old Uses Two Bodyweight Moves to Stay Strong and Independent—No Gym Needed

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Personal trainer Nicola Bretherton shared her mom's home routine, which helps her maintain functional strength, coordination, mobility and balance.

Fat-loss coach Nicola Bretherton helps women build fitness, confidence, and strength for today and for life.

“Especially as you get older...cardiovascular fitness is important but so is building and maintaining muscle,” the founder of the Next Level You program said on Instagram. “[It's necessary] for your strength, bones, and long-term health.”

She highlighted that if changing body composition is your goal, strength training is especially valuable.

“If you’re trying to lose fat and change your body shape, you need to give your muscles a reason to grow and that means challenging them and progressively getting stronger,” she said.

A prime example of maintaining strength and physical capability with age is Nicola’s own mother, whose home exercise routine she recently shared online. The moves build full-body functionality and the ability to carry out everyday tasks.

“This is my mom who will be 80 in October,” she said on Instagram.

“She is a strong lady! She lifts weights at home, does lots of bodyweight exercises—full press-ups! She can even do the splits! I’m so proud of the woman she is—strong, capable, and independent,” she wrote.

“Most 80-year-olds can stand up from a chair, but did you know that most 80 year olds cannot get up from the floor?” Bretherton pointed out. “And that's often because of a lack of strength, balance and mobility.”

The movement also requires coordination and flexibility.

“Most 80 year olds cannot perform a full body weight press up from the floor,” noted Bretherton. “A proper press up requires upper-body pushing strength, core stability and shoulder stability.”

She reminded readers of the real-world transferability of the movement.

“Imagine being able to push yourself back up after a stumble or catching yourself with your hands. This is why you don't train for the body you have now, but the body that you will need later.”

How This Man Transformed His Body with 20-Minute Home Workouts and Minimal Equipment

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

After years of feeling “skinny fat,” Skye Mackintosh set himself a challenge.

Between work, family commitments, and a social life, finding time to exercise can feel like one of the biggest barriers to getting in shape.

Skye Mackintosh set out to prove that you don't need hours in the gym to transform your physique. Instead, he committed to just 20 minutes of exercise every day, documenting his progress over almost a year.

After 340 days, his transformation has attracted thousands of followers. Throughout the challenge, Mackintosh relied on simple home workouts using little more than his bodyweight, a kettlebell, and a handful of basic equipment. More recently, he added a weighted vest to keep challenging himself.

Mackintosh's motivation came after years of feeling unhappy with his physique.

“As a teenager I was naturally skinny,” he wrote on Instagram. ‘Then, as an adult, my age, eating habits, and lack of consistent exercise caught up to me. For 10 years I was ‘skinny fat.’”

Before beginning the challenge, he focused on improving his diet. After cutting out added sugar for a year, he lost 18 pounds. But the weight loss alone wasn't enough to make him feel the way he wanted, prompting him to start strength training at home.

“I cut out added sugar for a year and lost 18lbs,” he shared. “I felt better but not great. I knew that I needed to change and that accountability would help. So I started working out in my bedroom and posting it online. I was humbled and blown away that thousands rallied to support and keep me going. I put on about 8 pounds and made it to day 100.”

Another 100 days later, he'd gained roughly 18 pounds in total. While he felt stronger, he admitted: “I felt strong but didn't feel confident with how I was looking. The extra weight had me feeling a bit sluggish.”

Mackintosh's next goal was to reduce his body fat while holding on to the muscle he'd built. By Day 300, he weighed 3 pounds less than he had on Day 1, but looked considerably leaner and more muscular.

“I feel strong, athletic, and most importantly: happy. I learned to trust myself and the process,” said Mackintosh.

Mackintosh's transformation is a reminder that you don't need marathon gym sessions to make meaningful progress. Twenty minutes of strength training performed consistently can add up over time, particularly when paired with progressive overload and a supportive diet.

As his results show, the best training plan isn't necessarily the most complicated—it's the one you can stick to for the long term.

Here's the Average Woman's Social Security Benefit by Age

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Women's average Social Security benefit runs about $410 less monthly than men's, a gap rooted in earnings history and workforce participation.

Social Security benefits for women average less than those for men across every age group. This report lays out the specific dollar amounts as of December 2025, the most recent data available, to show the current size of that gap.

The average benefit among women is $1,872.43 per month, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA). That figure is $409.85 lower than the $2,282.28 average monthly benefit for men.

The table below shows the average monthly payments for retirees aged 62 to 70. These figures reflect the amounts as of December 2025 and illustrate the consistent difference in checks between men and women.

The SSA bases your benefit on an average of your wages across your highest-earning 35 years. It adjusts those earlier wages for inflation and runs the results through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. The persistent gap in benefit amounts between men and women reflects historical differences in lifetime earnings and fewer years spent in the paid workforce.

These payments are designed to replace only about 40% of your pre-retirement income, which is well short of what most retirees need to cover their costs.

Full retirement age (FRA) ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. Workers who claim at 62 accept a reduction in their monthly check, while those who wait until 70 earn delayed retirement credits worth 8% per year.

While women receive lower average monthly checks, those benefits typically have to stretch over a longer period. A woman who is 65 today is expected to live about 21 more years, compared to about 18 more years for a man, according to the SSA. This means the average woman needs her retirement income to last roughly three years longer than the average man.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027 to help these checks keep pace with inflation. For a 70-year-old woman receiving the average $2,024.08 benefit, that adjustment adds about $73 per month to that figure. The dollar gap is widest at the oldest ages in the table, and spousal and survivor benefits are part of the picture for many women.

The above information focuses on benefits for retired workers, but many women claim spousal or survivor benefits based on their partner's work records instead. This could give them more money than they'd qualify for on their own.

Spousal benefits are the benefits you're eligible for on your partner's work record while they're still alive. They're worth up to one-half of the amount your partner qualifies for at their FRA. You will only be able to receive this amount once your spouse is claiming their retirement benefits.

Survivor benefits are the benefits you're eligible for on your partner's work record after they pass away. These are worth up to 100% of the amount your spouse was eligible for or receiving at the time of their death. You will only get this amount if it's larger than your own retirement benefit.

Contact the Social Security Administration to learn more about whether you qualify for these benefits and how much they could be worth to you.

Marketers Are BURNED OUT - And No One Gives AF

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I have read countless posts about bloated marketing roles where six jobs are crammed into one title. One listing asked for a “Marketing Manager” to handle brand strategy, paid media, SEO, email marketing, copywriting, and front-end web design. All of that... on a junior salary! These are not rare. They are the norm these days. Scroll LinkedIn or Reddit for five minutes and you will see post after post of people venting about being stretched thin, underpaid, and told to be “grateful for the opportunity.”

I have been reading these for a YEAR now. Watching the same story play out. Marketers are asking, “Is it just me, or has marketing completely lost the plot? Is this the job I wanted? Should I just switch careers?” I even asked the same things myself. I used to love marketing. Now, I don't even know what to say.

What even was marketing before all of this? Was it always like this and we are just noticing it now? Or did it get worse in the last few years?

What used to be a blend of strategy and execution turned into survival mode. AI promised efficiency. Instead, it created more expectation. Faster turnarounds. More output. Tighter budgets. Leadership sees AI as a shortcut, not a tool. So the workload? It went up. Headcount? Went down. Pressure? TRIPLED.

The marketer role did not evolve. It got gutted and repackaged.

This article is not about the trends. Not the hype. Just focusing on the one question we all dread to be answered. How real is marketer burnout, and how did we get here?

What happens when your creative energy gets consumed by meetings, client briefs, and endless edits? How do you stay motivated when everyone blames your team for poor performance but no one credits you when a campaign wins? Why do marketers, especially in digital, feel like they are always running on empty?

Burnout in marketing is not new. What’s different now is how fast it spreads and how deeply it affects people across roles. From content specialists and media buyers to strategists and SEO managers, burnout is everywhere. Not because people are lazy or bad at their jobs. They are overwhelmed, underappreciated, and stuck in systems that demand more while offering less.

Marketers today wear too many hats. Generalist roles stretch across brand, analytics, operations, and sales support. It used to be enough to run great creative. Now you are expected to prove ROI, optimize in real-time, adjust to AI tools, and still produce on-brand messaging across ten platforms. The second something underperforms, marketing takes the fall.

But when results are good? Sales or other department will take the credit. That’s not an exaggeration. One Reddit comment I saw earlier hit it perfectly: “The sales team shouldn't be the only ones getting commission since we (content team) did all the work.” That’s the norm in many agencies and in-house teams.

Marketers drive interest, create value, and nurture leads. Sales closes once the hard part is already done.

There’s a word for that dynamic: exhausting.

Burnout is tied to more than just being busy. It’s the layoffs. The increased cost of living. The consolidation of roles. Managers freeze raises. Employees fear losing their jobs, so they overperform to avoid the next round of cuts. That creates a cycle. You take on more. You work harder. You don’t complain. And then your reward is more responsibility, not more support. Not a better salary.

You become your own bottleneck. You say yes to everything because the team is small and leadership needs “just one more push.” Eventually, you hit a wall. Creativity dies. Enthusiasm fades. Work turns into survival.

Marketing roles are highly visible but rarely respected. Everyone thinks they can do your job. Leadership gives vague direction but expects strong positioning. Product teams shift priorities mid-sprint. Sales wants new decks with impossible turnaround times. People expect you to spin gold out of nonsense with a smile on your face.

Meanwhile, your inbox fills up with subject lines like “quick tweak,” “minor edit,” and “urgent request.” These aren’t minor. They’re entire projects disguised as favors. And they never stop.

This is why boundaries matter. The problem is, setting boundaries gets seen as being “difficult” or “not a team player.” So people stop trying. They take the hits in silence.

A common theme in marketing burnout is the rejection of sales culture. Some marketers say, “I don’t want to do sales. I’m an introvert.” That mindset makes you easy to ignore in corporate environments. One Reddit commenter put it plainly. Marketing is sales. You sell ideas to bosses, value props to clients, and narratives to users. You’re not on the phone closing deals, but you are pitching all the time.

People who thrive in marketing know how to talk. They present data with confidence. They make their case without waiting for approval. They build allies. And yes, sometimes they party with clients to keep them loyal.

The problem is not that marketers don’t work hard. It’s that most of them don’t get taught how to sell themselves. That disconnect builds resentment over time.

Fixing burnout in marketing requires more than taking a week off or downloading a mindfulness app. Companies and leadership need structural change.

1. Credit needs to be shared. If a campaign brings in leads, content, media, and analytics should all get credit. This is not a favor. It’s the truth! Stop handing out bonuses to sales without acknowledging who did the heavy lifting.

2. Boundaries must be normalized. No one should work weekends to chase someone else’s last-minute idea. Make deadlines real. Protect personal time. Normalize saying no.

3. Redefine performance. Measuring success only through ROI creates a narrow path to burnout. Not every channel performs the same way. Not every campaign wins in thirty days. Look at long-term impact, brand growth, and engagement quality.

4. Advocate up the chain. Teach marketers how to pitch. Not just to customers. To leadership. Make the case for headcount, for tools, for strategy. If you don’t make noise, you won’t get noticed.

5. Say the quiet part out loud. Marketers need space to admit when they are tired. When they feel ignored. When they need clarity. Create a culture where people can be honest without fear.

Burnout doesn’t mean you are in the wrong field. It means the structure around you is broken. You shouldn’t need to chase credit. You shouldn’t need to hide exhaustion. You shouldn’t be constantly afraid of layoffs or budget cuts or being replaced by automation.

Good marketing is emotional work. It requires A LOT of creativity, patience, and most importantly clarity. You cannot fake that for long. At some point, your output suffers and your health goes with it.

There is a way forward, but it starts with truth. Stop pretending everything’s fine. Start fixing the real problems.

If your team is burned out, they don’t need a pep talk. They need help. Give them that, or lose them.

Neuroscientists say people can be "trained" to recognize when they're dreaming — in other words, lucid dreaming may be an acquirable skill.

When you purchase through links on our site, we may earn an affiliate commission. Here’s how it works.

During dreams, bizarre scenes you encounter can seem as tangible as reality; you feel as though you're really flying through the sky or being chased through a jungle of bright-purple trees. But in special cases, people realize they're in the midst of a dream as it's unfolding.

Called lucid dreaming, this phenomenon occurs when we suddenly regain our capacity for metacognition, or thinking about our own thoughts, in the middle of a dream. Lucid dreamers can sometimes even control the content or trajectory of their dreams. Now, researchers claim that they've found a way to "train" people to lucid dream by having them meditate and complete virtual-reality-augmented exercises while they're awake.

In a study involving a few dozen participants, the scientists found that repeating this training twice a week for a month significantly increased how often participants experienced lucid dreaming. They say the approach could have practical applications as an intervention for people with nightmares, anxiety or post-traumatic stress disorder — but those applications are theoretical, for now.

5 unexpected uses for your old smartwatch

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Breath new life into your old tech with these handy tips.

Upgrading to one of the best smartwatches can be exciting, but it often means your old smartwatch is left with nothing to do. Unfortunately, it's at this point that users may toss their old smartwatch in a drawer and forget about it. But it doesn't have to be like that.

Your old smartwatch is a versatile piece of hardware, so despite how it seems, that small device that's been gathering dust still has some pretty unexpected and interesting uses. With the right guidance, you can give your old smartwatch a new lease on life, or at the very least, you could sell it for some quick cash. Even if your smartwatch no longer receives major OS updates, you can still use it as a dedicated AI tool, an easy-access note-taking device, a child monitoring tool, or a dedicated sleep tracker. So free your old smartwatch from its drawer and blow the dust off. Most of these unexpected uses only take a few minutes to set up and require an extra app or two, though some require a cellular plan or a phone for full functionality.

If you use AI tools like Gemini or ChatGPT, one of the more relevant uses for your old smartwatch is to turn it into a dedicated AI assistant device. Setting things up is pretty simple, allowing you to get answers from or talk to your AI, all without having to pull out your phone. On Wear OS smartwatches, Gemini is the primary option. While on the Apple Watch, Siri is the default — but you don't have to stick with Siri.

On watches running Wear OS 4 or later, you'll need to first make sure Gemini is installed and has been updated. Once that's sorted and voice activation is enabled, all you need to do is say "Hey Google," and Gemini will respond. If voice activation isn't your style, you can alternatively press and hold the side button to activate Gemini. On Samsung watches, you'll need to customize the side button so it can activate Gemini. To reassign, open Settings > Button and gestures > Double press > scroll to where you see Gemini and select it.

On the Apple Watch, activating Siri is straightforward; simply raise your wrist and say "Hey Siri" or press and hold the Digital Crown. You can use the Wrist AI app to gain ChatGPT or Gemini functionality, but the free tier only allows a small number of chats daily. For easier access, you can add a Wrist AI complication to your watch face. For Wear OS watches, to get ChatGPT going, you'll need the WristAssist app, but it's been removed from the Play Store, so you will have to sideload it. You'll also need an API key from OpenAI to get things going; creating the key is free, but usage is billed.

A good use case for your old smartwatch is simply making it a note-taking device thanks to more accurate AI transcriptions. So instead of having to pull out your phone from your pocket every time you get an idea or want to jot down some thoughts, you can raise your wrist and speak.

Your old smartwatch likely already has a note or voice recorder app, so what you need is preinstalled. On Apple Watch, you can say "Hey Siri," then "Record a voice memo" to get started.

You can get transcriptions of the notes recorded on both Wear OS watches and Apple Watch, but you'll need your phone. On Samsung watches, you can get transcriptions in the voice recorder app using the built-in speech-to-text mode, but it has a 10-minute limit. Recordings taken on your smartwatch should automatically sync across your devices, so a note you recorded during your morning walk should be waiting on your phone by the time you get home.

There are few things as important to the human body as sleep, and your old smartwatch can help you improve it. But you'll have to sacrifice charging your watch overnight, potentially leaving you with a drained battery in the morning. And that's precisely where your old watch comes in. You can charge it all day, then wear it to bed while your main smartwatch charges.

Setup usually takes just a few minutes. To track your sleep and view your sleep data on Apple Watch, set up Sleep in the Health app on your iPhone, then turn on the Track Sleep with Apple Watch setting. For a Galaxy Watch, connect the watch to your phone, then open the Sleep tile on your Galaxy Watch and follow the prompts to get it set up. Keep in mind that how well this works and how much data it can track depends on the model of your smartwatch, because they don't all offer the same sensors, especially older models.

Lastly, don't treat the reported numbers with the same weight as a diagnosis from your doctor, because sensors can be inaccurate. So, if you notice anything concerning in your sleep report, take it as a cue to talk to your doctor, not as a diagnosis.

Your old smartwatch can find a new purpose on your child's wrist as a dedicated kids' watch. When set up, your kids can call, text, and share locations without owning a smartphone. Do note that this only works on LTE/Cellular smartwatches, and a smartphone isn't needed for the child after configuration.

Samsung watches have a dedicated Kids mode that gives parents plenty of oversight over their child's experience. So apart from the usual location monitoring, calls, and texts, parents can also approve or block apps, set School Time limits, and use Google Family Link to manage the child's watch usage. Unfortunately, Kids mode isn't available on every Samsung Watch; you'll need an LTE Galaxy Watch 6 and above.

Apple Watch offers a similar feature called Apple Watch for Your Kids, and it's supported on Series 4 or later with cellular, or an SE with cellular on watchOS 7 or later. As you'd expect, it can make calls and send texts, has location tracking, screen time management, and Schooltime mode, which, like Samsung's equivalent, is basically restricted so the child doesn't get distracted during school hours.

Keep in mind that cellular features require a carrier plan, which means additional fees for you. And carrier support varies, so some carriers may not support standalone calling from a smartwatch.

If none of the uses work for you, or you'd just prefer to get rid of your device, then your best option is to sell your old smartwatch. It probably still holds some resale value; it's only a matter of finding the right place (or way) to sell it. There are two main routes to go with.

First, you can sell on marketplaces like eBay. Selling on a marketplace may give you the highest payout, but it also comes with the most hassles. You'll have to handle everything concerning the sale, including writing an accurate listing, taking clear pictures, setting a good price, answering questions from window shoppers, shipping it, and then calculating how much platform fees cost you.

Selling to a buyback company like Greenbuyback is the second option that will likely net you less than a marketplace, but it can be more convenient. Simply get a quote online, then ship your old smartwatch to the buyer. Once it's received, it'll be inspected, and if the condition you described matches, you get paid. If it doesn't, you can receive a revised offer. How long the whole process takes depends on the specific program; some pay in as little as 24 business hours after receiving your watch, while others can take up to five business days.

If you don't mind getting store credit and less value for your old smartwatch, then you can trade in your old smartwatch to retailers like Best Buy or manufacturer programs like Apple Trade In. Before you sell or trade in your old smartwatch, make sure to unpair it from your phone and wipe it. On an Apple Watch, unpairing erases it. On Wear OS, sign out of your accounts and factory reset it.

Lost in the Labyrinth of Nicotine Products

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

— Clinicians need a better way to talk about nicotine risk

Americans increasingly navigate a fast-changing marketplace of nicotine and tobacco products. Walk into a convenience store today and you will encounter a dizzying array of products: cigarettes, e-cigarettes, nicotine pouches, smokeless tobacco, snus, and heated tobacco products, often sold in bright packaging and flavors designed to appeal.

Accompanying this is a confusing information environment. Public health campaigns warn about the dangers of using any tobacco, while the FDA has authorized certain products to carry a modified-risk claim. News stories describe an epidemic of youth vaping, while research reports find that e-cigarettes can help adult smokers quit. Marketing of nicotine and tobacco products often features young, healthy-looking individuals engaged in athletic activities and promises "smoke-free" or "cleaner" experiences, while warnings on products emphasize the dangers of use.

A recent op-ed asserting that the risk of nicotine and tobacco products operates on a continuum illustrates the confusion. Although the byline was attributed to Tom Price, MD, former HHS secretary, a disclosure at the end of the article indicates that it was, in fact, produced in partnership with and paid for by PMI Corporate Services Inc. (Philip Morris International -- a major tobacco company).

While no tobacco products are completely safe, the FDA has recognized that combustible tobacco products (i.e., products that are burned and smoked) like cigarettes pose the greatest health risks and that noncombustible products generally fall lower on the risk spectrum. Reflecting this, the agency has permitted certain products to be marketed with a modified-risk claim that using those products instead of cigarettes reduces the risk of several serious diseases.

As researchers in tobacco communication and regulatory science, we argue that public health agencies and healthcare practitioners have a responsibility to help people navigate this complicated environment. As the nicotine and tobacco marketplace changes, so should our communication about the harm of these products. Individuals in the U.S. are confronted with an assortment of seemingly conflicting messages about nicotine and tobacco products. It is easy to see why people might not fully understand that different products present varying harms.

For someone who does not use any nicotine or tobacco products, the advice is straightforward: Don't start. Nicotine is addictive, and products containing nicotine present a variety of risks, including cardiovascular diseases. For someone who smokes cigarettes, quitting entirely is the best option, and approved treatments such as nicotine patches, gum, and lozenges, and prescription medications should generally be considered as first-line approaches.

But real life is rarely so tidy. Some people try repeatedly to quit and cannot. Others are not yet ready to give up using nicotine and tobacco products. For them, understanding the differences in risk between continuing to smoke cigarettes and using nicotine and tobacco products that are not burnt can impact their health. Eliminating cigarette smoke exposure, even if someone continues to use nicotine and tobacco, can lead to a meaningful reduction in harm.

Clinicians have recommended considering e-cigarettes as a smoking cessation tool for patients who smoke. However, as the marketplace expands far beyond e-cigarettes, doctors, public health agencies, and other trusted voices should be prepared to explain a continuum of risk among various nicotine and tobacco products rather than centering on any single product. This does not mean handing smokers a shopping list. It does not mean calling vapes, nicotine pouches, or smokeless tobacco harmless. And it certainly does not mean encouraging people who do not currently use nicotine to start.

It means being transparent about what we know: Cigarettes are extraordinarily dangerous, and nicotine is what makes them so hard to quit. Some other products can deliver nicotine, but carry substantially lower risks to one's health. Switching completely matters, and using cigarettes alongside another product does not deliver the same risk reduction.

To be clear, we do not know for certain the best way to deliver the information to people who smoke. It could be conversations between healthcare providers and their patients, public education campaigns, a trusted source anyone can consult easily, or some combination of the above. Each approach has trade-offs in reach, cost, and the risk of unintended consequences. What we need is evidence on which approaches help smokers understand relative risk without encouraging nicotine and tobacco product use among people who otherwise would not start.

One key principle is ensuring that whatever the format this communication takes, the information comes from credible and independent sources with no stake in selling these products.

This conversation cannot wait because people are already hearing claims in ads and on social media about reduced harm of certain nicotine and tobacco products. Influencers portray nicotine pouches as a lifestyle, with talk of "upper deckies" and what it means to be a "Zynner." Increasingly, people will see modified-risk claims that the FDA has authorized. If health professionals respond with silence or oversimplified messages, the tobacco industry, social media, and word of mouth will fill the gap. Communication revolving around nicotine and tobacco product harm should help people make sense of the growing array of products and understand how each one works and what risks they carry.

Some worry that acknowledging reduced-risk products sounds like endorsing the tobacco industry. Given the industry's history of deceiving consumers about the risks presented by tobacco, skepticism is warranted. But withholding meaningful information, supported by evidence from the scientific community, on the risk differences is not the answer.

We can hold two ideas at once: No nicotine or tobacco product is safe, and some products are far more dangerous than others. For decades, public health has taught Americans how dangerous cigarettes are, and we should not undermine that achievement. But an evolving marketplace demands an evolving message -- one that tells people not only what to avoid, but how products differ, what switching can and cannot accomplish, and where the evidence remains uncertain. Americans navigating an increasingly complicated nicotine marketplace deserve an accurate roadmap to protect the public's health.
