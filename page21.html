When We Forgive, What New Meaning Do We Find in Life?

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Posted November 20, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

Suffering could lead to despair, depression, hopelessness, and even not liking oneself (Yu et al. 2021; Reed & Enright, 2006). Yet one aspect of the forgiveness process, especially as one nears the end of the forgiveness journey, is the sometimes surprising development of finding meaning in that suffering and a new purpose in life. Finding meaning centers on an important question: What have I learned from going through this suffering, and what have I learned as I walked the journey of forgiveness? This is a cognitive exercise that can lead to new short-term and long-term goals (Enright, 2015). In other words, the cognitive exercise of finding meaning can lead to behaviors that support new purposes in one’s life.

The psychotherapist and Holocaust survivor, Viktor Frankl (1969/2014), pioneered this idea of finding meaning in suffering as he endured the persecution of his imprisonment. His motivation was to resist being destroyed by the abuse. By finding meaning, one can transcend suffering and make it more bearable.

When I discuss the theme of finding meaning in suffering through forgiveness, I am not focusing on the injustice but on its aftermath. The basic question is this: What are you learning about life as you extend mercy to those who were deeply unfair to you? This post explores the different meanings people tend to generate as they forgive.

Let us first start by examining what finding meaning in suffering is not, because it can be misunderstood.

Consider three themes that sometimes are confused with finding meaning in what an unjustly acting person has done to the forgiver.

First, the forgiver is not denying the disappointment, sadness, anger, or grief in what was suffered. The injustice did happen, and the negative response is real. In other words, finding meaning is not some kind of non-feeling, interpreted, at least temporarily, as relief from the effects of the injustice.

Second, the person does not play games with themselves, stating, for example, “Oh well, things happen for a reason, and I’ll just make the best of it by accepting it.” Yes, people can “make the best of it,” but if this is an exclusive meaning of suffering, the one who forgives is not going after the woundedness inside, which may linger for a long time. The “oh well” approach is passive. To heal from the effects of the injustice, a person needs a more active approach to the pain.

Third, the forgiver does not distort reality by thinking, “Well, all things happen for good reasons, and so I will try to see the good in what happened to me.” The injustice was not good. Some aspects of what happens after the injustice may be good, but the injustice itself is not.

1. Being more sensitive to the suffering of others, even those who never hurt you: James, upon forgiving, realized he had what he called a “short temper,” in which he imposed his anger on unsuspecting others. He has now committed, for example, to being more patient and kind to cashiers in grocery stores and pharmacies as he waits in line. He is more sensitive to the fatigue and stress of those working behind the cash register. This gives him a new sense of purpose, as he recognizes his intemperance in these contexts and now works to overcome his anger.

2. Being motivated to help others in their suffering: When Dr. Freedman (Freedman & Enright, 1996) worked with female incest survivors on a long-term forgiveness intervention, she saw that many of them, upon forgiving, became motivated to help other incest survivors overcome their deep resentment. They were not choosing as a goal to become mental health professionals, but instead to be of assistance to others who have suffered as they have.

As another example, I talked with a man who is imprisoned for life. Upon forgiving those who abused him, he said to me that he knows he will never get out of the correctional institution, but now he is motivated to help his cellmates forgive and to be unburdened of their anger. This led to a new purpose, or the behavioral manifestation of this desire to assist others.

3. Standing in the pain so you do not throw that pain onto others: The paradox of standing in the pain is this: As people bear the negative effects of unjust treatment by not throwing that pain back to the offending person or to unsuspecting others, the pain-bearers realize they are stronger than they thought. They see the positive outcomes of not seeking revenge. As Gandhi said, if we keep taking an eye for an eye, the whole world will be blind. The pain-bearers realize they can now translate this into a new purpose of contributing to a more peaceful world.

4. Forgiving helps us think more deeply about justice: If people are continually treated unjustly, they can find meaning in living a life of mercy and fairness integrated together. Thus, their understanding of justice can become wiser, and therefore their appropriation of justice can become more helpful to others.

5. Eventually, you can develop an overall philosophy of life, such as committing to be a conduit of good in this world: This is what Dr. Frankl called supra-meaning, or a new philosophy of life that encompasses an overall approach to how one interacts with others and why one does so in this way. For example, a person can commit to living a life of greater respect, kindness, and love toward both offenders and others. For example, Sally decided to resist impatience and negative emotions toward her own children, as a way of life in her family, so that these life-giving moral virtues can become norms there.

6. As people find meaning in their suffering, they can put their future purpose in life into sharper perspective: Finding meaning, in other words, becomes the cognitive rationale and emotional motivation to seek what matters to them.

In summary, our suffering is not for nothing. People who suffer and forgive can develop as they think carefully about what they are learning about suffering, their own humanity, and life itself. As Dr. Frankl learned and passed his wisdom on to us, finding meaning in suffering opens unexpected doors for survival and growing in our humanity.

1 High-Flying Stock to Buy Hand Over Fist if the Stock Market Crashes

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Moderna's cancer vaccine data were historic, but the stock prices in a flawless future.

By Prosper Junior Bakiny – Nov 20, 2026 at 12:21PM EST

Innovation often pays off. Moderna (MRNA -0.92%) has proven as much this year. The company's shares have soared in 2026 on the back of impressive clinical and regulatory progress. But is there any upside left for the vaccine maker? Not in my view. But Moderna would be a great stock to buy if the stock market crashes. Read on to find out more.

Moderna is pioneering mRNA-based personalized cancer vaccines. These products offer the potential to train patients' immune systems to recognize and destroy cancer cells based on specific mutations present in each patient's tumor. This approach could lead to much greater treatment efficacy than conventional cancer medicines. Personalized cancer vaccines may also more effectively prevent recurrence.

Moderna provided the first positive Phase 3 clinical trial data for an investigational personalized cancer vaccine, intismeran autogene, earlier this year. Moderna is developing intismeran autogene with Merck (MRK +1.59%), and in this Phase 3 study, the personalized cancer vaccine was tested as a combination therapy with Keytruda -- the best-selling cancer drug in the world -- versus Keytruda alone in certain patients with melanoma. Intismeran autogene, combined with Keytruda, led to meaningful improvements in recurrence-free survival. This clinical win sent Moderna's shares up by more than 100% overnight.

The market is seeing beyond Moderna's clinical win. The company proved that personalized cancer vaccines can reach significant clinical success. Intismeran autogene hasn't done so yet, but based on its Phase 3 results, it should eventually generate significant sales. Some analysts expect the personalized cancer vaccine to generate more than $10 billion in revenue at its peak, especially as it earns approvals across additional indications beyond melanoma.

It is being invested in as a treatment across several other forms of cancer. And the rest of the company's lineup and pipeline also looks robust. For instance, Moderna has earned approval for mFLUSIVA, an influenza vaccine, earlier this year. Its coronavirus vaccine is still on the market, and it is developing several other candidates in infectious diseases.

Some of the company's targets look ambitious. Moderna is developing a potential HIV vaccine, for instance. There is a long road ahead for this candidate, but if it is successful, it could be a game changer. That said, Moderna's most important strength isn't any single pipeline candidate or currently approved product.

It is the company's innovative mRNA-based platform that should produce significant clinical milestones over the next five to 10 years. However, there is a problem. The market may have already factored Moderna's success into its share price. Consider that the company's market cap is $82 billion, as of writing. Even assuming Moderna could generate $10 billion in sales next year (which it won't), it would be trading at 8.2x estimated 2027 sales, a fairly expensive valuation for a biotech stock of this size.

True, more clinical wins could jolt the stock, but even with that in mind, I am not convinced the valuation makes sense. After all, even intismeran autogene isn't approved yet, and some other advanced pipeline candidates may not earn U.S. approval for another two years. Adding commercial ramp-up times, we are looking at revenue far into the future even for the company's most advanced programs, and that's assuming they are successful at all.

The uncertainty of these future revenues (and cash flows) makes for a somewhat risky outlook at current levels: Too much could go wrong and sink the stock. I'd like a much more reasonable valuation and a significant margin of error -- considering we are discussing a volatile biotech stock -- before investing. If Moderna's market cap falls below $50 billion in a market crash, I'd definitely consider buying because the business looks strong, the science is impeccable, and the company's future looks bright.

But valuation is a major sticking point. I am keeping Moderna on my watch list for now.
