UTSA 'manhandled' in road loss to FAU as American title hopes take a hit

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

BOCA RATON, Fla. - Leaving the field after a lopsided loss to Florida Atlantic, UTSA coach Jeff Traylor returned to a locker room that he described as disappointed, embarrassed and disgusted.

The Roadrunners entered the night on the fringe of the national top 25 with everything in front of them, remaining in strong position for an American Conference championship and a College Football Playoff berth for as long as they could keep their winning streak going.

Instead, UTSA was overpowered and outplayed on both sides of the ball, allowing the highest point total of Traylor's tenure in a 60-29 road rout Thursday.

"We didn't give our kids a great plan as a staff. They gave their guys a better plan than we did," Traylor said. "So, it starts with the staff, but up front, we got manhandled on both sides of the ball."

The loss drops the Roadrunners to 7-2 on the year with a 4-1 record in the American Conference, snapping a five-game winning streak. UTSA remains in contention for the league title, as North Texas is the lone unbeaten at 4-0, and Memphis is the only other one-loss team at 4-1.

But the path to a College Football Playoff spot as the highest-ranked team in the Group of Six became much more perilous. UTSA entered the night receiving votes in both the Associated Press and coaches polls, joining the conversation with Boise State and New Mexico as the best in the Group of Six. The Roadrunners will now need both of those teams and perhaps a few others to drop games down the stretch.

"It's a new challenge now," UTSA quarterback Owen McCown said. "We'll see how our team responds. I know they'll respond the right way."

UTSA's defense had been spotty in stretches all season, entering the night ranked just outside the top 100 nationally in total defense at 391.5 yards per game.

But the Roadrunners had usually managed to find success in one aspect or another, often taking away an opponent's biggest strength and forcing them to play out of rhythm. The Owls' up-tempo, air raid attack entered the night ranked in the top five in the country in passing and in the bottom 20 in rushing, but Traylor said he was "pretty shocked" to see how heavily the Owls committed to the run, as FAU exploded for 339 yards on the ground.

FAU averaged 6.9 yards per rush, led by running back Leonard Farrow with 12 carries for 126 yards and a score.

"We knew everything coming in," UTSA defensive lineman Jeremiah Bailey said. "They were more prepared, got the best of us tonight."

The Owls' passing attack was effective when needed, as well, racking up 224 yards as FAU became the first team to eclipse 200 yards both through the air and on the ground against UTSA this year.

Quarterback Caden Veltkamp hit 17 of 25 passes for 210 yards with four touchdowns, including three to star wideout Easton Messer.

"It always starts up front," Traylor said. "Their O-line manhandled us."

That physical disadvantage was evident on the other side, too, as Traylor said UTSA's offensive line "couldn't protect our quarterback."

McCown completed 20 of 35 passes for 265 yards with three touchdowns but was sacked six times as the Roadrunners faced consistent pressure.

"I have full trust in those guys up front on the O-line," McCown said. "I just don't think they played as good as they wanted to tonight, and that's all right. It's part of football. It's more important to see the response."

UTSA's rushing attack was also held to 136 yards at an average of 3.8 yards per carry, and FAU often pulled a safety up closer to the line of scrimmage to limit the Roadrunners' ground game.

That put more of a burden on UTSA's receivers to beat their defenders, and Traylor said the Roadrunners targets "weren't winning on press coverage," forcing McCown to hold onto the ball longer.

"We've got to get receivers open," Traylor said. "As a coaching staff, we've got to do a better job, and receivers have to get open and win some of those downfield throws. That's really it."

After having a few extra days to heal up through the open week, an array of Roadrunners went down to injuries on Thursday, and Traylor said UTSA "lost some great players tonight." The extent of most of the knocks remains unknown, Traylor said, but UTSA's ability to shuffle the depth chart where needed is "going to be the biggest deal" as the Roadrunners look to regroup.

North Texas will present a very similar challenge, entering the week ranked atop the FBS in total offense at 539.6 yards per game. A win vaults UTSA back into a first-place tie atop the American, and McCown expects the group to answer the challenge.

"I hope as a team this puts a little chip on our shoulder going into a really big game next week," McCown said. "We're still in this hunt. It's the top of the conference, but we're having to play a really good team next week in the Dome in North Texas. So, we have to respond the right way. I know we will."

Why Nier: Automata's Latest Update Increased Its File Size To 366GB

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Those with Nier: Automata installed on Steam were given a bit of a shock this week when they suddenly realized that either a significant chunk of their free storage was suddenly occupied, or nothing new would install because there was no room. Turns out a bug tied to the latest Nier update was to blame.

Nier: Automata is a relatively modest (for modern-day games) 50GB on PC. However, some who installed its latest update noticed that file size had inexplicably ballooned to a whopping 366GB on Thursday. No indication of why in the patch notes, with the update a fairly routine one.

Even if it wasn’t, and there was planned DLC for Nier, it would have to be quite something to make the game more than seven times bigger than it was this time two days ago. Unfortunately, there is no secret expansion. I say unfortunately, I’m not sure how many people would welcome more Nier DLC if the trade-off was having to find a spare 366GB of space in which to keep it.

No, this is a bug, and a pretty significant one considering just how much room the game now requires. Turns out that the update has accidentally added the base game’s files to all of Nier’s DLCs. That means the entire game is in every single DLC file; hence the file size now being so big, as the update means you are downloading the base game multiple times.

The issue doesn’t appear to have been fixed yet either. Shout out to GamesRadar+, which tried to install Nier, only to be told the game now requires 366GB of storage. However, the storage requirements listed on Steam still claim the game is the original 50GB. Understandable, as this is a mistake and not an intentional ploy by Square Enix to make people delete every other game in their Steam library until only Nier remains.

If Nier’s file size were to balloon up to a massive 366GB for some reason, it would make it one of the beefiest games of all time. Call of Duty is infamously one of the bigger games around today, although that’s largely due to its games being part of a bigger launcher that will install everything if you let it. Players can now cherry-pick what they do and do not want to install to make sure the latest CoD doesn’t take up too much room.

It would even be significantly larger than GTA 6, the file size of which was officially revealed earlier this week. Almost certainly the most ambitious game ever made in terms of visuals, gameplay, and just sheer size, GTA 6 will be 212GB. That currently has nothing on Nier, although I imagine the 2B title will shrink back down to 50GB fairly soon.

The new product will appear on the Airbus A350-900ULR and Boeing 777-9, bringing first class to the airline’s ultra-long-range A350 fleet for the first time.

Singapore Airlines’ new first class specifications reveal a small cabin with substantial personal space. The A350-900ULR will have four seats, while the Boeing 777-9 will have six. Both use a 1-2-1 arrangement with direct aisle access and a curved cabin layout.

PriestmanGoode designed the product, with Collins Aerospace manufacturing the seats. The interiors feature Poltrona Frau leather and padded walls incorporating Singapore Airlines’ batik motif.

The centerpiece is the pair of center seats. A fully retractable divider allows passengers to share the space, with the seats moving together to create either a two-person sofa or a double bed. That bed measures 80 inches long and 60 inches wide. Singapore Airlines describes it as the largest double bed in commercial aviation.

Solo passengers receive an 80-inch-long, 30-inch-wide bed. The seat itself measures 23 inches across with the armrests raised, expanding to 30 inches when they are lowered.

Every seat also supports face-to-face dining with a companion, a feature I appreciate in first class: traveling together should include the option to sit across from one another for dinner, even when you prefer separate window seats.

The seat includes electronically adjustable side wings, upper and lower lumbar support, adjustable armrests, a leg rest, and an ottoman. Integrated myKrisWorld controls can save preferred seat positions and lighting settings for future flights.

Both aircraft receive a 32-inch 4K entertainment screen, but the A350ULR version is OLED. The 777-9 specification lists a conventional 4K display.

Passengers will be offered Bang & Olufsen wireless headphones or can pair Bluetooth headphones with the system. A separate touch panel displays flight information, connectivity status, and lavatory availability without interrupting the main screen.

The new cabins are scheduled to begin entering service in the second half of 2027. Unlike with ANA, the unveiling does not signal an imminent onboard debut. Meteorites that may have delivered carbon to early Earth carry an oxygen signature older than their asteroids

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Organic specks in primitive meteorites carry an oxygen signature set before their asteroids formed, likely in the cold outer solar system.

Some of the carbon on the early Earth may have arrived inside meteorites, carried as dark specks of organic matter far too small to see.

Where those specks were first made is still an open question. A new study suggests their oxygen dates from before the asteroids that carried them had formed.

In seven primitive meteorites that were never strongly heated, the organic matter carries nearly the same oxygen fingerprint.

Water on the asteroids barely changed it. That makes the oxygen a record of where the material formed, not of what happened to it later.

Where the organics formed is the team’s interpretation, drawn from a small set of meteorites and two lab tests.

Daniel Crocker and David Johnston of Harvard’s Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences led the work. Their co-authors were Conel Alexander of the Carnegie Institution for Science and Mark Thiemens of the University of California, San Diego.

Oxygen atoms come in three stable forms: oxygen-16, oxygen-17 and oxygen-18. They differ only in weight. Scientists call these forms isotopes, and the mix of the three in a sample works like a fingerprint.

On Earth, almost any process that sorts oxygen by weight changes the share of oxygen-17 about half as much as the share of oxygen-18.

The oldest minerals in some carbon-rich meteorites break that rule. In them, the two heavier forms change by equal amounts.

That odd pattern is usually explained as a blend of two ancient pools of oxygen, one rich in oxygen-16 and one poor in it. Researchers drew a reference line for it in 1977 from minerals in the Allende meteorite, but what produced it is still debated.

The meteorites in the study belong to a group called carbonaceous chondrites. The most primitive ones hold up to 4 percent carbon by weight in their organic matter. Between 30 and 80 percent of that organic matter won’t dissolve in acids or solvents.

Alexander’s lab separated that material from the rock. Crocker then measured its oxygen with a method the Harvard group had recently developed.

Water had altered the seven primitive meteorites on their home asteroids, always below about 302°F (150°C). Their organic matter came out almost identical.

The oxygen-18 readings varied by only about 2 parts per thousand, and every sample was within the margin of error of the reference line.

“What surprised me was how similar the oxygen isotope compositions were across meteorites that experienced very different alteration histories after they formed,” Crocker told Earth.com.

These asteroids once held liquid water, and water can swap oxygen atoms with the material around it.

The team calculated what would happen if the organic matter had traded much of its oxygen with that water. Its oxygen-18 reading would be 27 to 58 parts per thousand above a standard.

The actual readings were 10 to 12. So the organic matter most likely swapped very little oxygen with the water. If it had, samples from several separate asteroids would have needed nearly identical conditions to end up with the same values.

The water still changed the material’s chemistry, and some oxygen-bearing parts of the molecules were lost. But it left the oxygen’s mix of weights mostly alone.

“That preservation gives us an opportunity to look past what happened to the organic matter on the asteroids and investigate the environment in which it originally formed,” Crocker said.

Organic matter from six meteorites heated on their asteroids, or by impacts, looked different. It held less of both heavy oxygen forms, and the readings formed a trend away from the unheated group.

To test whether heat alone could do that, the team warmed organic matter from the Mighei meteorite in a vacuum for an hour. One run reached 932°F (500°C) and the other 1,112°F (600°C).

The samples lost about 59 and 67 percent of their oxygen respectively, and what remained shifted along the same trend as the heated meteorites. Undo the heating, and they would move back toward the primitive group, which fits one shared starting fingerprint.

Water didn’t set the fingerprint, and heat changes it in a different direction. So the authors concluded the oxygen most likely went in when the organic matter itself formed, before it was gathered into any asteroid.

The organic matter holds less oxygen-16 than almost every mineral in these meteorites. In the early solar system, water was the main ingredient low in oxygen-16. So the authors think the organics took their oxygen from ancient water as they formed.

Extra heavy hydrogen and nitrogen in the organics already hinted at a cold place with strong radiation. The team favors a birthplace in the outer solar system, with the same well-mixed supply ending up in several asteroids.

“Rather than starting with the chemistry that occurred on the early Earth, we can ask what organic material Earth and other young planets inherited from the Solar System environment in which they formed,” Crocker and Johnston told Earth.com.

The researchers said they “cannot yet identify a single location or oxygen-bearing molecule as its source.” They also checked for lab effects, such as stray mineral grains and the chemicals used to free the organics. They concluded neither changed the readings in any meaningful way.

The unusual pattern in the ancient minerals, first spotted more than 50 years ago, still lacks a full explanation. “That doesn’t solve the mystery, but it provides a new constraint,” the two researchers added.

Next, the team sees the oxygen as a way to narrow down where, and under what conditions, the material formed. That could show how widely the raw ingredients for life’s chemistry were spread among the young planets.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
