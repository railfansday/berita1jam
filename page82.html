Jake Ferguson, Tyler Goodson will miss multiple weeks for Cowboys

The Cowboys will play "multiple weeks" without starting tight end Jake Ferguson and backup running back Tyler Goodson, coach Brian Schottenheimer said Monday.

Ferguson sprained the posterior cruciate and the medial collateral ligaments in his left knee in the third quarter Sunday, and Goodson injured his knee in the second quarter but played through it into the second half.

Goodson had a career-high 77 yards on 12 carries against the Colts.

"A lot of these guys, they play on adrenaline, and this is a guy that has been incredible for us really the last two weeks," Schottenheimer said, via Todd Archer of ESPN. "And these guys, they want to tough it out. They care. They want to win. They want to do whatever it takes. And just doing some more exams and scans and things like that, a few things showed up that we'll keep an eye [out] for."

Ferguson is second on the team with four touchdown catches.

The Cowboys have only one slot remaining on the injured reserve designated-to-return list, having already designated seven players.

"We're working through it," Schottenheimer said.

Tight end Luke Schoonmaker has missed the past two games with a groin injury but will return this week, Schottenheimer said.

Fullback Hunter Luepke is the only player behind starting running back Javonte Williams on the active roster. Israel Abanikanda and Jashaun Corbin are on the practice squad.

"There's a lot of different ways to do it," Schottenheimer said of the running back situation. "I have a lot of confidence in Izzy. We'll be looking at a lot of different potential moves that we can make, but I'm not going to pretend that losing Goody doesn't affect us. The guy's been playing great football."

BBC’s Near-Perfect Detective Thriller Teases Major Changes for American Remake

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Greer Riddell is a London-based film and television journalist writing about comedy, streaming and the evolving media industry in the UK and USA. She works at Collider, covering the latest in film, television and entertainment through interviews, features, reviews and industry coverage.

Her recent brand collaborations include HBO, Warner Bros., Netflix, Hulu, Sky, Disney+ and DC Studios. She has covered titles including Saturday Night Live UK, Rivals, Scrubs, Stuart Fails to Save the Universe, Ludwig, Supergirl and Stranger Things.

Her recent interviews include Florence Pugh, Mike Faist and Christopher Abbott for Netflix's East of Eden, Taika Waititi and Jenna Ortega for Klara and the Sun.

David Mitchell has made an unlikely detective out of a socially awkward, semi-reclusive puzzle setter in Ludwig, the BBC's hit murder mystery from Mark Brotherhood. With its combination of soft murder, a postcard-perfect British city and Mitchell in a role he seems born to play, the series has become one of the UK's most recent successes. Now, it's getting an American counterpart.

Kenton Allen, executive producer of Ludwig and CEO of Big Talk Productions, the company behind the series, spoke to Collider ahead of the Season 2 finale arriving on BritBox. He gave an update on what murder mystery fans can expect from Rabbit, the U.S. adaptation being developed for ABC by writers Humphrey Ker and Megan Ganz. But with the original series already finding an audience across North America through BritBox, why make an American version at all? And how much of Ludwig will make the journey across the Atlantic?

John Taylor lives a quiet, highly ordered life in his mother's house, rarely venturing beyond his familiar routines. He prefers the company of his books and puzzles, publishing his work under the professional pseudonym Ludwig. His identical twin brother, James, on the other hand, is a DCI in the Cambridge police force, married to Lucy (Anna Maxwell Martin), with whom he has a son, Henry. When James mysteriously disappears following his involvement in a murder investigation, Lucy turns to John for help finding him. Reluctant to get involved, John is eventually persuaded to take his brother's place at the police station, impersonating a detective while trying to establish what happened to James. However, his extraordinary ability to recognize patterns and solve complex puzzles proves surprisingly useful when it comes to solving murders, and he soon discovers that he's rather good at a job he has no qualifications for.

Britain makes more detective television than any country has any business making, and somehow almost all of it is good. Here is the one you belong in, what it says about you, and the case for it being the best of the four.

You want the tea, the gossip and the vicar behaving oddly. Midsomer is a county where the scenery is beautiful, the fete is on Saturday and the murder rate is roughly that of a small war, and absolutely nobody involved thinks this is strange.

You feel other people's grief as if it were handed to you personally. Broadchurch is less a whodunnit than a study of what a small town does to itself when it finds out that the answer was always going to be somebody everybody already knew.

You go through things rather than around them. John Luther is the best detective in London and a liability to everybody who works with him, and the show is completely honest that those two facts are the same fact.

You say the thing and you do not dress it up. Vera Stanhope turns up in a hat and a mac, is rude to almost everybody, and is the only person in the room prepared to ask the question that the family has been waiting years for somebody to ask.

The first season sees John navigate the increasingly complicated business of maintaining his brother's identity while investigating the circumstances surrounding his disappearance. By the Season 1 finale, the need for the impersonation has come to an end, but John's involvement with the police force doesn't. In Season 2, he continues helping the Cambridge detectives as a crime consultant, using his talents to solve a new murder each week while the wider mystery of James's whereabouts remains unresolved.

Alongside Mitchell and Maxwell Martin, Ludwig brings together familiar faces from British television for its weekly murder mysteries. Season 1 featured Sir Derek Jacobi as teacher Mr. Todd and Felicity Kendal as Lady Camilla Bright, while Season 2 welcomed Stephen Merchant as snooker player Danny Sloane in Episode 4. Rebecca Front and Doon Mackichan also appear as sisters whose mother is murdered at the theater. These guest appearances have made Ludwig a vehicle for seeing nostalgic names from British comedies in a completely different genre.

The show's success has extended well beyond its initial premise. According to the BBC, Ludwig Season 2 became 2026's biggest scripted launch across broadcasters and streamers to date, attracting 9.1 million viewers over 28 days. It also performed strongly on BBC iPlayer, where the opening episode attracted 5.5 million viewers, making it the second-biggest audience for the opening episode of a BBC comedy this year. The series has already been renewed for a third season, alongside a Christmas special. With those numbers, Ludwig has firmly established itself as a successful British detective series. So why does it need an American makeover?

The answer begins with understanding what makes Ludwig so appealing to international audiences, and why Allen believes some elements of the original are particularly well suited to export. Murder mysteries are hardly new for American television, with shows like Columbo and Monk helping establish the murder-of-the-week format long before John Taylor arrived in Cambridge. "There are some examples in the States now of this, and our inspiration for Ludwig," explains Allen.

However, he also believes the British setting is a significant part of what makes the series so distinctive. The production deliberately chose Cambridge as its backdrop and that sense of place is part of what Allen describes as "exportable UK" appeal. British murder mysteries have a long history of finding international audiences, and the combination of historic locations, familiar genre conventions and distinctive British talent gives them a particular appeal overseas. As Allen puts it, "Brilliant actors, brilliant writers, full stop. Our on-camera and off-camera talent is world-class." While the British setting and production talent might be part of the attraction, neither is necessarily something that can easily be recreated in an American adaptation.

Season 4 premiered in the U.K. last month.

Mitchell's portrayal of John Taylor is also central to the show's identity, and rather than attempting to find an American equivalent of Ludwig, the creative team wants to build something with its own personality. "Rabbit will be a brilliant cousin to Ludwig," Allen says. He believes the differences between the two countries will provide enough material to make the new series a distinct proposition, pointing to "where it's set and what the attitude is towards crime and crime prevention in North America" as important differences. "Our police don't carry guns," he jokes. "There's lots of cultural differences that I think will make an interesting companion piece to Ludwig."

Allen's thinking is that Rabbit should offer something familiar to fans of the original without becoming a direct copy. He points to the American adaptation of The Office as an example of how a British television concept can find an entirely new identity when moved across the Atlantic. Originally created by Ricky Gervais and Stephen Merchant, the BBC sitcom followed David Brent (Gervais) and the employees of the Wernham Hogg paper company in Slough, Buckinghamshire. When the concept was adapted for American television, Steve Carell took over as Michael Scott, managing the Dunder Mifflin paper company in Scranton, Pennsylvania. The very early episodes even borrowed directly from the British series, including the famous scene of Jim (John Krasinski) putting Dwight's (Rainn Wilson) stapler in jello. However, the American version quickly developed beyond those initial similarities. While the original British The Office consisted of 12 regular episodes and two Christmas specials, the American series ran for nine seasons and 201 episodes, eventually becoming a television institution in its own right.

Allen sees a similar opportunity with Rabbit. Rather than just reproducing the British series for a different audience, the intention is to take its central premise and explore what it could become in a different cultural setting. "I think that's what we're hoping to do with Rabbit, which is why it's called Rabbit and not American Ludwig," he explains. The important thing for Allen is that the new series has a reason to exist beyond the success of its predecessor. "It's seeing the fundamental setup through a different lens," he explains. "Nobody wants to make a facsimile of a show that we're making and is on air. So it has to have some reason to exist."

A large part of making that possible comes down to the writers involved. Rabbit is being developed by Ganz and Ker, two comedy writers with considerable experience across British and American television. Ganz previously worked on Community and Modern Family, earning an Emmy for her work, before becoming an executive producer on It's Always Sunny in Philadelphia and co-creating Apple TV+'s Mythic Quest alongside Rob McElhenney. Ker also has a connection to Mythic Quest, appearing in the series himself, and has worked closely with McElhenney through their shared involvement in football. Ker is the executive director of Wrexham AFC, the Welsh football club purchased by McElhenney and Ryan Reynolds. He subsequently became a familiar face to audiences through FX's Welcome to Wrexham and is also part of the writing team behind Saturday Night Live UK. The duo are also husband and wife.

Allen is particularly enthusiastic about what Ganz has brought to the project, praising her approach to making Rabbit feel connected to Ludwig without simply repeating it. He says that Ganz has found "a very cute, smart way of making a cousin rather than a copy." That distinction will be important for the adaptation. Ludwig already has an established audience, both in the UK and internationally, and its success on BritBox means American viewers can continue watching Mitchell's original portrayal of John Taylor. Rabbit doesn't need to replace that version or convince audiences that they should choose one over the other. Instead, the hope is that viewers who enjoy one will have another detective series to turn to, with its own characters, setting, and approach to crime.

Ludwig Seasons 1 and 2 are both streaming now on BritBox. ​​​​

Colin Farrell's Remake Of A '90s Sci-Fi Movie Is One Of Prime Video's Biggest Streaming Hits

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

"Total Recall" (2012) is directed by Len Wiseman, who most recently oversaw the 2025 "John Wick" offshoot "Ballerina" and previously helmed the "Underworld" films between 2003 and 2016. He is, unfortunately, also responsible for 2007's "Live Free or Die Hard," which, despite making the third spot on /Film's "Die Hard" movie ranking, we simply could have done without.

Unlike the 1990 original, "Total Recall" is set entirely on an Earth ravaged by chemical war, leaving only the United Federation of Britain (UFB) and the Colony (Australia) habitable. Whereas Arnold Schwarzenegger's Douglas Quaid had dreams of Mars, which turn out to be long-buried memories, Colin Farrell's counterpart finds himself experiencing nocturnal visions of being a secret agent. Bored of his factory job on The Colony, he enlists the company Rekall to implant artificial memories to match his dreams, which he soon learns are, in fact, memories of having worked as a UFB agent. Hunted by his former employers, he teams up with Jessica Biel's Melina, a member of a rebel group known as The Resistance. But as the pair try to outrun the UFB and their Chancellor Vilos Cohaagen (Bryan Cranston), the line between fantasy and reality becomes increasingly blurred.

Ahead of the film's debut, /Film asked: "Is 'Total Recall' the least essential film of the summer?" The answer, according to most critics, was yes. But it seems the Prime Video crowds are willing to give it another shot. Per streaming viewership tracker FlixPatrol, "Total Recall" is the No. 3 most-watched movie on Prime Video in the United States as of November 9, 2026. What's more, based on the film's trajectory thus far, it could be set for a chart-topping run. "Total Recall" debuted on the charts at No. 9 on November 7, 2026, before jumping to fourth the following day and hitting No. 3 the day after. By the end of the week, Farrell's forgotten remake might just hit No. 1 — not bad for a film with a 30% critic score on Rotten Tomatoes.

Both Philip K. Dick's story and the 1990 "Total Recall" film feature Mars. But the 2012 remake actually has more in common with Paul Verhoeven's rendition than the novel, using characters that only appear in the movie and adhering more closely to the big-screen storyline. That might have seemed like a reasonable approach, given how the first film was so successful. Unfortunately, Len Wiseman's effort didn't emulate the offbeat comedy of the original, which was so sharply rendered by Arnold Schwarzenegger and gave the film such a unique tone. Despite featuring some impressive action scenes, Wiseman's version also managed to feel like a generic sci-fi actioner, all of which combined to yield a less-than-impressive critical response.

That said, Roger Ebert was impressed enough to bestow three stars on the film — just half a star less than the 1990 version — and called it a "well-crafted, high-energy sci-fi." Still, even he had to admit that the film was one of several sci-fi movie remakes that should have never happened, ending his review by writing, "It never touched me emotionally, though, the way the 1990 film did, and strictly speaking, isn't necessary." Others were much more condemnatory, with David Sexton of the London Evening Standard giving the film one star and writing, "We used to do the future so much better. Let's not forget that. Let's forget this instead."

Normally, that should deter you from joining the "Total Recall" renaissance on Prime Video. But considering that, since the movie's debut, we've been subjected to an onslaught of streaming movie shabbiness, it might be worth a go to see if the remake really is bad or whether that's merely a memory implanted by the critics.

Alzheimer’s may have early warning signs outside the brain

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Alzheimer's disease is best known for its effects on memory and thinking, but new research suggests that some symptoms may develop in a surprising place: outside the brain.

Scientists at the University of Central Florida (UCF) have discovered that genetic mutations associated with Alzheimer's can interfere with the nerves that control muscle movement, even without involvement from the brain or spinal cord. The findings could help explain why some people with Alzheimer's experience changes in walking, balance, or coordination long before noticeable memory problems emerge.

The discovery raises the possibility that movement difficulties could provide earlier warning signs of the disease and that treatments focused exclusively on the brain may overlook important problems elsewhere in the body.

The research was led by James Hickman, a professor at UCF's Nanoscience Technology Center, and Research Professor Xiufang "Nadine" Guo, working alongside scientists at the healthcare technology company Hesperos. Funded by the National Institutes of Health's National Institute on Aging, the study used laboratory systems built from human cells to investigate how inherited Alzheimer's mutations affect movement. The findings were published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association.

"Motor deficits may be an earlier indication [of Alzheimer's]," Guo says. "If we can detect those changes and intervene earlier, that could help delay the onset of central nervous system symptoms."

Why Alzheimer's Symptoms May Appear Before Memory Loss

Alzheimer's is generally associated with progressive memory loss, confusion, and declining cognitive abilities. However, doctors have also observed that some patients develop physical symptoms, including changes in balance, coordination, and gait (manner of walking), years before clear cognitive difficulties become apparent.

These observations have raised an important question: Are such movement problems simply a consequence of damage occurring in the brain, or could Alzheimer's also interfere directly with nerves elsewhere in the body?

To investigate, the UCF team focused on familial Alzheimer's, a rare inherited form of the disease that typically develops earlier in life (from 40 to 65 years of age) than the more common form.

Their experiments revealed that motor neurons carrying mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's could disrupt the connections responsible for controlling muscles, even when the brain and spinal cord were completely absent from the experimental system.

Motor neurons are specialized nerve cells that transmit signals to muscles, telling them when to contract and produce movement. The new findings suggest that Alzheimer's mutations can interfere with this process directly, rather than affecting movement only through damage to the brain.

"This is the first time it's been demonstrated that deficits in the peripheral nervous system can arise directly from these mutations," Hickman says. "It means drugs that target the brain may not fix problems in the rest of the body."

The peripheral nervous system includes the nerves located outside the brain and spinal cord. These nerves carry signals between the central nervous system and the rest of the body, helping control movement, sensation, and other essential functions.

Guo also points out that preserving the ability to move could have benefits beyond physical health. Regular physical activity is associated with better cognitive well-being, so protecting motor function may also help support brain health.

How a Miniature Human Cell System Revealed the Discovery

To examine the relationship between Alzheimer's mutations and muscle movement, the scientists used an innovative laboratory platform known as "human-on-a-chip" technology.

Developed and manufactured through Hesperos, a company co-founded by Hickman, these miniature systems bring different types of human cells together under controlled conditions. They allow researchers to recreate specific biological functions and observe how diseases interfere with them.

Unlike conventional laboratory experiments that examine isolated cells, these systems can reproduce important interactions between different tissues. They also provide an alternative to animal models, which do not always accurately reflect how human diseases behave.

For this study, the team created a miniature model of the neuromuscular junction, the connection where a motor neuron communicates with a muscle cell.

The model was deliberately designed without a brain or spinal cord. This was a crucial part of the experiment because it allowed the researchers to investigate whether Alzheimer's-related changes could cause movement problems independently of the central nervous system.

The scientists combined healthy muscle cells with motor neurons derived from stem cells that carried genetic mutations associated with familial Alzheimer's disease. Stem cells are cells that can develop into specialized types of cells, making them valuable tools for studying human diseases in the laboratory.

The researchers then examined how effectively the altered nerve cells communicated with otherwise healthy muscle tissue.

The results showed that the Alzheimer's-associated mutations could interfere with normal nerve-to-muscle function. This suggests that at least some movement difficulties linked to the disease may arise directly in the peripheral nervous system rather than being caused entirely by degeneration in the brain.

Why Damaged Nerve Connections Can Affect Walking and Balance

Every voluntary movement depends on successful communication between the nervous system and the muscles.

At the neuromuscular junction, an electrical signal traveling along a motor neuron triggers chemical communication that causes a muscle to contract. This process makes everyday activities possible, from walking and maintaining posture to lifting objects and performing precise movements.

When the connection becomes impaired, muscles may respond less effectively to nerve signals. Depending on the severity and location of the problem, this can contribute to weakness, reduced coordination, and difficulty sustaining physical activity.

To evaluate these effects, the research team measured how consistently nerve signals produced muscle contractions and how long the muscles could continue contracting before becoming fatigued.

These measurements provided a direct assessment of how well the nerve and muscle cells worked together. They also resemble aspects of the functional testing physicians use when examining patients with movement disorders.

"You can't move unless the motor circuit works," Hickman says. "When a doctor taps your knee to check your reflex, they're testing that exact connection."

By demonstrating impaired nerve-to-muscle communication in a system containing no brain or spinal cord, the study provides evidence that mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's can produce effects beyond the central nervous system.

However, the experiments do not establish that these changes necessarily occur before memory loss in patients. Further research will be needed to determine how closely the laboratory findings reflect the progression of Alzheimer's in people, particularly those with the more common forms of the disease.

Could This Discovery Lead to Earlier Alzheimer's Detection?

The findings suggest a potentially important new direction for Alzheimer's research: looking beyond memory and other cognitive symptoms when investigating the disease.

If future studies confirm that changes in motor function are detectable early in certain patients, tests of walking, coordination, or nerve-to-muscle activity might eventually help identify disease-related changes before more obvious neurological symptoms develop.

The results also raise questions about treatment. Many Alzheimer's therapies are designed to address processes occurring in the brain, but medications that target the central nervous system may not necessarily correct abnormalities affecting peripheral nerves and muscles.

Understanding these separate effects could help researchers investigate treatments that protect physical function alongside cognitive health.

Human-on-a-Chip Technology Could Transform Disease Research

Beyond the implications for Alzheimer's, the researchers see considerable potential in using human-on-a-chip systems to investigate other diseases and evaluate possible treatments.

Because these platforms contain human cells and reproduce measurable biological activities, they can help scientists observe disease-related changes in conditions that more closely resemble human physiology than some conventional experimental models.

This approach may be particularly useful in drug development, where researchers need to understand not only how a potential treatment affects individual cells but also how it influences interactions between different tissues.

Such systems can also uncover biological effects that may be difficult to detect in animal experiments, potentially helping researchers identify promising therapies and recognize limitations earlier in development.

For Hickman, the progress represents the culmination of three decades of work devoted to understanding disease through more realistic laboratory models.

"These systems let us study disease in a way that's closer to what actually happens in the human body, and that's what we need to develop better treatments," he says.

Destination Spotlight: Crystal Mountain

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Skiing at Crystal Mountain, Michigan. (Photo: Crystal Mountain)

As a proud Michigander, I spent many childhood summers “Up North,” a region of the state with beautiful lakes, cozy cabins, extensive hiking and biking trails, and every other manner of outdoor summer fun. But “Up North” isn’t strictly a summer destination; it’s a four-season adventure hub that offers everything from spectacular fall leaf-peeping to world-class winter skiing.

Among the best of these Michigan winter playgrounds is Crystal Mountain. Just a short drive from Traverse City and a stone’s throw from Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore, Crystal Mountain got its start 70 years ago with a single rope tow, an old car engine, and a dream. From these humble beginnings, it has blossomed into one of the best and most visited ski hills in the state, with 59 downhill runs, six chairlifts, two magic carpets, and more than 100 acres of skiable terrain.

The mission at the heart of the operation has always remained the same: to be a place where families come together to have fun, make memories, and experience all the best the outdoors have to offer. This philosophy has immortalized Crystal Mountain in the hearts of midwesterners like me, encouraging generation after generation of skiers to fall in love with the sport and the outdoors. It’s where I learned to ski—and where you can still take lessons from PSIA/AASI-trained instructors.

Beyond the ski hill, there’s no shortage of things to do at Crystal. Glide through this winter wonderland on cross-country skis—the resort’s 25 kilometers of trails were named best in the state by Midwest Living Magazine. Or let a team of Belgian horses do the gliding for you as you enjoy the views of the forests and hills from a horse-drawn carriage. Lace up your ice skates in the heart of the resort’s village at Barr Park, or explore the Michigan Legacy Art Park, a 30-acre reserve located on the grounds of Crystal Mountain where you can snowshoe, hike, and take in the sculptures and scenery.

When you’ve had enough of the cold, unwind at the outdoor hot tub, the indoor pool at the Peak Fitness Center, or the award-winning Crystal Spa. Consistently recognized as one of the best in the country, Crystal Spa features infrared saunas, eucalyptus steam rooms, and a relaxation lounge with a cozy fireplace, making it the perfect oasis after a day spent out in the winter weather.

There are endless ways to enjoy Crystal Mountain, but the true magic of this place can’t be credited to any one thing—it’s the feeling you get when you’re here. It’s the memories you’ll cherish for a lifetime.

Established in 1956, Crystal Mountain is a family-owned, four-season resort located 28 miles southwest of Traverse City, Michigan, and a short drive from Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore. The 1,500-acre resort features lodging, dining, downhill and cross-country skiing, two championship golf courses, shopping, the award-winning Crystal Spa, Michigan’s only alpine slide, Peak Fitness, the Park at Water’s Edge, Michigan Legacy Art Park, weddings, a conference center, and real estate options.

Roger Ebert Called Disney's Attempt To Update A Classic Cartoon Character 'Transcendently Bad'

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Kids of today likely won't have heard of the visually-impaired cartoon millionaire known as Mr. Magoo. But for a large part of the 20th Century he was a pop culture fixture who entertained generations of kids with his slapstick shenanigans. By 1997, however, his appeal had waned, which makes Disney's decision to reboot the IP with a Leslie Nielsen-led live-action film all the more confounding. Thankfully, Roger Ebert was on-hand to let the studio have it — which he dutifully did with a scathing review that, bizarrely enough, actually awarded the film half a star.

The critic handed out some damning reviews in his time. Ebert hated Leonard Nimoy's box office flop "Funny About Love" and even walked out of the Oscar-winning war movie "Mediterraneo." All of which left filmmakers terrified of being handed the dreaded "thumbs down" for their efforts. Around 60 movies earned this mark of shame during the critic's career but somehow, "Mr. Magoo" managed to eke out half a star. If you read Ebert's thoughts on the film you'll no doubt wonder how it managed such a feat.

In his review, Ebert revealed that the only thing he liked about the film was the titular character's red Studebaker — that was it. Otherwise, he considered "Mr. Magoo" to be so bad it "soars above ordinary badness as the eagle outreaches the fly." How, then, did this ill-advised comedy reboot manage to get a half-star out of the old boy? Did that Studebaker really save "Mr. Magoo" from the thumbs down club? It's hard to imagine given just how much Ebert hated this movie.

There are plenty of movies Roger Ebert hated that are worth watching, but "Mr. Magoo" isn't one of them. The character first appeared in the 1949 theatrical short "The Ragtime Bear," where he was voiced by none other than future "Gilligan's Island" star and legendary character actor Jim Backus. Backus voiced the United Productions of America (UPA) character until 1977, portraying him in theatrical cartoons, TV series, and TV specials. While Mr. Magoo's character developed over the course of his existence, he's best known as a lovably oblivious old millionaire, whose extreme nearsightedness sees him wander into dangerous situations only to miraculously escape unharmed — causing significant distress to his nephew and frequent sidekick Waldo.

While Backus stopped voicing the character after the 1977 TV series "What's New, Mr. Magoo?" the visually-impaired millionaire endured. The most recent incarnation of the character appeared in the "Mr. Magoo" cartoon, which wrapped up in 2023. Prior to that, however, Disney decided that kids of the mid-'90s needed to be reintroduced to Quincy Magoo, and hired Hong Kong filmmaker Stanley Tong to make it happen.

In what turned out to be Tong's one and only English-language film, the frequent Jackie Chan collaborator teamed up not with a martial arts legend but with Leslie Nielsen. The "Naked Gun" star played the eponymous millionaire in a film that saw him hunted by the FBI and criminals eager to recover a priceless gem to which Mr. Magoo is entirely oblivious. Much of the movie follows the tried-and-true formula of its title character getting himself into absurd situations and coming out unscathed, such as when he commandeers an ironing board to ski after thieves. The movie made $28.9 million on a $30 million budget and upset Roger Ebert considerably.

In his half-star review of "Mr. Magoo," Roger Ebert described the film as being "transcendently bad," and accused it of being entirely without laughs. The critic admitted to never having been a fan of the original cartoons, which he recalled as essentially telling the same joke over and over. Likewise, the film was, in Ebert's estimation, "a one-joke movie without the joke."

Interestingly enough, this might be the only time he ever took aim at the bloopers, which play during the film's credits and evidently failed to amuse the critic. "Even the outtakes at the end aren't funny," he wrote, "and I'm not sure I understood one of them, unless it was meant to show stunt people hilariously almost being drowned." Ebert wasn't even impressed with Leslie Nielsen, who'd long since established himself as a comedic force but was, according to the reviewer, "unable to overcome the fact that a little Magoo at six minutes in a cartoon is a far different matter than a lot of Magoo at 90 minutes in a feature."

On "Siskel & Ebert," neither of the hosts could bring themselves to say anything nice about the movie. Gene Siskel called it "uninspired" before Ebert took the opportunity to once again lay into the movie, calling it a "wet blanket." Roger Ebert was wrong about a fair few movies in his time, but a 10% critic score on Rotten Tomatoes suggests he was right on the money with this one. And yet, he gave the movie half a star. Why? Did the Studebaker really save it from the shame of a thumbs down rating?

Indiana Jones Star John Rhys-Davies Slams The Dial Of Destiny

James Mangold's 2023 adventure film "Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny" was met with a lot of ambivalence back when it was first released, and star John Rhys-Davies, who has played the role of Sallah in two of the previous movies, wasn't fond of the way it turned out either. In a video interview posted on the Off/Frame Instagram account, Rhys-Davies admitted to being very disappointed in the film, largely because it focused too much on all-new characters, rather than on Indy (Harrison Ford), and himself:

"I'll be honest with you, I was very disappointed in it. The film is meant to be a nostalgia film. They filled the cast up with people we had never met before, so there can be no nostalgia, really. I found the scenes that I'd been promised in the thing were cut immediately after I signed the contract. I think it's a disappointing film."

"Dial of Destiny," despite catching up with Indiana Jones in the year 1969, and featuring Sallah in a small role, was more focused on the adventures of the irascible Helena Shaw (Phoebe Waller-Bridge), Indy's adult goddaughter and something of a con woman who sells artifacts on the black market. Helena is a great character, I'd say, but Rhys-Davies has a point in that the movie was a little too focused on her, and not on the nostalgic characters the audience paid money to see.

And as an actor, of course, he has every right to be upset about his part being reduced after he signed his contract to appear in the movie. Rhys-Davies doesn't go into detail about what larger role Sallah was to play in "Dial of Destiny," but in the finished film he certainly doesn't play a major role at all.

John Rhys-Davies also noted that Antonio Banderas appears in "Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny," and that he wasn't utilized correctly either. Banderas plays a frogman named Renaldo whose screentime is very brief indeed. Regarding Banderas, Rhys-Davies said:

"And what is tragic, really, is ... I mean, there are a number of tragic things. I mean, Banderas, the most exciting actor of his generation. [...] I think he's a wonderful actor. But, really, what a terrible thing to do to him.

Some might defend the tone and the visuals of "Dial of Destiny," which are largely accurate to Steven Spielberg's Indy movies, but no one could argue that Antonio Banderas or John Rhys-Davies were used well. Perhaps the filmmakers didn't want to write a 2023 adventure story with two actors — Ford and Rhys-Davies — who were born in the 1940s.

It certainly could have been cheaper. "Dial of Destiny" was, according to some reports, a $419 million movie, making it possibly one of the most expensive movies of all time. This means that its worldwide box office total of just under $384 million didn't come close to turning a profit. /Film even saw the writing on the wall a week before the movie's release: "Dial of Destiny" wasn't going to make nearly enough.

Now, had the filmmakers scaled back on the film's action mayhem and caught up with an elderly Indiana Jones and Sallah who spent their time in libraries and slowly trekking into quiet caves, maybe you could have had something. Perhaps John Rhys-Davies could have played a much more central role. But then, an action-light Indiana Jones movie might be anathema to the nature of the character.

It has only been a few years since the release of "Indiana Jones and the Dial of Destiny," and the film doesn't seem to have undergone any kind of major relitigation in that time. The weary ambivalence towards the legacy sequel still seems to be firmly in place.

Reviews of "Dial of Destiny" were generally good, but no critic seemed to be all that overwhelmed. /Film's review, by Lex Briscuso, was positive, giving the film an eight out of 10, and said that "the film has a fun plot that feels completely true to the sense of adventure the series has always brought to its audience."

Although Owen Gleiberman's review in Variety just as accurately called it "a dutifully eager but ultimately rather joyless piece of nostalgic hokum." "Dial of Destiny" was the first Indiana Jones movie not to be directed by Steven Spielberg, and the entire affair felt like a tired victory lap with many new figures juggling the baton. It didn't help that star Harrison Ford was almost 81 when the movie was released to the public. He was hardly the same action star he was in "Raiders of the Lost Ark," released 42 years prior, and wasn't as well-suited for the fighting and whip-swinging the character was better known for.

"Dial of Destiny" isn't a low-quality embarrassment, but it's also simultaneously too large and too small. It only ever felt like a means to milk more money out of 1980s I.P., and never a serious step in the evolution of the character. John Rhys-Davies wasn't the only one who was disappointed.

Meta also uses the Muse name for a family of AI models. The price is what makes Ember interesting. On Artificial Analysis's benchmark, Ember and Anthropic's Claude Haiku 5.5 both rounded to a score of 36 at their highest reasoning settings. OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna scored 28 and cost about 15 cents per task at its highest setting.

In Meta's published results, Ember beat Haiku on a terminal coding test, while Haiku did better on a test that judged whether code changes were ready to merge. Meta added that it already has even cheaper models in training.

As the company continues to bet that AI assistants will take on more everyday tasks, developers will need an affordable way to handle all the little steps behind the scenes. A flight search, for example, can mean sorting through dozens of fares, each with its own rules. If Meta wants people to hand off that kind of chore every day, it has to make checking the carry-on policy cheap on its end, too.

HOUSTON, Nov. 9, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Creality, a global leader in the 3D printing industry, today introduces mBox, an AI 3D printer designed specifically for kids. Priced at $329, mBox is now available exclusively on TikTok Shop in the U.S. from November 9 to December 10.

mBox is built for families with children aged 6â16 who enjoy drawing, block building, model making, DIY projects or exploring technologies. It is particularly designed for young creators who want to go beyond just buying ready-made toys and instead turn their own imagination into tangible and personalized creations, whether through independent projects at home or hands-on activities in the company of parents. mBox is also ideal for STEAM learning spaces where educators seek to integrate safe and simple 3D printers into everyday classroom subjects, like science experiments, engineering design, and creative arts.

Developed specially for kids, mBox prioritizes safety in its product engineering. Its fully enclosed chamber keeps the nozzle and moving components behind an enclosure while children watch their creations take shape. During printing, the unheated build plate eliminates the need to handle a heated bed when removing finished prints, while activated carbon filtration helps filter impurities from the chamber air.

Notably, a configurable door-open alert offers three response options: no action, alert only, or alert and pause. When the alert-and-pause is set and triggered, printing stops and parents receive an app notification.

Together, these thoughtful design features help create a safe and friendly space for printing and creating, ensuring parents feel more at ease as children build 3D models on their own.

mBox focuses on simplified operations to make 3D printing easier for kids to get started with.

With the 3.97-inch full-color touchscreen, kids can pick a model and start printing in just a few clicks without connecting to a phone. Before printing, auto leveling simplifies print preparation and helps improve first-layer adhesion, reducing the need for parents' manual adjustments. During printing, the CoreXY motion system with vibration compensation supports stable printing and cleaner surface details, while AI detection uses the built-in camera to identify issues like warping, foreign objects, and spaghetti failures, and then alerts are sent to parents through the app.

In addition, there is RGB status lighting to show print progress and a low-noise mode which keeps operating noise at or under 46 dB.

Beyond the printer itself, mBox bridges kids with dedicated software, Creality Park App and Creality Park Studio. The Creality Park App is a 3D playground showcasing curated models and STEAM courses in a fun way and offering pick & print experience for younger users. For those ready to take their ideas further, the Creality Park Studio, a PC-based platform, offers approachable 3D modeling tools to help design original creations.

The ecosystem also incorporates AI tools CubeMe and SnapForm, enabling kids to turn photos into personalized, printable figurines, keychains, and accessories.

From a simple idea to a piece of creation, mBox aims to spark imagination and encourage hands-on making, helping young creators turn inspiration into something they can hold in hands.

mBox is now available exclusively on the U.S. TikTok Shop at the price of $329 from November 9 to December 10.
