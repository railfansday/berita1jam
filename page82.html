The "shameful" measles data that lost the US its elimination status

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Measles became endemic in the US months ago—and it’s only accelerating.

International health authorities formally announced on Tuesday that the US has lost its measles elimination status, a hard-fought public health triumph won in 2000 after decades-long vaccination efforts. The loss had been widely expected by public health experts for months, and the decision was leaked by the press last week. Still, having the designation officially stripped stings.

After the announcement Tuesday, public health experts, doctors, and political leaders decried the dramatic backslide for the US, calling it “shameful” and a “national embarrassment.” News articles point to the staggering vertical rise of measles cases this year, currently over 5,400 cases, more than any other year since 1991. An explosive measles outbreak in Pennsylvania accounts for over 1,500 of those cases. There have been 10 confirmed deaths across three states, several deaths just in the last week.

But the measles data that caused the US to lose its status—the bits of it that are public at least—paint a darker picture of where the US is in its rematch with measles, the most contagious virus known on Earth. For one thing, almost none of the data from this year—the cases, the explosive Pennsylvania outbreak, the deaths—factored in to the loss. It was lost earlier. What’s happening now is a runaway epidemic.

The US measles elimination status is a designation granted by the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), which is a regional division of the World Health Organization. Last week a committee of experts assembled by the PAHO, called the RVC, met to review measles data for countries across the region, including the US. (RVC is short for the Measles, Rubella and Congenital Rubella Syndrome Elimination Regional Monitoring and Re-Verification Commission.)

For their review of the US data, the RVC heard from an independent committee of experts from the US, called the NSC, which presented measles data collected from January 2025 onward. (The NSC is short for the US National Sustainability Committee for the Elimination of Measles, Rubella, and Congenital Rubella Syndrome.)

According to the PAHO, an elimination status is lost when epidemiological and laboratory data shows that a single viral lineage has spread continuously for at least 12 months within a country. At this point, the virus is considered to be reestablished or endemic. So, to withdraw the US elimination status, the RVC needed to see evidence that a single introduction of a measles virus led to a 12-month-long chain of cases, which requires a lot of data.

The full set of data the NSC presented to the RVC is private for now. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention needs to review and approve such data for public release, which can be a lengthy process that has not yet occurred.

The Department of Health and Human Services, which oversees the CDC, did not respond to a question from Ars Technica regarding when the data may be released.

But Ars Technica spoke with the chair of the NSC, Noel Brewer, who tried to talk through the pieces of data in the NSC report to the PAHO that he could discuss—these are pieces that have already been made public at some point by others, such as by state health officials or researchers. Brewer said the NSC presented the PAHO with genetic data spanning January 2025 to January 2026, and also epidemiological data that extended to June 2026.

In a press briefing Tuesday, officials for the PAHO made clear that only some of the data presented by NSC factored into its assessment of the US elimination status. The RVC only looked at the reporting period between January 2025 and January 2026, which doesn’t include the massive outbreak ongoing in Pennsylvania, which began in April. “The number of cases in Pennsylvania did not actually contribute to the decision that was made by the Reverification Commission,” Rhonda Sealey-Thomas, PAHO’s assistant director, explicitly told reporters Tuesday. For 2025, the US reported 2,289 cases nationally.

Still, there was no ambiguity about the US losing its status; “multiple lines of data show clearly that the US has sustained transmission of measles,” Brewer told Ars.

Brewer, who is also the Gillings Distinguished Professor in Public Health at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, confirmed that the beginning of the end of US elimination was January 20, 2025, in Texas—that date was included in the letter PAHO sent to the US informing it of the loss of elimination status. That Texas outbreak was declared over on August 18, 2025, after 762 cases were confirmed in the state. But the virus carried on elsewhere and is still simmering.

“Since January 20, 2025, the US, Canada, and Mexico have been passing measles cases back and forth across borders,” Brewer said, and the national data shows multiple, “genetically distinct” measles introductions into the US. “However, there is one genomic lineage that predominates across the cases and ties many of them, the majority of them, together,” he said.

For people closely tracking the US measles status, a key question has been whether the big outbreaks that followed the initial one in Texas were clearly linked. If they were, it would be immediately obvious that the US had lost the elimination status; but if not, and the outbreaks were driven by different virus introductions, the US could have kept its measles-free status based on the technicality of how the status is defined.

Data that might hold the answer to this question has been elusive. The measles strain in Texas, D8-9171, appeared similar to the viruses seen subsequently in big outbreaks in Arizona and Utah. But the strain was also similar to those from Mexico and Canada. Generally, there can be relatively small genetic changes among measles viruses, and genetic data alone is not used by PAHO to evaluate elimination. In June, ProPublica tried to get at the answer itself, conducting its own genetic analysis of measles strains in the US. It concluded that “the measles virus still spreading in Utah as of this May is very closely related to the one that sickened Texans over a year ago.” But the outlet acknowledged this wasn’t a “smoking gun.”

To date, the question lingers. Brewer cannot yet explicitly disclose what the NSC reported to the PAHO about possible links between the Texas outbreak and any of the subsequent large outbreaks in 2025. That includes the one in nearby Utah or one in South Carolina, which began in October 2025 and ultimately tallied 997 cases by the time it was declared over in April 2026.

For its part, the PAHO did not respond to multiple requests for comment from Ars Technica on linkages between the outbreaks.

But in a twist, Brewer noted that the answer to the question might be moot; the lineage that started in Texas on January 20 might not be the only one that has been sustained and could have lost the country its status.  There are “one or two that have gone on for more than 12 months,” he said.

With the designation now formally lost, health and medical experts are coming to grips with the reality that measles is once again endemic in the US. Details of how the US should move forward are up for debate; the US is now required to submit a “corrective action plan” to the PAHO, outlining how the country intends to regain its elimination status. But the fundamental solution is obvious and straightforward: The US needs to increase vaccination rates.

While the virus has been raging in religious communities with low vaccination rates, the entire US has seen slipping vaccination coverage. That makes it easier for the virus to gain a foothold virtually anywhere.

A protective vaccination rate is considered to be 95 percent; according to the latest data, only 92.4 percent of kindergartners were vaccinated with the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in the 2025–2026 school year. Only 10 states have MMR vaccination rates at or above the 95 percent threshold. Meanwhile, non-medical exemptions from school vaccination requirements have never been higher, at 4 percent. Currently, 24 states have exemption rates at or above 5 percent, meaning that even if they are able to vaccinate every single non-exempt child, they would still not meet the 95 percent target. And this is just the data for young children. The vaccination coverage for adults is another concern, and the data there is scarce. In Pennsylvania’s massive outbreak, 1,055 of the 1,561 cases this year have been in adults ages 18 and older (less than 1 percent of the cases were fully vaccinated).

While some health officials have noted optimistically that 92.4 percent in children is just a few percentage points from the target, the reality of bringing up vaccination rates is more onerous.

PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa stressed the point in the press briefing Tuesday: It’s “not the national average or the average in the state or even the average in a big city, you need to guarantee that in every community, village, you have this vaccination coverage up to 95 percent. This is very important,” Barbosa said.

The point has been demonstrated in the US. In 2019, the US came very close to losing its measles elimination status. Unlike now—when the virus is on a nationwide rampage, with 47 states and jurisdictions reporting cases—in 2019, the US nearly lost its status from a single, large outbreak that spread in New York. Though it didn’t explode on a national level, it took 11 months to stamp out in those undervaccinated communities. In that year, the US reported 1,274 measles cases total.

The situation in the US has only gotten worse, and the outlook is bleak, Brewer warns. “The measles outbreaks in Canada and Mexico are winding down,” he noted. “However, in the US, our measles problem continues to accelerate. There is no end in sight.”

You can't fairly judge a phone's true merits after a few hours, or even a few days. I’ve been using the iPhone Duo as my daily driver for the last fortnight, and now feel I’ve had time to take stock of what shines (and what doesn’t) when it comes to Apple’s first foldable phone.

Is this one of the best foldable phones you can buy? Unquestionably. But that doesn’t mean there aren’t some imperfections to be wary of. The good news for anyone on the fence about this $2,000 handset is that even after 14 days, the wow factor hasn’t worn off. Folding away what's essentially an iPad mini and tucking it into your pocket just doesn’t get old — even though I wish more of the apps were optimized for the 1:4:1 aspect ratio displays.

The iPhone Duo is a first-generation product, and it offers a different proposition from the tried-and-tested formula of the iPhone 18 Pro and iPhone 18 Pro Max. But even when you compare the Duo to Samsung’s eighth iteration of a foldable phone, it’s impressive just how much Apple has got right.

Speaking of the Galaxy Z Fold 8, I’ve seen a lot of reviewers — including my colleague Mark Spoonauer — wishing the Duo was closer to that phone’s 201g weight. When I first picked up the Duo at Apple HQ, I didn’t mind the 254g weight — and I still don’t. It’s concentrated when folded but balances out when the device is open. The iPhone Duo 2 will surely trim the fat a bit, but for me the extra heft isn’t a strike against the Duo yet.

Here are a few other things that I like best about the iPhone Duo

It’s obvious, I know — but being able to run two apps simultaneously on my iPhone has proved a huge productivity win time and time again. For example, we cover a lot of live events at Tom’s Guide, and the iPhone Duo lets me keep Slack open on one panel with a livestream broadcast locked to the other.

It also makes uploading images a cinch. Keep the Photos app open on the left with Google Drive on the right, and you can just drag and drop pictures into your desired location. It’s a huge time-saver that I’ve gotten very used to.

This Duo-exclusive feature hasn’t been getting as much appreciation as I’d have thought, considering we’re accelerating towards Thanksgiving and the holidays at an alarming rate. If, like me, you’ll be trying to get sugar-fueled children to sit still for Christmas photos, this camera trick will come in clutch.

Fire up the Duo’s camera and when the phone is unfolded the outer screen will play some clips of Snoopy to grab their attention while you snap the perfect portrait shot. The only downside I’ve noticed is that the kids won’t be looking exactly at the camera because the Duo’s 48MP Dual Fusion camera is positioned to the side. Still, it’s a pretty ingenious little feature that’ll earn its stripes on Thanksgiving weekend.

Every single workday for years, my phone has sat face up on the desk, occasionally illuminating with a notification or a call. Since I started using the iPhone Duo, I prop the little guy up in “Tent Mode”, where the outer display shows my calendar, a photo slideshow or the music I’m listening to. If needed, you can also stream content on the outer display.

For the most part, I use it to display multiple time zones at a glance. I’m based in London and routinely need to work with colleagues in New York and Los Angeles. It’s true you can achieve the same effect with a regular iPhone and a MagSafe stand, but if you’re hot desking or working from coffee shops, then being able to prop the iPhone Duo into StandBy mode all by itself is a big win.

As I said at the outset, not everything is perfect with this phone, and there are a couple of things that give me pause when I get asked if the Duo is worth the price of admission. I’ve already mentioned app incompatibility, so I won’t rehash that here, but here are the two big sticking points that I bump into again and again when using the iPhone as my daily driver.

Apple is reportedly actively working on Face ID for the iPhone Duo 2 (along with adding an Action Button) and it can’t come soon enough. I don’t have anything necessarily against Touch ID — except in this case because I’m left-handed. When I’m using my right hand, I can swipe up, thumb the power switch and unlock the Duo in a split second.

But the majority of the time, I instinctively pick up my phone with my left hand and curl my index finger around the back to hit the button that way. I’ve grown accustomed to it but it’s slower and more awkward than Face ID and feels like a step backwards in usability.

The latest development beta includes something of a workaround: users will be able to activate the front-facing camera to unlock the phone within a 45-minute window of using their passcode or Touch ID. It's still in the early stages, but it could become a huge accessibility boost for lefties like myself.

Smaller tablets make for excellent notebook replacements, and this was one of the things I was most looking forward to using the iPhone Duo for. Alas, the only Apple Pencil I have in my possession is the Apple Pencil 2nd Generation from an aging iPad Pro. Which means I need to buy the Apple Pencil (USB-C) in order to use with my Duo.

I’ll admit, this is a minor hang-up; I expect many people already own the $75 Apple Pencil (USB-C) and are happy to have it working on the Duo. I’m just not one of them and would have appreciated if the $2,000 iPhone Duo worked with serviceable Apple tech I already own, rather than having to spend even more money to unlock its full potential.

Still, the good news is the Apple Pencil (USB-C) is currently reduced in the Black Friday sales, so if — like me — you haven’t picked up one to work with your Duo yet, now is the time to do so.

Before the first cold snap, have your car’s battery tested free at an auto parts store, check tire tread and pressure, top up antifreeze and winter washer fluid, and replace worn wipers. Then pack an emergency kit for the trunk in case you’re stranded anyway.

Cold weather lowers a battery’s power just as the engine needs more of it to start, so a battery that starts the car slowly in October can fail on the first frigid morning.

AutoZone and O’Reilly Auto Parts test batteries free, right in the car. Replacing a weak one now costs only a battery you’d soon need anyway, while waiting until it fails adds a jump-start or tow, plus a late start to work.

Where snow and ice are routine, switch to winter tires, which keep their grip below about 45 degrees, when all-season tires stiffen. Your regular tires don’t wear while they’re stored, so they last more seasons. If you’ll keep the car a few years, put the winter set on its own wheels, so each spring and fall swap skips paying a shop to remount the tires. Where snow is occasional, all-weather tires can stay on all year, and unlike most all-season tires they carry the mountain-and-snowflake symbol for severe snow.

Stock the trunk with an ice scraper, a blanket, a flashlight, jumper cables or a jump starter, kitty litter for traction, water, snacks, and a phone charger. Save a roadside assistance number in your phone, and decide whether a AAA membership is worth it.

Drivers stuck in snow should stay with the car, keep the tailpipe clear so fumes can’t back up inside, and run the engine about 10 minutes an hour for heat, with a window cracked.

Arsenal and Chelsea are reportedly keeping a close eye on Norwegian wonderkid Gabriel Rajkovic, with the highly-rated teenager attracting interest from several of Europe’s biggest clubs ahead of a potential transfer battle next summer.

"This generation is really coming back to theaters, which is wonderful," he said. "So I just try to instill in them that it can be like that at home, too. If you give it that same kind of reverence, it can be just as fun.

Israel launches new ground operation in Gaza

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Israel has said its military is targeting Hamas infrastructure in central Gaza and urged civilians to avoid the area. The fight is unfolding against the backdrop of a dire food and shelter situation for Palestinians.

Israel says its military is involved in operations on Wednesday in central Gaza in an effort to dislodge Hamas militants.

France's AFP news agency reported "intense Israeli artillery shelling amid ongoing gunfire and airstrikes."

A statement from the Israeli military (IDF) said, "The IDF launched a precise operation within Gaza earlier today west of the Yellow Line to remove a threat posed to IDF troops operating in the Gaza Strip."

The Yellow Line in Gaza was established in an October 2025 ceasefire between Israel and Hamas, and separates Israeli and Hamas-controlled territory.

On Wednesday, COGAT, an Israeli military body responsible for civilian matters in the Palestinian territories warned international aid agencies and Palestinian civilians to avoid areas near the Al-Bureij and Nuseirat refugee camps and Gaza's main thoroughfare, the Salah al-Din road.

Palestinian medics told Reuters news agency that one person had been killed in an Israeli airstrike in central Gaza and that two more had been killed in separate incidents unfolding in Deir al-Balah and Gaza City.

Hamas — which is listed as a terrorist organization by Israel, the US, Germany, the EU and others — blasted the Israeli operation, calling it "a flagrant violation of the ceasefire agreement and confirmation that the occupation has reneged on its obligations."

Israel's military countered that it was reacting to Hamas ceasefire violations and the group's attempts to rearm.

The October 2025 ceasefire halted major operations, but both sides have continued to carry out limited attacks. Gaza health authorities report at least 1,600 Palestinian deaths and Israel reports the loss of fewer than 10 soldiers since the ceasefire went into effect.

In all, Palestinian authorities say more than 74,000 people have been killed inside Gaza during Israel's retaliation for Hamas' October 7, 2023, assault — which killed roughly 1,200 Israelis, most of whom were civilians.

Beyond the threat of violent gunfire and airstrikes, civilians in Gaza continue to face ongoing food insecurity and lack of adequate shelter as winter approaches.

On Tuesday, the International Organization for Migration (IOM) and the UN's World Food Programme (WFP) warned that funding shortfalls and access restrictions put untold numbers of Palestinian lives at risk.

Currently, more than 1.7 million Gazans are displaced, with IOM and WFP saying 1.4 million face acute hunger and 1.98 million require shelter assistance.

With Gaza's infrastructure decimated by years of Hamas mismanagement and pounding Israeli attacks, more than 80% of Palestinians are unemployed, meaning they cannot afford food and have been forced to burn garbage to try to keep warm.

"Families in Gaza are facing their first sleepless nights of a very long winter ahead," said WFP Palestine Country Director Shaun Hughes. "Unless we get immediate support to scale up the humanitarian response, we risk this becoming not only a shelter crisis but also a deeper food security crisis."
