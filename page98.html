Elon Musk says: ‘I’m not MAGA’

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Elon Musk, who spent $277 million to get Donald Trump reelected president in 2024 and who, during that campaign, declared himself “dark MAGA” and who, after the election, was subsequently photographed sporting a cartoonishly large MAGA hat while visiting the Oval Office, has some news for Americans: None of that necessarily makes him MAGA.

“I’m not MAGA,” Musk declared Wednesday during a conversation with Andrew Ross Sorkin at The New York Times DealBook Summit. Musk joined the conversation via video call, as he is currently in China, joining President Trump for the APEC summit.

“Well, at least I’m not like … I’m not sure what MAGA means,” he backtracked. “If that means sort of blind adherence to a set of policies by the Republican Party, then I certainly don’t have that.”

The comments were made amidst a broader question about how Musk felt in the wake of this year’s midterms, during which a “blue wave” led to Democratic victories throughout the country. Musk, notably, spent a gargantuan amount of money (reportedly some $445 million) to support Republican candidates. Musk admitted Wednesday that such vast political spending might not have been a good use of his money.

“I feel pretty foolish about having spent so much money and not seen much for it,” he said. “Or maybe it would have been much worse without that. But it certainly shows that, you know, American elections are not just about money.”

During his conversation with Sorkin, Musk also seemed to lean away from his viral edgelord persona and claimed that he held no extreme views. He said: “I don’t think I have like, you know, any kind of extreme political views.”

Sorkin pushed Musk on this a little, noting that some people found his views on immigration extreme, to which Musk said: “Like what?” Mild laughter spread throughout the audience.

Ironically, the last time Musk spoke at DealBook, in 2023, he was in the middle of a very public battle with his social media site X’s advertisers. Critics had accused Musk of platforming a typhoon of hateful content on his site, and advertisers were threatening to walk over it. In response, Musk famously told Sorkin that he hoped those advertisers would “go f—” themselves.

Now Musk has an entirely new set of concerns. Democratic lawmakers in various committees have signaled that they may investigate Musk and his business activities as they probe potential corruption throughout the Trump administration.

Specifically, officials have said they’re interested in scrutinizing Musk’s role in DOGE, the “government efficiency” initiative that Musk launched inside the White House at the start of Trump’s new term, as well as Starlink’s activities in Ukraine, where the company has been accused of throttling the military’s access to its internet service.

Sorkin broached some of the potential concerns for the Tesla CEO: “You hear people like the chairman of the oversight committee saying, ‘Lawyer up,’ and you hear the president saying, ‘If I have to give him a pardon’ — meaning you — ‘I’ll give him a pardon.’”

Musk replied: “I don’t think I need a pardon by the way, because I haven’t committed any crimes.”

Musk then made one of those controversial statements he’s known for, appearing to compare an incoming Texas official to Lavrentiy Beria, the top head of the Soviet secret police under Josef Stalin.

“The incoming Texas, you know, House Majority Leader, said that I should go to prison,” Musk said. “I’m like, the guy’s trying to, you know channel Beria from, like, Stalin, where it’s, you know, show me the man, I’ll show you the crime type of thing.”

It’s not clear who (or what) Musk was talking about. Texas doesn’t have a House Majority Leader. However, he may have been referencing Gene Wu, who is the incoming chair of the Texas House Democratic caucus and who has suggested that Musk be investigated for potential criminal activity.

“Hopefully, in this country, you still need to do an actual crime before you go to prison,” Musk added.

Sudan: Transfer War Crimes Suspects to ICC

(The Hague) – Sudanese authorities should immediately transfer former Interior Minister Ahmed Haroun to the International Criminal Court (ICC) to face charges in The Hague, Human Rights Watch said today. Haroun was recently taken into Sudanese custody and faces ICC war crimes and crimes against humanity charges, committed in Sudan’s Darfur region, in 2003 and 2004.

On November 8, 2026, the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) reportedly re-arrested Haroun, interior minister of Sudan in 2007, when an ICC arrest warrant was issued against him. He was arrested one day after he took part in a video podcast interview, his first public appearance since 2019. The armed forces have not officially confirmed Haroun’s whereabouts, but media reported that he is detained in Khartoum state.

“For two decades, victims of atrocity crimes in Darfur have witnessed Haroun and others evading justice, leading to impunity-fueled cycles of violence that are claiming more victims every day.” said Liz Evenson, international justice director at Human Rights Watch. “If Sudan’s authorities transferred those sought by the ICC to the court in The Hague, as they are obliged to do, it would send a powerful message to those committing crimes today that they too may find themselves in the dock.”

Authorities should also transfer former President Omar al-Bashir—who is believed to be in Sudanese custody in Khartoum state as well—and locate, arrest, and transfer former Defense Minister Abdelrahim M. Hussein. The ICC has also issued arrest warrants for both of them, though there is no information available on Hussein’s location.

The ICC issued an arrest warrant against Haroun in 2007, and another against Hussein in 2012, for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity committed in Darfur between 2003 and 2004. During that period, Sudanese government forces and allied militias carried out targeted attacks against civilians on the basis of their ethnicity, including killings, rape, forced displacement from their homes and land, and burning of their villages. In 2009 and 2010, the ICC issued two arrest warrants against al-Bashir for alleged war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide committed in Darfur between 2003 and 2008.

Following al-Bashir’s ouster in April 2019, Sudanese authorities held Haroun, al-Bashir, and Hussein in custody for charges not related to atrocities in Darfur but failed to transfer them to the ICC. 

After fighting erupted between SAF and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) on April 15, 2023, the authorities moved al-Bashir to Alia military hospital in Omdurman, Khartoum state, where he reportedly remained under guard, while Haroun left detention amid the collapse of prison security, and under circumstances suggesting coordination with judicial authorities.

On September 30, 2026, SAF granted al-Bashir initial approval to travel outside of Sudan for medical treatment. Based on media reports, al-Bashir continues to receive medical care at Alia military hospital and is yet to travel abroad.

UN Security Council Resolution 1593, referring the situation in Darfur to the ICC prosecutor, requires the government of Sudan to cooperate with the court even though it is not a party to the Rome Statute, the ICC’s founding treaty. In 2021, the ICC’s Office of the Prosecutor signed a memorandum of understanding with the Sudanese authorities addressing cooperation for arrests. The ICC lacks the ability to enforce its own orders and relies on state cooperation to conduct investigations and make arrests.

In January and July, the Office of the Prosecutor reported to the United Nations Security Council on its investigation in Darfur, including its continued engagement with Sudanese authorities. In addition to addressing crimes from July 2002 onward, the Office of the Prosecutor is also investigating crimes committed since April 2023 in Darfur. The prosecutor’s office reported “positive” and “significant” cooperation by Sudanese authorities, and assistance in support of its investigative activities, including deployment of the office’s investigative team to Port Sudan.

Nevertheless, Sudan has not fulfilled its obligation to carry out the court’s arrest warrants and transfer the wanted suspects to the Hague. The authorities should provide public information on the whereabouts of all the wanted suspects and take immediate steps to allow their transfer to the court, Human Rights Watch said.

The African Union, the members of the Atrocity Prevention and Justice Coalition for Sudan–– particularly the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Sierra Leone––as well as the European Union and its member states should directly engage with Sudanese authorities, calling on them to comply with their obligation to fully cooperate with the ICC and to transfer Haroun, al-Bashir, and Hussein to the court’s custody.

ICC judges have referred to the Security Council several findings of noncooperation against Sudan and ICC member states in relation to the arrest of ICC suspects. However, the council has never taken steps to enforce these findings. UN member states should urge the Security Council to back the ICC’s mandate in Darfur, including by enforcing the court’s findings of noncooperation on arrests.

The ICC prosecutor’s office should use its next biannual report to the council, in January 2027, as an opportunity to raise any concerns related to Sudan’s cooperation on arrests. 

In 2025, the ICC convicted Ali Muhammad Ali Abd-Al-Rahman (also known as Ali Kosheib), a former leader of Sudan’s “Janjaweed” militias, for war crimes and crimes against humanity in Darfur in 2003 and 2004, and sentenced him to 20 years in prison. Kosheib’s trial was possible after he surrendered himself voluntarily to the ICC’s custody in 2020.

“For victims and survivors of crimes in Darfur, Sudanese authorities’ promises of cooperation mean little without concrete action,” Evenson said. “Sudan’s international partners should use every diplomatic tool at their disposal to press for the immediate transfer of all ICC suspects in Sudanese custody and make clear that cooperation with the court is not optional.”

Fathom Urges Rapid Implementation of California’s AI Oversight Plan

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI governance group Fathom said on November 18, 2026 that Governor Gavin Newsom’s new frontier AI roadmap reflected many of its recommendations, and urged California to implement mandatory independent evaluations for catastrophic risks quickly.

Fathom developed and advocated the independent verification organization (IVO) model, under which outside evaluators assess AI models and systems for risk. CEO and co-founder Andrew Freedman described the report as a concrete plan for putting independent oversight into practice and urged movement with “all deliberate speed.” He listed legislation, rulemaking, state-versus-federal authority, logistics, timing and costs as unresolved questions, and said Fathom would press state and federal governments for transparency, mandatory IVO risk measurement and government-enforced guardrails.

The California Government Operations Agency’s (GovOps) report, dated November 16, 2026 and released publicly on November 18, recommends legislative changes rather than imposing requirements itself. Its broader package includes baseline standards, emergency shutdown authority and possible limits on recursive self-improvement, building on SB 813’s IVO framework and AB 1405’s AI-auditor registry.

Its central oversight recommendation would require every large frontier AI developer to undergo continuing reviews by licensed IVOs, replacing the current voluntary approach for those developers. GovOps would license and continuously supervise the evaluators, with authority to revoke licenses when they lack sufficient expertise, independence or rigor. IVOs would submit unredacted assessments to the state, which would decide whether corrective action was warranted after engaging with the developer.

On both the division of responsibility and the legal test, Fathom says the report tracks its October 30, 2026 implementation memo. California would define acceptable safety outcomes in law, while IVOs would develop and update the technical methods and tests used to assess compliance. The proposed standard asks whether a frontier model is “unlikely to increase the risk of catastrophic harm above the level that would exist absent the model” and whether its anticipated benefits outweigh the residual risk. The design is meant to let testing methods change with the technology while preserving the state’s statutory outcome.

Recursive self-improvement (RSI), the point at which automated AI development becomes self-sustaining, is another area of overlap. The report would make verification of automated AI research and development metrics and assessment of the growing risks an initial IVO responsibility. Fathom’s memo also sought scrutiny of internal and external use, with pre-registered capability thresholds triggering state notification and a hold on further internal scaling pending reassessment.

The memo proposes phasing in each mandate once GovOps determines that designated evaluators have sufficient capacity and expertise in the relevant risk area, arguing that a dependable expectation of mandatory work would attract the capital and talent needed to expand independent verification instead of waiting for a mature market across every risk.

Initially, the state would assign an IVO from a predesignated pool, while the developer paid directly on a time-and-materials basis at state-benchmarked rates. Developer choice would expand in later phases as the pool deepened, but the state would retain powers to veto selections, require rotation, cap concentration, appoint additional assessors and decertify evaluators that lack rigor or independence. Payments would not depend on an assessment’s outcome.

Embedded evaluators would receive continuous access to necessary records, personnel and systems, including access for physical presence at defined decision points, backed by statutory confidentiality and anti-retaliation protections. Enforcement would remain with the state: the Attorney General could seek injunctions and civil penalties, while IVOs would make findings, prescribe corrective measures and refer imminent risks rather than seeking injunctions themselves or making their findings self-executing.

The practical test now sits with the Legislature and GovOps: converting the recommendations into binding duties while developing enough licensed evaluators and preserving credible state enforcement. Fathom’s design makes those steps interdependent, because each mandate depends on available expertise and an IVO’s findings require state action before they can carry coercive consequences.

The New York Times reported on November 18, 2026, that New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago school officials are considering replacements for Google Search because Google’s coming school controls tie Search access to its AI features, in a report carried by The Verge.

The control change due in 2027 links the two settings: a district that turns off Gemini-powered features will turn off Search as well. Google education vice president Chris Phillips said in comments carried by the report that Search and AI are deeply integrated and that schools turning off the AI features would not retain access.

New York City schools chancellor Kamar H. Samuels said in a statement carried by the report: “If Google doesn’t reconsider their position, we will explore other search options.” Chicago Public Schools also said it would consider other providers if Google could not supply Search without AI.

Google’s announcement on November 3, 2026 set out the control changes and promised more details before rollout. Under the plan, K-12 student accounts will have Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search switched off unless administrators explicitly enable them.

In a statement to The Verge, Google spokesperson Laura Breen described the 2027 controls as a response to educator feedback and said Google hears concerns about students using AI to shortcut learning while sharing the goal of helping students learn.

Google’s educational case for the integration rests on Guided Learning, which the November 3 announcement describes as a way to keep students actively challenged without overwhelming them. It uses open-ended questions and step-by-step breakdowns rather than immediately supplying final answers.

When Search identifies certain kinds of homework queries, it will prompt students to reason through the material step by step rather than display a solution outright. Google said it would pilot the Search version in select U.S. schools later in November 2026 to gather educator feedback before expanding to other districts over the following months.

Guided Learning also became globally available in Google Classroom that day. Activities there are anchored in teacher-assigned class material, and teachers receive real-time insights to identify where a class may need extra support.

In a release issued November 13, 2026, New York City Council Speaker Julie Menin and Council Member Shekar Krishnan said they had written to Google CEO Sundar Pichai and requested written answers by November 19, 2026. Krishnan chairs the council’s Committee on Oversight and Investigations.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced a one-year moratorium in September 2026 on AI use by public-school students from 2-K through eighth grade. The council said Google’s guidance puts those students at risk of losing Search, turning an AI restriction into a choice about access to a basic research tool.

The letter asks whether students will retain Search without opting into AI Mode, AI Overviews or any student-facing generative AI, and why Google cannot offer a student version that excludes those features. It points to YouTube Kids as an example of Google modifying a product for younger users through different features and content controls.

It also asks what safeguards would preserve genuine district choice and how Google measures, monitors and mitigates AI-generated misinformation in educational settings. On privacy, the council asks whether Gemini and other classroom AI products collect student data, what the data is used for, where it is stored and whether Google earns revenue from selling or using it.

Google’s AI in Education report says schools retain ownership of their data and that, within core Workspace for Education services, student data is not sold, used for advertising or used to train generative AI models. That policy’s stated scope is core Workspace services, while the council’s request explicitly names Gemini and other classroom AI products.

For district leaders, the dispute turns an administrative AI setting into a question of student access to research. The report also noted that U.S. schools use Google Classroom, Chromebooks and other services extensively, making a complete break with the company potentially difficult. A new search provider could address one service, but any wider separation would involve far more than replacing a search box.

Finally, on the 16th of November 2026, Judge Jessica G. L. Clarke, when granting an acquittal, wrote,

The question before the Court is not whether the Peraire-Buenos’ conduct was right or moral, or even whether it violated uncharged crimes.

It is simply whether the evidence at trial, construed in a light most favorable to the Government, could support wire fraud and related convictions in this case. Because the Government failed to prove that the Peraire-Buenos made any material misrepresentations in the execution of their scheme, the motion is granted.

A culture and policy-encouraged default of investing in a safe non-crazy thing that you can’t easily liquidate.

If someone handed you $8,300 every second, what would you do? That’s how much pure profit Nvidia now makes. By the time you finish reading this paragraph, Nvidia will likely have cleared $45,000 and change.

It’s easy to look at Nvidia, a company that just lost its visionary founder CEO due to a series of scandals, and think that the company and maybe the entire AI industry are in terrible trouble. Perhaps so?

But I want you to understand: Nvidia isn’t your average semi-successful company that might tempt a new CEO to fix things that are broken. It is a company so staggeringly, mind-bogglingly successful it’ll do everything in its power not to rock the boat. There is so much money at stake.

It’s so much money that when I heard about Jensen Huang’s resignation, my first thought was: I need a way to visualize this jaw-dropping amount of money for you. I want to show you that Nvidia has risen so far, so fast, that rival chipmakers AMD, Intel, and Qualcomm barely qualify as competition now.

If you’re on a desktop OS, you could mouse over each dot in our chart above to see the staggering sums. But we built another way to visualize the rise of Nvidia I like even better. It’s a horizontal bar graph that not only puts Nvidia’s revenue growth in perspective, but shows you its monster profits right alongside them — plus the world events that made Nvidia a star.

TL;DR: The crypto boom and the great GPU shortage have nothing on the AI boom. Those earlier periods saw Nvidia’s profits briefly peak at $1.24 billion and $3 billion a quarter respectively, while the rise of data centers has driven them to $65 billion per quarter with no sign of stopping.

Yes, Jensen may have set a trillion dollars of Nvidia’s market cap on fire — from around $5 trillion at the start of November to “only” $3.6 trillion now — but the smoldering company is raking in $700 million in profit a day on average.

Put another way: Nvidia could give a $1 million bonus to every one of its 42,000 employees, every three months, without running out of cash. Or, you know, Nvidia could end world hunger or provide clean water for everyone, now that it’s making over $200 billion in pure profit a year.

Maybe that’d make people feel differently about data centers?

But even if we stick to self-serving capitalism, you’re probably still a happy investor if you bought Nvidia a decade ago. A $1,000 investment 10 years ago is now worth over $67,000. You’d have over $1 million if you invested $15,000 back then.

And make no mistake, Nvidia is sticking to capitalism. Nvidia interim CEO Colette Kress just promised to return as much money to investors as possible on the company’s November 17th earnings call. “Going forward, we intend to return all excess free cash flow net of strategic uses to shareholders,” she announced. “All” is a lot more than the 50 percent baseline that Jensen promised five months ago.

Nvidia isn’t the only company whose fortunes have risen this dramatically. RAMageddon revealed that just three companies — Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron — controlled nearly 90 percent of the entire global DRAM market, and those three have repeatedly signaled that they’d prefer to celebrate record profits instead of ramping up production too fast.

But while their revenues and profits have been skyrocketing even faster over the past year or so, even Big RAM hasn’t caught the AI wave quite the same way as Nvidia. Samsung, of course, doesn’t just make memory — its revenue numbers include phones, tablets, TVs, home appliances, chips, displays, camera sensors, audio, networking equipment, medical equipment, and more.

In the most recent quarter, Micron made $37.7 billion in profit, SK Hynix made $29.8 billion, and Samsung came in at around $55.3 billion, but they’re all shy of Nvidia’s $65.3 billion. Historically, RAM has also been a bit more cyclical: Both Micron and SK Hynix actually lost money in 2023 because they produced too much memory.

On its latest earnings call, the first since cofounder Jensen Huang’s resignation, interim CEO and CFO Colette Kress reinforced the idea that Nvidia expects the good times to keep on rolling.

Asked how things might change under new management, she suggested that they wouldn’t: “From a business perspective, it’s business as usual,” she said. She added that the company has secured the backing of customers and suppliers including TSMC, the all-important firm that actually fabricates Nvidia’s chips.

“Our momentum is real, our demand is accelerating, our supply is growing. Our customers and partners are with us, and employees are committed,” she claimed. “We’ve got this.”

Nvidia claims it’ll rake in even more money next quarter, with projected revenue of $130 billion. Profit may be proportionally smaller since gross margins are estimated to drop to 71–72 percent (compared to nearly 75 percent), but Kress said Nvidia intends to get them up as high as 73 percent next year.

In other words: This astounding gravy train isn’t going anywhere. At least that’s the plan.

In an interview with Polygon, Rockstar North co-head Rob Nelson explained why GTA 6 takes players to the mountains.

Nelson said that one of the biggest challenges the team faced when designing the game was figuring out the map. Rockstar’s map team began working on the game in 2015, Nelson said, well before anyone else at the studio. While creating another city was easy enough for a team that has mastered that art, expanding to surrounding Florida locales was a tougher proposition. That’s because Florida is a very flat state.

The problem with depicting it as is, though, is that GTA players likely would have some expectations about what should be in a modern GTA map. GTA 5, for instance, built off of Los Angeles to create Los Santos. Because of that, the map has a lot of variety in elevation. That wouldn’t be possible if Leonida stuck too close to reality, so Rockstar ultimately decided to include some mountains to keep GTA 6 a little more consistent with GTA 5.

Though it may not be accurate, it’s a welcome addition to the map. As fun as it is to drive around Vice City, weaving around the same highways can get old. Lummox County offers a lot more open space for off-roading, biking, and other activities that just wouldn’t be possible in a city. For instance, when I found a bicycle perched atop a hill, I found myself racing down a natural BMX course that was built into the world. Every time I head up to Lummox County, it feels like I’m on vacation.

Crucially, it’s also the best date spot in the entire game. You can take Jason and Lucia out on impromptu dates throughout GTA 6, letting the couple play some mini-golf together or blow off steam at a shooting range. One of my favorite play sessions so far simply happened when I got the two in a truck and took them up north to a part of the map I’d never seen. We got out when we found a hiking trail and wound up stumbling into some ruins high up on a hill. It was a peaceful moment of discovery that the two got to share together. Moments like that are what make GTA 6 special, and Leonida’s intricate design is the thing that powers it all.

First thing I try is to refresh the quotes screen; sometimes the quote is stale by the time you click Preview. If you do this on the order entry (Trade) screen (not the full DOB screen), you need to re-enter the quantity to get the correct price.
I suspect a stale quote is a problem with any system that shows you a market quote. If you wait too long to execute the trade, the market can move against you, possibly by only a tiny amount, and then the order fails. One solution would be for the system to let you buy at the market at the time of execution, although you'd risk a big market move in a short amount of time (a low risk IMO).
This happened to me again today at Fidelity in buying qty 1 of the Jul 2036 TIPS'; refreshing the quotes then reentering the qty enabled me to place the trade, which was executed immediately.

This almost never happens to me at Schwab--can't remember that last time it did. I think this is because Schwab provides the min qty 1 quotes from its own inventory, and prices are relatively stable for those.
