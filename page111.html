How Do You Land on a Moon With Almost No Gravity?

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Japan’s MMX mission aims to sample the Martian moon Phobos

Japan's MMX spacecraft will fire its engines to slow down and enter orbit around Mars.

Last August, looking up from the edge of an ancient river delta on Mars, the Perseverance rover’s electronic eye spied an oblong shadow crossing the distant, glowing Sun. Phobos, the Martian moon named for the Greek god of fear and panic, was transiting the solar disk. Too small to darken the pale, dust-tinged sky, but orbiting close enough to eclipse a significant fraction of the Sun, Phobos passed in less than a minute.

NASA’s Perseverance rover captured Phobos passing in front of the Sun from the surface of Mars.ASU/MSSS/SSI/JPL-Caltech/NASA

The tiny moon, with its murky origins, is an enigma, and one whose makeup and history could tell us much about Mars and the wider solar system. And now the international Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission, which successfully launched on 20 October, seeks to explore that mystery with an unprecedented series of landings on the unknown surface of Phobos.

This mission brings unusual challenges—for example, designing an orbit to study a microgravity moon orbiting a much larger planet. Engineers also had to figure out how to assess and land on this low-gravity pile of loosely assembled dirt and rock, how a rover might rove across it, and how the spacecraft might take samples and return them to Earth.

“I often say that landing on Phobos is like trying to land a balloon,” says Yasuhiro Kawakatsu, project manager of MMX at the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). “It’s very difficult to drop a balloon to the ground and prevent it from tilting or bouncing.”

Phobos is one of two small Martian moons; the other is Deimos, named after the Greek god of dread and terror. Their cosmic origins are unknown, though there are two prevailing theories. One holds that the moons were once asteroids from the belt between Mars and Jupiter, captured by the smaller planet’s gravity. In the other theory, Phobos and Deimos formed when a large celestial body slammed into Mars, sending debris into orbit. Some models suggest the pair are the last survivors from a cluster of satellites.

The enormous Stickney crater, roughly 9 kilometers across, dominates this view of Phobos taken by NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.University of Arizona/JPL-Caltech/NASA

Scientifically speaking, Phobos is considered a rubble pile, a loosely consolidated aggregate of rock and dust. Far too small to become spherical under its own mass, it’s shaped like a lumpy potato, roughly 27 kilometers in length, with only 1/1,800th of Earth’s gravity. On Phobos, a typical adult would weigh about as much as a tennis ball does on Earth.

More than 10 years in the making, MMX launched on Japan’s flagship H3 rocket from Tanegashima spaceport on 20 October local time. It will arrive at Mars in 2027 carrying scientific payloads from several space agencies, including NASA, and a new rover called Idefix developed by the French and German space agencies (CNES and DLR) in collaboration with JAXA.

Engineers assembled MMX’s modules at Japan’s Tanegashima Space Center before the spacecraft’s October 2026 launch.JAXA

MMX consists of three modules: a propulsion module to power its journey to Mars and apply the brakes to enter orbit around the red planet; an exploration module carrying the rover, sampling gear, and other scientific instruments; and a return module, attached to the exploration module, that harbors the capsule destined for a high-speed reentry into Earth’s atmosphere. After collecting samples on Phobos, the exploration and return modules will lift off together. The exploration module will then be jettisoned while the return module sets a course back home.

If all goes according to plan, MMX will deliver a 10-gram sample to Earth in the second half of 2031. But the mission will face a series of daunting challenges to reach that end point, including navigating complex orbits around Phobos and landing on its surface while Mars periodically blocks communications with Earth.

The MMX’s modular design includes the propulsion module [left] and the exploration and return modules [right].Kyodo News/Getty Images

“It’s the most complex mission I have ever seen,” says Patrick Michel, director of research at CNRS (the French National Center for Scientific Research) and co-principal investigator on Idefix.

An H3 rocket carried MMX into space on 20 October 2026, beginning the mission’s five-year journey to Phobos and back.The Asahi Shimbun/Getty Images

Once MMX reached low Earth orbit, its propulsion module’s engine fired, kicking off a nearly year-long deep-space cruise to Mars. On arrival, the spacecraft will need to fire its engines in an extended, carefully timed burn to slow down enough to be captured by Mars gravity. The propulsion module will then be jettisoned, leaving the exploration and return modules to orbit Mars.

“If something doesn’t go according to plan, MMX will fly by Mars and get catapulted into the solar system,” says Nicola Baresi, an astrodynamicist who coauthored a paper on specially designed orbits for MMX.

From that initial orbit, MMX will transition into a specially designed quasi-satellite orbit that allows it to keep pace with the small moon but also move around it. Since Phobos is too small to orbit in the conventional sense, the spacecraft will orbit Mars alongside the moon, following a clever egg-shaped path that slings MMX alternately ahead of and behind the moon. “When you’re closer to Mars, you’re accelerating with respect to Phobos, and when you’re farther from Mars, you’re decelerating. So you essentially go back and forth,” explains Baresi.

Martian Maneuvers To reach Phobos, MMX will execute three major maneuvers: MOI-1 will put the spacecraft into orbit around Mars, MOI-2 will transfer it toward Phobos, and MOI-3 will place it into its final orbit alongside the moon.Chris Philpot

To reach Phobos, MMX will execute three major maneuvers: MOI-1 will put the spacecraft into orbit around Mars, MOI-2 will transfer it toward Phobos, and MOI-3 will place it into its final orbit alongside the moon.

The best existing images of Phobos, from spacecraft such as NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter and ESA’s Mars Express, have resolutions of only a few meters per pixel. That’s larger than Idefix, the mission’s rover, itself about the size of a microwave oven. To map the surface in greater detail, MMX will loop in front of and behind Phobos at distances ranging from 200 kilometers to less than 20 km from the surface.

During its two-year remote sensing campaign, the exploration module will use several cameras and a laser altimeter to map the surface in high resolution and look for candidate landing sites. When Mars passes behind the Sun as seen from the Earth, causing a roughly two-week communications blackout, MMX will retreat into a larger, stable orbit. After communications resume, MMX will prepare to send its first visitor down to the surface.

Before the exploration and connected return modules attempt to land, Kawakatsu says, MMX will send a scout that in Japanese is called “the first penguin,” leaping into unknown waters. For this mission, the first to leap will be the Idefix rover, named for the canine companion to Obelix in the French comics series Asterix. The exploration module will release the rover during a low-altitude pass of between 40 and 100 meters above Phobos’s surface in 2028 or early 2029.

MMX will use its TENGOO camera [left] and LIDAR laser altimeter [right] to map Phobos’s terrain.JAXA

It will be a tense moment that calls back to 2005, during Japan’s Hayabusa mission to explore the asteroid Itokawa. In a similarly designed drop, the MINERVA rover was released from an orbiter, but erroneously at 200 meters instead of the intended 70 meters. The rover missed the asteroid and, without propulsion, drifted off into deep space.

Idefix will get a slight nudge out of the exploration module toward its negligible-gravity target, freefalling and likely tumbling as it goes. It’s expected to touch down with a velocity of less than 1 meter per second. Unlike rovers that have landed on other surfaces with airbags or dedicated landing legs, Idefix will land without protection, apart from some extra padding on the solar panels due to their critical nature. The barely-there gravity of Phobos should allow the 23-kilogram rover to land softly, though exactly how this landing will play out is hard to predict.

The Idefix rover will travel to Phobos with its wheels tucked in and solar panels folded.DLR

“It will bounce probably several times,” says Stephan Ulamec, Idefix’s co-principal investigator and project manager at DLR. “It will come to rest somehow in some position.” Once it’s still, four retracted legs will deploy to right the rover autonomously, regardless of its orientation. It will then unfold its solar panels to charge its batteries for exploration tasks ahead.

A simulation shows how Idefix is expected to tumble onto Phobos, right itself using its articulated legs, and begin exploring the moon’s surface. DLR

Once operational, Idefix will roll at a snail’s pace, topping out at one millimeter per second. Its metal wheels have protruding blades like a paddle wheel, designed to gain traction in Phobos’s microgravity. “If we were to drive our rover faster, then it would actually be more like we were swimming through instead of driving across the surface,” says Cecily Sunday, a doctoral student involved in testing Idefix’s WheelCams at the Institut Superieur de l’Aeronautique et de l’Espace (ISAE-Supaéro) in Toulouse, France.

The WheelCams, a pair of cameras placed on the underside of the rover, will observe the wheels and their tracks during Idefix’s planned 100-Earth-day primary mission. The images will help researchers study the size and distribution of grains in Phobos’s regolith and how cohesive they are. How much or how little the grains stick together will greatly impact how Idefix moves. Sunday likens this to the difference between driving on dry and wet sand. “Think of trying to build a sandcastle with dry sand,” she says. “You can’t. Add water, and suddenly you can.”

The Idefix rover was mounted between the MMX spacecraft’s landing legs for the journey to Phobos.DLR

The team tested the rover’s nine-bladed wheels on a variety of materials, including Phobos regolith simulants provided by JAXA and DLR, lunar simulants, glass beads, quartz sand, and clay pebbles. “We characterize the mechanical properties of each simulant precisely, then test across the full range, with varying grain size, angularity, and even color, so we can map what we see in the WheelCam images back to actual surface properties,” Sunday says.

Despite the microgravity and Phobos’s rubble-pile nature, Sunday says Earth-based tests can approximate the rover’s motion on the surface of the small moon fairly well. While Phobos’s reduced gravity lowers the rover’s effective weight, there is also reduced pressure between the grains beneath its wheels. “As long as you’re moving very, very slowly and using the same materials and the same weight of the system, they should be representative,” Sunday says.

With four paddle-like wheels and deployable solar panels, Idefix is designed to crawl across Phobos.Ardan Fuessmann/Imago/Alamy

Idefix’s interactions with its new home will offer “sub-millimeter resolution and information on porosity, on grain size, on surface strength, on how hard is the material,” Ulamec says. “It fills a gap between remote observations from orbit and detailed observations of the return samples back on Earth.”

What Idefix finds on this rocky, rugged world will help determine how and where JAXA attempts to land the main spacecraft. Michel says the touchdown will provide ground truth for understanding of Phobos. When NASA’s OSIRIS-REx spacecraft touched down on the asteroid Bennu to collect a sample, its sampling arm surprisingly sank nearly half a meter into the loose material. But Ulamec doesn’t think Idefix will sink deep into Phobos, noting that Bennu is very different and much smaller, with a diameter of 500 meters.

Once Idefix has investigated the surface, MMX will face an even greater challenge: landing its much larger exploration module on Phobos. The spacecraft will make a controlled descent to roughly 10 meters above the surface, then drop the remaining distance in free fall. With so little gravity to pull it downward, even a small sideways motion could cause the lander to tip or bounce away. A successful, upright landing would mark the start of up to two hours on Phobos for the crucial sampling phase.

The MMX mission draws on the experience and technologies of Japan’s two Hayabusa sample-return missions, which landed on the asteroids Itokawa and Ryugu. But this mission has many more moving parts. MMX’s exploration module will use two sampling devices—a coring sampler developed by JAXA and a pneumatic sampler provided by NASA and fabricated by Honeybee Robotics, a subsidiary of Blue Origin. Data from these tools, and those carried by the Idefix rover, could answer questions about the origin of Phobos and how the Mars system has evolved.

The coring sampler uses a robot arm and a cylindrical corer designed to reach at least 2 centimeters below the surface. The arm can maneuver to find the best site for digging. “That by itself is a challenge because we’ve never done it,” Michel says. “You never know what can happen at the very sampling site itself, where you might have a boulder and the arm cannot move.”

Probing beneath the top layer will be valuable because solar radiation, galactic cosmic rays, and micrometeorites have battered and weathered the surface. The material below should offer a better record of the moon’s origins, and JAXA aims to collect at least 10 grams of it.

MMX will carry both the Idefix rover [bottom] and a robotic coring sampler [top] designed to dig beneath Phobos’s weathered surface.JAXA

The pneumatic sampler takes a different approach. Mounted on one of the lander’s legs, it will inject pure, non-reactive nitrogen gas into the surface and capture the grains stirred up. This sample could illuminate the changes Mars and its moons have undergone over time. The dual system also adds redundancy in the face of Phobos’s unknowns. “If the surface of Phobos proves to be very hard, [the coring sampler] may struggle to penetrate the ground. [The pneumatic sampler] would not experience this issue and still be able to gather material,” Kawakatsu says.

One of the most intriguing aspects of MMX is the additional possibility of collecting material from the planet below. It’s likely that Phobos has been peppered by Martian ejecta thrown into space by meteor impacts, so MMX also provides an opportunity to return the first Martian samples to Earth. Michel estimates that the samples could contain “a few hundred grains coming from Mars.” Scientists can distinguish those grains from native Phobos material by analyzing their oxygen isotopes.

That possibility raises a planetary-protection question. A direct Mars sample-return mission would have to follow strict rules to ensure that potentially living biological material from Mars doesn’t contaminate Earth. But those protocols don’t apply to MMX, even if its sample does collect grains of Martian soil. A panel of experts concluded that any microbes blasted off Mars wouldn’t survive the violent impact and ejection or the intense radiation exposure on the way to Phobos. This saved MMX from implementing expensive and time-consuming quarantine and containment protocols for the returning sample.

After landing on Phobos, MMX will have roughly two hours to collect samples from the moon’s surface before lifting off again.JAXA

During the brief surface operation, the robotic arm will deposit the samples in a storage unit, from which they’ll be transferred into a capsule aboard the return module and sealed in a vacuum. After that, lift off.

Once back in Mars orbit, the exploration module will resume its observations of Phobos and make several flybys of Mars’s other diminutive moon, Deimos, to gather data on its surface, structure, and composition. In November 2030, during a favorable alignment of Mars and Earth, MMX will begin the homeward leg of its trip. The return module will separate from the exploration module and power the voyage home, its sole objective to bring the dust of Phobos to Earth.

MMX’s sample return capsule will carry material from Phobos through Earth’s atmosphere in 2031.JAXA

Finally, in 2031, the 60-centimeter-wide sample capsule will plunge into Earth’s atmosphere at roughly 12 kilometers per second. Assuming it survives the extreme heat of reentry and lands safely in Australia, scientists will begin to tease out clues about Phobos and the early solar system. JAXA’s Institute of Space and Astronautical Science will receive and curate the samples, which will also be shared with mission partners.

“We have macroscale measurements from the spacecraft, microscale measurements with the rover, and then sample analysis,” Michel says. “So we can relate the three kinds of scales together because we have all of them.” The insights gleaned from orbital images, close-up images of the regolith, and the chemical analysis of the samples could have wider implications for our understanding of our solar system.

“MMX is a mission to understand the Mars system and shed light onto how terrestrial planets like the Earth, and maybe Mars, develop a habitable environment,” Kawakatsu says. “We strongly suspect that our terrestrial planet region formed dry, and water and organics were scattered inwards from the icy-rich outer solar system.” Mars, he says, sits at the gateway between the inner and outer solar system, making its moons valuable witnesses to the processes that shaped potentially habitable worlds.

The European Space Agency's (ESA) Hera spacecraft has gotten its first close-up look at the Didymos binary asteroid system. If that system sounds familiar, it's because it made headlines in September 2022 when NASA's DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test) spacecraft smashed into it.

The aim of the DART mission was to test whether an impact could divert an asteroid away from a collision course with Earth. The purpose of Hera is to follow up on DART by making up-close observations of the Didymos system.

"The first thing we said was, 'Wow,'" Hera Principal Investigator Patrick Michel said in a statement about the image, which ESA released on Thursday (Nov. 5). "This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART’s camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world."

The Didymos system consists of the eponymous 2,560-foot-wide (780 meters) space rock and its "moonlet" Dimorphos, which is about 530 feet (160 m) in diameter.

The DART mission impacted Dimorphos on Sept. 26, 2022, with the aim of causing a change in the orbital period of the moonlet. And it worked: Before the impact, Dimorphos completed one lap around Didymos in around 11 hours and 55 minutes. The DART collision reduced the length of this period by around 33 minutes.

The change is a result of the fact that Dimporphos is now traveling at around 2.7 millimeters per second more slowly than it did before the collision.

The newly released photo shows Didymos in detail. Just a sliver of Dimorphos is visible, below and slightly to the left of the main asteroid.

The changes caused by DART were small. But in a real Earth-threatening scenario, even such a tiny shift could help divert an asteroid on a collision course — as long as scientists get enough advance warning of its approach.

Hera's observations should help reveal what other changes have occurred on and around Didymos.

Scientists are already seeing that, while Didymos has the same central ridge along its equator as before the impact, many changes have occurred. This includes the creation of multiple boulders, craters, and patterns of grooves or ridges.

"Since we expect a lot of impact-ejected material to have made it across the approximately 1 kilometer [0.62 miles] of space from Dimorphos to Didymos, it may be that some of these characteristics originate from this transport of material, and we have exciting work ahead of us to sort it out," said Michael Kueppers, ESA’s Hera mission scientist. "We look forward to seeing much more of both bodies and systemically mapping all the changes we identify."

The poisoned public rights doctrine and Sun Valley Orchards

On Tuesday, Nov. 10, the court will hear argument in Department of Labor v. Sun Valley Orchards, LLC. This case concerns the H-2A visa program for temporary alien immigrant workers. The issue is whether the Department of Labor’s Article II administrative law judges have the authority to collect ruinous fines from employers who violate their employment contracts with such individuals.

In early August of this year, I signed an amicus brief supporting Sun Valley Orchards along with former Attorneys General Edwin Meese III and Michael Mukasey and law professor Gary Lawson, prepared and filed by our lawyer Amit Vora. We argued that the respondent was entitled not only to a Seventh Amendment civil jury but also to an Article III federal court.

After reading the government’s opening and reply briefs by Solicitor General D. John Sauer, I stand by our argument that a DOL administrative law judge may not deprive Sun Valley of property by imposing ruinous fines for violating a contract that bound it to follow certain labor laws in employing foreign immigrant H-2A workers. Rather, Sun Valley has a Seventh Amendment right to have its claims heard by a jury if it desires to do so.

The government is obviously correct that the government has a sovereign right to control immigration into the United States under the H-2A program. It does not follow from that, however, that the government can condition the admission of foreign workers under this program on Sun Valley’s surrendering its constitutional right to have employment disputes with those workers adjudicated by Article III courts with a Seventh Amendment right to a jury trial. Constitutional rights cannot be taken away without due process of law or in violation of, for example, the First, Fourth, or Seventh amendments. But that is exactly what the government is trying to do in this case.

By way of example, in 1987’s Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, the Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Antonin Scalia, held that the California Coastal Commission could not use its plenary power over zoning to condition a renovation construction permit on the Nolans ceding a public easement to walk along their valuable California coastal property, as doing so would violate the takings clause of the Fifth Amendment. Likewise, the government cannot impose unconstitutional conditions when using either its zoning power or its plenary power over immigration. Nor can the government use its commerce power or any of its other enumerated powers to violate one’s constitutional rights. The government can shut down the foreign immigrant H2A program. But it cannot use it as a cudgel to make Americans surrender their constitutional rights simply because the rights of immigrants are at stake.

Central to the government’s argument otherwise is the public-rights doctrine. This doctrine – properly understood – allows for privileges created by the government to be adjudicated by non-Article III judges, such as the privileges to receive social-security, disability, or welfare payments; the privilege to immigrate into the United States; or the privilege to receive tax rebates under the federal government’s partial waiver of sovereign immunity. With regard to such privileges, the Constitution’s guarantee of an Article III forum and the Seventh Amendment civil jury trial right are inapplicable because the receipt of these were not property rights recognized at common law, but privileges created by the government. The government argues that because immigration into the country is also a privilege, not a right, Sun Valley’s conduct related to this are not subject to Article III protections.

It is true that the public rights doctrine was profoundly altered (one could say poisoned), in the 1977 case of Atlas Roofing Co. v. Occupational Safety and Health Administration. In Atlas Roofing, Justice Byron White denied access to an Article III court and a Seventh Amendment civil jury trial to a party subject to an OSHA enforcement order. According to White, because such orders were based on “new statutory obligations,” they could be “committed exclusively to an administrative agency [for] deciding whether a violation has in fact occurred.” This was incorrect: while OSHA created new torts that can occur in the workplace, these were analogous to the kinds of tort actions that would have been heard at common law. Indeed, such torts are classic “Cases in Law,” which Article III gives sole jurisdiction over to life-tenured federal judges assisted by a civil jury if the plaintiffs choose one. Thus, Atlas Roofing Co. changed the law to say that the public-rights doctrine not only allowed Article II administrative law judges to deprive persons of privileges, but, for the first time, it also eliminated the guarantee of a Seventh Amendment jury trial when a person or entity was deprived of property rights by the government.

Fortunately, this dangerous precedent was limited by the more recent 2024 case of SEC v. Jarkesy. In Jarkesy, the Supreme Court held that when the SEC imposed civil penalties against a defendant for securities fraud, the Seventh Amendment entitled the defendant to a jury trial. This is because in 1787 a fraud case, like the employment contract case at issue in Sun Valley (or, for that matter, the property seized in Atlas Roofing), would have been “Case in Law” assigned to the sole jurisdiction of the Article III courts.

So the lesson of Jarkesy is this: While the vast majority of cases which ALJs decide only “privileges” and not “rights” only require an Article II hearing, when federal regulatory agencies impose ruinous fines on a corporation or person, those fines are deprivations of one’s right to property. And in depriving one of such property, it is not enough to have an administrative hearing: The case must be heard by an Article III court with the right to a Seventh Amendment civil-jury trial.

The public-rights doctrine then does not apply to enforcement orders that deprive a person of property by agencies such as OSHA in Atlas Roofing, the SEC in Jarkesy, or the DOL in Sun Valley. Nor does calling a case an immigration matter empower the government to take away the constitutional rights of Americans even if immigration into the United States is itself a privilege and not a right.

So let’s hope that the Supreme Court in Sun Valley rejects, as unconstitutional, the DOL’s attempt to funnel a private-rights dispute away from an Article III forum. And let’s further hope that the court clearly reframes the public-rights doctrine, as limited in Jarkesy, as a mere manifestation of the rights/privileges distinction – and continues to prevent the doctrine from taking the form that Justice White allowed for in Atlas Roofing: an ominous threat to Article III, the Seventh Amendment, and the due process clause.

Like “Rocky” itself, it’s an inspiring and extremely entertaining crowd-pleaser; it also devotes a surprising amount of screen time to Stallone’s writing process and the machinations required to actually get his screenplay produced — the movie doesn’t get to the actual production of “Rocky” until over an hour into its running time.

When Travolta saw “Rocky III,” he realized Stallone was a filmmaker with the narrative and visual energy he needed for his sequel. Stallone was game, and together he and Travolta crafted “Staying Alive,” a crowd-pleaser in which Tony Manero would trade the disco floor for the Broadway stage.

Much as Stallone had minimized the grittiness of the original “Rocky” in the slicker “Rocky III,” “Staying Alive” ditched the racist and misogynistic characters of “Saturday Night Fever” (and their relatively bleak prospects) in favor of a rousing showbiz tale in the tradition of “42nd Street” and “Footlight Parade.” Critics scoffed, but audiences loved it — “Staying Alive” was one of the biggest hits of 1983, and after a brief stumble with “Rhinestone,” an ill-advised musical from the director of “Porky’s” pairing Stallone and Dolly Parton, Stallone the screenwriter came back with three smash hits in a row.

1985 represented a peak year for Stallone as both writer and star, with the release of two of his most successful pictures, “Rambo: First Blood Part II” (cowritten with a young James Cameron) and “Rocky IV.” Then came 1986’s “Cobra,” the cop movie in which Stallone’s screenwriting voice reached its fullest apotheosis and purest form. The film grew out of Stallone’s aborted stint playing Axel Foley in “Beverly Hills Cop”; Stallone drastically rewrote the screenplay before leaving the project, but when Eddie Murphy came on board and the tone shifted from action to comedy, Stallone’s material was scrapped.

Stallone reworked his discarded scenes into “Cobra,” a blatant “Dirty Harry” ripoff that replicates the vigilante formula of that film and its sequels but removes every trace of their ambiguity. In “Dirty Harry,” “Magnum Force,” and “Sudden Impact,” parallels are drawn between Eastwood’s detective Harry Callahan and the villains to suggest that the cop and his prey are not as far as apart as Callahan would like to believe. “Cobra” doesn’t entertain such moral complexity for a single second — from the moment the first bad guy is introduced parking in a handicapped spot to later revelations about other villains sexually assaulting children, it’s made clear that Stallone’s Lieutenant Marion “Cobra” Cobretti is completely justified doing whatever he has to to wipe these guys off of the planet.

Just as Stallone pared down the structures of his own “Rocky” and “Rambo” films to the essentials in their sequels, with “Cobra” he takes the iconography of the “Dirty Harry” movies — right from the “Magnum Force”-inspired pre-credits sequence in which Stallone provides crime statistics in voice-over as a gun fires into the camera — and expresses it with pure id, stoking the audience’s bloodlust to a fever pitch. This is not a criticism. “Cobra” is, in its way, as perfect a script as “Chinatown,” a supremely engineered entertainment machine without a wasted line or shot. While some of this was reportedly dictated by commercial concerns — the movie was cut down to its lean 87-minute running time in an effort to squeeze in an extra showtime each day — the ruthless efficiency is so characteristic of virtually every movie Stallone ever wrote that it can’t really be arbitrary.

Stallone the screenwriter would follow “Cobra” with “Rambo III” and “Rocky V,” two of the weaker entries in their respective franchises, but 1993’s “Cliffhanger” was a truly perfect action movie that revived Stallone’s career as an actor after a pair of comedy flops, John Landis’ criminally underrated “Oscar” and the merely criminal “Stop! Or My Mom Will Shoot.” After that Stallone slowed down as a screenwriter and focused more on acting — he didn’t take another writing credit after “Cliffhanger” until “Driven,” another collaboration with “Cliffhanger” director Renny Harlin, in 2001.

Stallone returned to his typewriter (or, presumably by this time, laptop) in 2006 to revisit Rocky Balboa for the sequel of the same name, and the project seems to have reignited something in the writer’s artistic soul. “Rocky Balboa” begins a new phase for Stallone as a writer, one in which he switches from trying to perfect his formulas to pondering their purposes and effects. This film and the “Rambo” sequels that would follow are among Stallone’s best work as a screenwriter, and they tend to be overlooked given that they fall outside of his most popular era as a movie star.

“Rocky Balboa” doesn’t abandon the franchise’s standard tropes, but it pokes around at their edges to rediscover the humanity that made the original “Rocky” such a hit. Rousing and deeply heartfelt, it’s a formula movie that rarely feels clichéd thanks to Stallone’s genuine love for his characters and his determination to end the series on a high note. At the time, after a series of underperforming and undernourished films, Stallone had as much to prove as his onscreen alter ego, and in “Rocky Balboa” he wipes his slate of artistic and commercial failures clean with 102 minutes of unadulterated cinematic passion.

As “Rocky Balboa” begins, the title character is living a comfortable but dull life as a restauranteur, and mourning the death of his beloved wife Adrian. Desperate to prove to himself that he still has what it takes, Rocky applies for a new boxing license and, through a series of slightly unconvincing but quickly paced circumstances, ends up in the ring with the current heavyweight champion of the world. The boxing story is a lot of fun, and as a director Stallone stages the final match with energy and style, but the movie’s real heart is in the scenes leading up to the fight.

As usual, Stallone packs a surprising number of subplots and emotional high points into the film’s compact running time, and he’s able to do this by building on what has happened in the previous films via a kind of cinematic shorthand. He doesn’t need to waste a lot of time on what Rocky has lost or where he has come from, because those of us who have seen the other movies can fill in the blanks. Stallone uses his alter ego to explore all the things that matter to him as an aging legend: missed opportunities, lost and found loved ones, and the desire to give what he was born to do one last big shot.

It’s impossible to avoid the parallels between Balboa’s story and Stallone’s own, but this isn’t a case of an actor playing himself — he and Rocky may have the same feelings, but they express them in completely different ways. One of the pleasures of the first two Rocky films was Stallone’s blunt, authentic dialogue writing, and his talent for clumsy but entirely appropriate urban speech returns in “Rocky Balboa.” The film contains several significant relationships, from the expected sparring with brother-in-law Paulie to a new romance and a sweet father-son love story, and in each of these storylines the dialogue is pitch-perfect.

Early on Stallone figured out how to write in such a way that Rocky communicates his emotions with total clarity even though he’s an uneducated, inarticulate character, and the style reaches its zenith in “Rocky Balboa.” This kind of screenwriting is awfully tricky, but Stallone never gets the balance wrong; less astute viewers might mistakenly assume that the awkward dialogue is stupid or unintentionally obvious, but it’s exactly right for this character and this story.

After “Rocky Balboa” got Stallone’s creative juices flowing again, his output as a screenwriter resumed; in 2010 he created a whole new franchise with “The Expendables,” and scripted two solid Jason Statham vehicles, “Homefront” and “A Working Man.” He even returned to Rocky Balboa one more time for “Creed II.” Stallone’s best late-career screenwriting efforts, however, were devoted to his other iconic character, Vietnam vet and one-man killing machine John Rambo. In fact, the last two entries in that series are so good they recalibrate our entire understanding of the character and the franchise as a whole.

That the “Rambo” series became one of the more durable franchises in American cinema is a bit surprising in retrospect, given that it didn’t really find its voice until the fourth installment and began with a film that didn’t lend itself to sequels at all. By the time Stallone returned to the saga in 2008 for the simply titled “Rambo,” he had clearly spent years thinking about the character and where his own ambitions for the franchise might intersect with his audience’s expectations.

The result was a movie in which Stallone was able to have it both ways, digging deeper into the self-doubt and unremitting pessimism that always made John Rambo an unusually interesting mass appeal action hero while also delivering bloody set pieces that made “Dawn of the Dead” look like “Driving Miss Daisy.” The most recent entry in the cycle, 2019’s “Rambo: Last Blood,” continues along those lines with a tale that brings Stallone’s ongoing philosophical inquiry into the possibility of meaningful action in a meaningless world to its fullest fruition.

“Last Blood” was underestimated during its initial theatrical release, including by me — I didn’t fully grasp its greatness until revisiting the entire series. Watching the films in order I was surprised to find that for all the stylistic disparities between the movies, they do have a common thread in Stallone’s depiction of Rambo as a hero who doesn’t really like himself or what he has become — the cumulative emotional impact is comparable to what might have happened had Clint Eastwood decided to make a whole series of movies about his William Munny character from “Unforgiven.”

The authentic turmoil that Stallone conveys gives even the cartoonish second and third “Rambo” films more depth than they’re normally given credit for, and undercuts their reputations as simplistic expressions of rah-rah Reagan-era patriotism. (In championing the films, Reagan showed that he misunderstood them almost as badly as he misunderstood Bruce Springsteen’s “Born in the U.S.A.”) Stallone’s status as an eighties action icon alongside Arnold Schwarzenegger and Chuck Norris has overpowered his actual appeal and value as both an actor and a writer in the films that made him a star; his specialty isn’t invulnerable supermen but haunted antiheroes plagued by regret.

James Mangold understood this about Stallone and used the actor’s traumatized quality to enormous advantage in “Copland,” a movie for which Stallone the actor deservedly received his best reviews since the original “Rocky.” Mangold was one of the few filmmakers who really knew how to use Stallone to his maximum potential, though others (like Richard Donner in the underrated “Assassins”) occasionally came close, and Stallone recently pulled off a late triumph with Taylor Sheridan’s “Tulsa King.” For the most part, though, Stallone the actor has always been best served by Stallone the writer — not just in the days documented by “I Play Rocky,” but throughout his career.

The best rock bands create their greatest work with their backs against the wall — witness the Rolling Stones — and the legendary British hard-rock powerhouse Motorhead was no exception.

Forty years ago, the band had seen the dissolution of its classic “No Sleep ‘Til Hammersmith” lineup accompanied by a pair of sub-par studio albums, and had been largely given up for dead. But the band’s leader, Lemmy Kilmister, was nothing if not a survivor. He regrouped with a pair of blazing guitarists and thunderous ex-Saxon drummer Pete Gill and, for this album, joined forces with the unexpected choice of producer Bill Laswell, who was well known in experimental-leaning New York rock/jazz circles — and producer Herbie Hancock’s left-field 1983 hit “Rockit” — but seemed an odd fit for Motorhead. The result of this unlikely union was “Orgasmatron,” certainly Motorhead’s most experimental album and one that drew a polarized reaction from many fans and the band itself — the press materials for this reissue euphemistically state, “The album’s production was the subject of much discussion among the band and their fans” — who said its mix was too cluttered and took the band too far from the basics. But personally speaking, I loved it and still do.

In an era when music fans were much more tribal than today, Motorhead were one of the rare bands who were respected in both punk and heavy metal circles. Like AC/DC, their earlier music was rock and roll stripped down to muscle and bone — scorching guitars, piledriving drums and Lemmy’s vein-bulging roar of a voice — like a Terminator version of Chuck Berry. And on “Orgasmatron,” we got all of that in a more-adventurous sonic setting: The songs are classic Motorhead, but hazy guitar overdubs, percussion and effects swoop in and out of the mix, especially on the closing title track, a cauldron of seething guitars, driving drums and Lemmy’s voice, which is laden with effects that make him sound like Darth Vader and Vecna morphed together. Subsequent re-releases have punched up the mix, and this new 40th anniversary edition presents not only a remastered version of Laswell’s original mix, but a 2026 remix from the band’s longtime latter-day producer Cameron Webb that clears away some of the haze and presents the guitars and drums in cleaner sound (although to be honest it doesn’t sound radically different). They’ve also cleaned out the vault, adding some rehearsal tapes (which sound like they were recorded on a boombox), an outtake and a BBC session featuring several songs from the album along with the classic “Killed by Death” — as well as Lemmy reading the lyrics to the song “Orgasmatron,” unaccompanied, like some sort of evil poet.

While the bonus tracks here are nice to have but not necessarily electrifying, the remastered and otherwise soup-ed up sound on the original album makes this one of the top reissues of the year — “Orgasmatron” now roars louder than ever.
