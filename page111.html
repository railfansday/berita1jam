Nabrawind’s Crane-Free Wind Towers Face a Supplier Test

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Spain’s Nabrawind has developed a way to install exceptionally tall wind turbine towers without bringing an enormous crawler crane to the site. Its Nabralift system uses a tubular upper tower supported by a three-legged steel structure. Hydraulic equipment raises the assembled turbine while modular sections are inserted below it near ground level. The lifting frame can then be removed and used on another installation. This addresses genuine logistical and construction problems, particularly at remote sites where specialist cranes are expensive to transport and where wind can interrupt conventional lifting operations.

The company has progressed beyond demonstration-scale hardware. In September 2026, UL Solutions issued type certification for Envision’s 7.8 MW turbine on Nabrawind’s 188-meter tower. Fortescue is using 17 of those tower configurations at the 133 MW Nullagine wind project in Western Australia’s Pilbara region, where construction is underway. Nabrawind previously erected a 160-meter prototype in Spain in under two weeks and has supplied smaller commercial towers in Morocco and France.

The engineering benefits are credible. Nabrawind reports using roughly 20% less tower steel than comparable conventional tubular designs and reducing foundation concrete by as much as 80%, depending on the design and site. Those are supplier claims rather than independently established savings across all wind farms. The potential to avoid large cranes, simplify transport and raise turbines in windier conditions could also matter substantially to total installed cost and construction schedules. High hub heights only earn their keep when those costs are justified by the electricity generated over the project’s life.

The current deployment has an unusual commercial structure. Fortescue acquired Nabrawind in September 2025 and is building Nullagine to serve its own mining operations. Fortescue has the capital, internal electricity demand and industrial incentive to support a technology it owns. Successful installation in that context would be important evidence of construction performance, but it would not establish that an unrelated wind developer can finance and maintain the same system under ordinary third-party commercial arrangements.

Nabralift’s proprietary construction approach creates a procurement issue. Conventional tubular towers can be sourced from several qualified manufacturers, and experienced contractors can compete for much of their construction work. A developer selecting Nabralift currently depends on one supplier for the core tower system, specialist lifting knowledge and associated support. Wind turbine original-equipment manufacturers also create supplier dependencies, but concentrating another critical part of the project in a proprietary installation system requires its own contract and risk assessment.

A type certificate provides valuable technical assurance about the certified turbine-and-tower configuration. A lender or insurer still needs enforceable delivery schedules, long-term warranties, repair and maintenance provisions, clarity on replacement parts, and a workable remedy if the supplier’s capacity or commercial priorities change. These questions become more important when a wind farm must operate for 25 years or longer and there is no qualified alternative provider of the same system.

Nabrawind’s next important proof point is an independently developed and financed large wind farm that accepts those risks on commercial terms. Qualified installation partners, credible long-term service arrangements and greater competitive capacity could make its engineering advantages easier for developers to buy. The 188-meter tower has earned technical attention; its broader market depends on financing and procurement confidence catching up with the machinery.

The Dow soars 1,500 points as a Democratic midterm election sweep lifts markets

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Oil prices fell more than 7% and Nvidia stock dropped 3% as the S&P 500 gained 1.58% and the Nasdaq rose 1.44%

The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed up 1,569.40 points, or 3.03%, at 53,356.12 on Wednesday, a day after Democrats won majorities in the House and the Senate in the midterm elections.

The S&P 500 rose 1.58% to 7,688.62, and the Nasdaq $NDAQ Composite gained 1.44% to 25,736.51. It was the Dow's best day since April 9, 2025, according to CNN and CNBC. CNN, citing CFRA Research data, also reported that the gain was the Dow's largest single-day move after a midterm election since 1982. The VIX, a gauge of expected volatility, fell almost 20%, and bond yields declined.

President Donald Trump told The New York Times that he was "absolutely willing" to go along with the outcome. In remarks to The Wall Street Journal, he said he and Republicans were prepared to cooperate with Democrats. The rebound was partly a response to the tone Trump struck at first, according to CNBC, which quoted Jay Woods, chief market strategist at Freedom Capital Markets.

Stocks tied to the broader economy led the gains. Walmart $WMT stock rose 3% and Bank of America $BAC stock rose about 4%.

Oil prices dropped after Vice President JD Vance blamed the war with Iran for Republican losses in a post on X $TWTR. "The reason we lost is the war," Vance wrote. Brent crude settled at $94.62 a barrel, down almost 8.7%. West Texas Intermediate fell more than 7% to about $88.60 a barrel.

Technology stocks split. Nvidia $NVDA stock fell 3%, and CoreWeave stock fell about 10%. Shares of Nebius and Tesla $TSLA fell 6.7% and 3.3%, respectively. Amazon $AMZN stock climbed as much as 8.3% and Microsoft $MSFT stock as much as 7% during the session, as investors appeared to wager that a Democratic Congress would limit AI spending.

Investors had been concerned ahead of the vote that a Democratic sweep might bring tougher curbs on data centers, according to CNBC. Gina Hinojosa, who defeated Republican Gov. Greg Abbott $ABT in Texas, has called for ending data center development in the state. Republicans hold the majority in the Texas Legislature, which limits how much she can change state policy.

Dan Ives, a partner at Yorkville Ives & Co., said the Democratic gains would hold back AI infrastructure construction across America for the next few years, according to ABC News.

Uber Wants Thousands Of Robotaxis By 2027. Waymo Is Already There

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Uber has racked up a ton of autonomous vehicle partnerships, attempting to position itself as the key platform for driverless rides. And the company has no plans to slow down, according to executive comments on its earnings call on Tuesday. Uber says it wants to spend $10 billion to get 2,000 robotaxis into its global fleet by the end of 2027.

“Our focus on autonomous is now on commercial execution," said Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi, per The Times. "The next phase is about delivering the results that demonstrate Uber’s advantage as a platform for commercializing AVs [autonomous vehicles] at scale.”

Around half of those 2,000 robotaxis will apparently be for the United States market. The other half will be split up between Europe, Asia, and the Middle East. The self-driving fleet is planned to populate about 20 different cities worldwide. The goal is 2,000 robotaxis by the end of 2027, with 700 of those online by the end of this year.

Here's the kicker, though: even if Uber hits its goal by 2027, it will still be far behind Waymo, which is both a major competitor and, for now, a partner. Waymo, the most prominent robotaxi company in the world, already has a fleet of around 4,000 cars currently on the road, and it currently operates in 15 different U.S. cities.

Waymo also wants to double its robotaxi count to 8,000 by the end of the year. And while users in cities like Atlanta have to use the Uber app to call a Waymo robotaxi, the two companies could be heading for a split soon.

The Financial Times reported that the two companies are having internal disagreements about robotaxis and are discussing ending their current contract. A Waymo executive even said that the partnership was “a little bit of a crapshoot,” per Bloomberg, since Uber customers never knew if they were getting a robotaxi or a human driver. The company says it plans to end its exclusive deal with Uber in Austin and Atlanta in January of 2028. So, while both companies are successful, Waymo should have four times the number of robotaxis by the end of 2026 than what Uber will have by December 2027. By then, Waymo plans to have a fleet 20,000-deep.

Uber said it eventually wants a fleet of 120,000 robotaxis, per the Financial Times, but didn't put a timeframe on such a lofty goal. It says it's going to fund this fleet of robotaxis with its own earnings, but that it might have to “bring in third-party capital," once its self-driving fleet expands beyond 10,000 cars. Uber claims its gross bookings were up 20 percent, year-over-year, to the tune of $59.8 billion, so it's got a big book of business to fund its future projects. But with it splitting from Waymo, it's never been more important for the company to get its other partnership projects off the ground and expand them quickly.

How better to chronicle a generational evening in US history than on social media? Our founding fathers recounted watershed moments via post riders and printed broadsides; in the mid-19th century, dispatches were sent via the Pony Express. President Lincoln’s shooting was rushed to the public via Morse code tapped over the wires, and during World War I, we utilized undersea telegraph cables. Yet in our long history spanning radio broadcasts, satellite television, and smoke signals, nothing has quite captured the zeitgeist like an X post.

“CNN is now saying the national anthem has been changed to a reggaeton cover of Pink Pony Club,” tweeted one user last night, right as the media joined a chorus of policy analysts and pundits to announce a new dawn as Democrats took control of Congress—a blue wave. Or rather, a blue tsunami.

Social media users across the country then took to X to mourn, deny, or celebrate the victory. Yet amid the cacophony of social media’s tweets, posts, replies, comments, and reels, the most notorious influencer grew shockingly quiet: President Donald Trump himself. Despite posting a half-hearted image of a MAGA-red hat reading “VETO,” Trump has not said much online in the hours following the victory.

In the months leading up to the midterms, social media did more than simply sway victories. In fact, it propelled candidates like Jon Ossoff to the forefront through short-form vertical videos, made contenders such as Abdul El-Sayed go viral through clips set to songs like Eminem’s 2010 hit “Not Afraid,” and directed Gen Z focus to key races through memes (“TEXAS JUST WENT BLUE. More than three quarters of young adults relied on social media or platforms like YouTube as a source for their midterm election news this year. An astounding 67% of millennials also got the lowdown from content creators. Call it absurd, call it brain rot, or call it sensational: There’s no doubt that social media has become a phenomenon, and the Twitterverse is still alive and well in 2026.

OpenAI is already trying to shape the 2028 election

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane thinks the 2028 presidential election could be a defining moment for AI. And he wants politicians to start developing policies that address Americans’ concerns about the technology, rather than simply paying lip service to anti-AI sentiment and the backlash against data centers.

In a new essay, Lehane compares AI’s potential to reshape the economy to President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal, the 1930s set of federal programs and reforms enacted after industrialization and the Great Depression shattered the old economic order. He observes that Americans today feel similarly anxious about losing control over their economic futures and urges politicians to find a “compelling third path” that allows people to share in AI’s benefits. He lays out five policy priorities, spanning worker protections and education to AI safety and data center development.

The essay comes on the heels of a stunning Democratic victory in the midterm elections, in which Democrats flipped control of Congress amid widespread voter frustration over the cost of living. Kate Brennan, who leads AI and corporate power policy at the AI Now Institute, told TechCrunch that Lehane’s rhetoric marks a notable shift from OpenAI’s policy agenda last year, which focused on accelerating AI development, reducing regulatory barriers, and expanding infrastructure.

Unsurprisingly, many of Lehane’s proposals would encourage wider AI adoption and support the infrastructure expansion companies like OpenAI need to grow. The original New Deal established protections for workers and placed constraints on corporate power. It’s less clear how Lehane’s vision would hold AI companies accountable for the economic disruption their technology could create.

“Invoking a New Deal metaphor is to invoke state capacity to actually benefit the public,” Brennan said. “This is written by a private company that is trying to advocate for policies for its own benefit. It is ahistorical at best and harmful at worst.”

While AI wasn’t a dominant issue in the midterms, opposition to data centers emerged as a major bipartisan concern in several races in the midterms. And Lehane may be right that AI will become a bigger part of the conversation over the next two years.

Affordability is already shaping up as a key issue for the 2028 election as Americans grapple with inflation, tariffs, and the rising cost of living. AI could become an increasingly large part of that conversation as voters stress about data centers hiking up electricity bills, AI replacing their jobs, and technology’s economic gains flowing only to a handful of wealthy companies.

Lehane acknowledges many of these concerns, but he offers few concrete proposals to prevent AI’s gains from becoming concentrated. Instead, he largely pitches AI as a path to greater prosperity for Americans willing to embrace it.

Take his proposals for the tax code. He suggests incentives for workers to learn AI skills, easier capital access for entrepreneurs, and new approaches to employee ownership and profit-sharing. Those are not the same as binding requirements that would ensure workers share in AI-generated wealth or that companies pay the costs of displacing workers.

“I was struck by how much of this policy is for a future…that is already predicated on mass AI adoption and AI benefiting society, which we have yet to see,” Brennan said. “All of it is predicated on benefiting from AI’s upside.”

Brennan said it would make more sense to see things like a revised corporate tax rate or tax incentives for hiring human beings rather than automating jobs, as well as ways to keep companies from amassing outsize market power.

Lehane’s emphasis on expanding AI’s economic opportunities, rather than limiting potential harms, extends to data centers. He notes concerns about data centers while emphasizing the construction jobs and local tax revenue they could generate. He then warns that politicians who support a data center moratorium could jeopardize America’s lead over China, going so far as to compare that stance to the 2002 vote to authorize the Iraq war.

Lehane also makes child safety and education central to his proposed agenda, stressing the importance of age-appropriate protections and safeguards around AI companions for minors. He also warns against keeping AI out of classrooms, arguing that doing so could lead to inequality between wealthy students and “children in rural communities.” But the argument takes for granted that AI improves learning, a claim that remains unproven and one critics say deserves more examination than more funding or individualized attention from human teachers.

Lehane also calls for enforceable AI safety standards, independent evaluations, public oversight, and the ability to restrict AI systems or use a kill switch to shut them down — though recent studies show that OpenAI and other AI labs haven’t published comprehensive plans for what they would do if a model escaped human control.

Brennan called these stances “disingenuous,” pointing to OpenAI’s history of opposing legislation designed to impose such measures. In 2024, for example, OpenAI opposed California’s AI safety bill, SB 1047, which would have mandated catastrophic risk evaluations and shutdown capabilities. Last year, OpenAI also sought to limit state regulation, arguing that states could undermine American innovation. The company has more recently shifted some of those positions, backing several state safety laws and saying it would be open to independent evaluators embedded in the company.

Lehane also argues that people should maintain control over consequential decisions made using AI, rather than handing off judgment entirely to machines. But that raises the question of what human control really looks like in a society where humans are increasingly being required to use AI in education, work, healthcare, and government.

Throughout his essay, Lehane frames AI as a technology that could give Americans back their sense of agency and economic opportunity. But he ties average Americans’ “middle class” success to participation in an economy increasingly shaped by companies like OpenAI. Whether his policies will meaningfully redistribute power or subtly entrench it is less clear.

TechCrunch reached out to OpenAI for more specifics on Lehane’s proposals and the company’s stances, but it did not respond by the time of publication.

E.l.f. Beauty on Wednesday blew past Wall Street expectations as it posted a 51% increase in net sales in its second fiscal quarter.

The beauty company said it saw its 31st consecutive quarter of net sales growth, attributing the streak to strong performance across its portfolio. The revenue spike was driven by both retail and e-commerce channels across the U.S. and internationally, the company added.

"The thing I'm most pleased about is we saw strength across our entire portfolio," CEO Tarang Amin told CNBC in an interview. "Every one of our brands contributed strong growth this quarter."

Amin added that E.l.f. is the most-searched brand on TikTok Shop and the No. 2 brand in cosmetics on the platform. The company also said it saw strong results for Rhode, the skincare brand it acquired last year.

The company raised its full fiscal-year guidance following the better-than-anticipated quarter. It now expects net sales in the range of $2 billion to $2.03 billion, compared with a previous range of between $1.94 billion and $1.97 billion. It also upped its adjusted EBITDA projection to a range of $421 million to $427 million from between $401 million and $407 million.

E.l.f. said it now expects adjusted earnings per share for the year to come in between $3.82 and $3.86, exceeding prior guidance of between $3.50 and $3.55 per share. It's also expecting a net income range of $231 million to $233 million, up from a previous range of $212 million to $215 million.

It had already raised its guidance last quarter after outperforming its expectations.

Amin said the company is "very bullish" on the holiday season, expecting high sales through the end of the year.

As much as anniversaries are cause for celebration, they naturally also prompt introspection. For Palmer Harding, this season marks 15 years in operation—and their 60th full collection. These are milestones well worth raising a glass to for any independent brand, not least one that has weathered the market instabilities and headwinds that we’ll take your understanding of as a given.

The past year has prompted particularly significant operational developments for the brand – a noteworthy one being the opening of a retail space within its East London studio. In part prompted by the widely attested decline of global wholesale retail, this direct-to-consumer pivot has allowed for an intimate atmosphere that, Matthew Harding says, “feels almost like when we first started; there’s more interaction between us, the clothes and the customer.”

This, in itself, became something of a prompt for their latest collection; less a nostalgic ‘greatest hits’ offering, more a zeroing in on the pieces they’ve seen resonate. “It’s very much a collection about refining and celebrating our key pieces, translating them into new silhouettes, new categories,” Harding adds.

Contemplating what exactly those pieces were didn’t take them too long. “When we actually hunkered down and said, ‘Okay, what are we about?’ The answer was cotton shirts and shirt dresses. When we find a shape, let's just really hammer it home," Palmer says, holding up a hefty poplin button-down with articulated pleating along its oversized sleeve, creating an easy, boomerang bend. First introduced two seasons ago, here, the aptly-named “Intention” shirt returns across a gamut of plain and striped cottons, and as a full-length dress imbued with a satisfying, fanning swish.

Elsewhere, the asymmetrically-hemmed “Super” shirt—“one of our hero pieces that really launched us way back when,” Palmer recalls, “but that we haven't had in the collection for a long time”—showcases the duo’s knack for sculptural cut and drape with its tie-front and cape-like back. Though the piece that showcases that most intriguingly is what Harding dubs the “Precision” shirt, which features origami-like criss-cross pleating at the waist to create a line that skims the form.

There are, granted, more than crisp cotton shirts and shirt dresses on offer here. A denim riff on the “Intention” silhouette stands out, as does a ruby satin dress with swooping poet sleeves. Their insistent focus on their chosen fabric and garment, however, is in itself somewhat allegorical of the alchemical formula that’s spurred their trajectory over the past 15 years. “There's something really wonderful about taking what’s seen as a boring, everyday fabric and elevating it in a really sculptural way,” Palmer says. “The result is something this blend of something that's really understandable, but also very special.” A decade and a half on from setting out with that mission, the eclectic array of women willing to journey far beyond London’s well-trodden luxury shopping lanes to try and buy their wares in person proves that still holds true.

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services finalized sweeping Medicare payment reforms it says will shift the program from volume-based "sick care" toward prevention, primary care and accountable care.

The 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule rule includes a roughly 2% reduction in baseline physician payment rates with targeted increases and new payment opportunities for accountable care, primary and chronic care and behavioral health. CMS finalized steeper physician payment cuts than it had proposed in July.

An unpublished PDF version of the 2,468-page final rule can be found here.

In a press release, CMS said it is "prioritizing prevention, strengthening primary care, cracking down on duplicate billing and reinforcing fiscal stewardship across Medicare."

The final rule modernizes physician payment structures to "reward health maintenance and coordinated primary care rather than disjointed, volume-based intervention," CMS said.

"Medicare should reward clinicians for keeping people healthy," HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. said in a statement. "Under President Trump’s leadership, we are putting prevention and primary care at the center of Medicare, closing payment loopholes, and cracking down on waste. This rule directs resources toward coordinated care that puts patients first. We are changing what Medicare pays for to change what our health system delivers."

Under the final rule, CMS will officially sunset the traditional Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) reporting option beginning with the CY 2031 performance period. Clinicians will have through CY 2030 to transition to the MIPS Value Pathways (MVPs), which already cover approximately 98% of clinicians, or Advanced Alternative Payment Models. This reflects two additional years of transition time compared to the proposed rule. The rule also finalizes three new MVPs focused on diabetes, hypertension, and hospitalists, and requires clinicians to report at least one specialty-specific "Core Measure" starting in CY 2027.

For physicians, while the proposed rule includes statutory payment increases, Medicare payment rates face an overall baseline decrease for 2027 due to the expiration of a temporary 2.5% legislative increase from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) in 2026.

Under the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act (MACRA), the CY2027 conversion factors reflect statutory updates of +0.75% for qualifying Alternative Payment Model (APM) participants and +0.25% for non-qualifying participants. The changes to the PFS conversion factors for CY 2027 include these updates as required by statute and an estimated -0.12% adjustment necessary to account for proposed changes in work RVUs for some services, according to CMS in a fact sheet.

For clinicians who don't qualify through an advanced alternative payment model, the conversion factor drops from $33.40 to $32.63, a 2.31% reduction. For qualifying APM participants, the conversion factor drops 1.83% from $33.57 to $32.95. In the draft rule issued in July, CMS projected cuts of 1.68% and 1.19%, respectively.

CMS is also addressing what it sees as overpayment and duplicate reimbursement when multiple services are delivered during the same patient encounter. Under the final rule, when an office visit and minor procedure occur on the same day, CMS will reduce payment by 20% for the lower-cost service, reducing duplication in payment by patients and the Medicare program for clinical work already covered in the procedure’s valuation.

CMS had initially proposed paying the higher-cost service in full and reducing payment for the other service by 50%.

Physician groups were quick to criticize CMS' payment changes in the final rule.

"Notwithstanding modest policy improvements to enhance value-based care through MSSP, MGMA is disappointed by CMS’s decision to finalize several destabilizing policies in the 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule," Anders Gilberg, senior vice president of government affairs at the Medical Group Management Association, said in a statement.

"Conversion factor cuts of 1.83% for qualifying APM participants and 2.31% for all other clinicians further degrade an already fragile payment system and amplify severe financial challenges caused by years of inadequate Medicare reimbursement," Gilberg said.

Compounding this ongoing reimbursement decline, CMS will cut payments by 20% when office visits billed with modifier 25 are performed on the same day as certain procedures, "interfering with physician practices’ ability to offer same-day care," he noted.

MGMA is urging Congress to intervene by immediately fixing cuts to the conversion factor, along with changes to modifier 25. "To prevent annual disruption, Congress should prioritize comprehensive MACRA reform by passing the Patients First Act," Gilberg said.

The agency is overhauling the office/outpatient evaluation and management visit complexity add-on code, HCPCS code G2211. CMS is transitioning HCPCS code G2211 from a flat add-on payment to a modifier that would increase the payment of the associated E/M code by 16%. That would maintain an equal percentage increase across all levels of E/M codes, CMS said.

The agency also finalized a new modifier that would offset the costs incurred by practitioners in accountable care organizations (ACOs) when providing longitudinal care and care coordination. The modifier would only be available for practitioners participating in a Shared Savings Program ACO or participant providers in a Long-term Enhanced ACO Design (LEAD) Model ACO and would increase payment of the associated E/M visit by 32%, CMS said.

The final 2027 PFS rule also establishes new 340B reporting requirements. Covered entities will have to report certain Medicare Part D claims involving drugs purchased through the 340B Drug Pricing Program to the Medicare Part D Claims Data 340B Repository beginning with claims with a date of service on or after Jan.1, 2027. The deadline for the first quarterly submission is delayed until Sept. 30, 2027.

In the final rule, CMS tightened remote patient monitoring and remote therapeutic monitoring requirements but backed off an immediate restriction on contractors.

Beginning in 2027, as CMS outlined in the proposed rule, RTM services can only be furnished to established patients, and providers must provide a separately reportable initiating visit before billing for RPM or RTM services. CMS initially proposed only allowing payment for RPM or RTM services when performed by clinical staff employed by the practice and not when those services are delivered by third-party vendors. The agency provided a two-year reprieve on that requirement as it goes into effect Jan. 1, 2029, "giving practices that rely on contractors two additional years to adapt," CMS said.

Health tech companies, providers and lawmakers pushed back on the proposal to restrict third-party vendors, urging CMS to reconsider the proposed changes to preserve patients' access to these tech-enabled services. Many rural, small, and independent practices, as well as both urban and rural health systems, rely on specialized clinical and technology partners to make remote monitoring services available to their patients.

CMS finalized changes to the Medicare Shared Savings Program, the nation’s largest value-based payment program, to "accelerate accountable care service delivery, further CMS’ goal of aligning spending and value in Original Medicare, and support achieving other related strategic objectives," the agency said in a fact sheet.

The overhaul to the MSSP program aims to make ACO participation more financially attractive, encourage organizations to take on risk, expand beneficiary participation and ease some quality-reporting requirements.

The agency says the changes are projected to grow the program by adding up to 1 million assigned beneficiaries in an average year and produce $5.5 billion in net federal savings over 10 years. CMS also projects the changes will generate an overall increase in shared savings net of losses to ACOs by $1.1 billion over a decade.

The final rule changes the Shared Savings Program’s benchmarking and financial methodology to balance incentives between Level E of the BASIC track and the ENHANCED track. The shared savings rate for Level E of the BASIC track increases 50% to 60% and reduce the maximum weight used in calculating the positive regional adjustment for lower-spending ACOs participating under the ENHANCED track from 50% to 35%.

The agency is increasing the prior savings adjustment's scaling factor from 50% to 75%, allowing more of an ACO's previous savings to be reflected in future benchmarks.

Under the final rule, CMS will risk-adjust the 5% cap on benchmark increases, potentially allowing ACOs that care for sicker, higher-cost patients to receive larger upward adjustments.

Data shows that patients aligned with an ACO are less likely to experience uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension and are more likely to receive recommended preventive care, according to CMS. In performance year 2024, 75% of the 476 participating ACOs earned shared savings totaling $4.1 billion, while generating net savings for the Medicare Trust Funds of approximately $2.5 billion compared to projected spending benchmarks.

Under the final 2027 PFS rule, approved ACOs may reduce or eliminate Part B cost-sharing for many items and services for assigned beneficiaries, directly lowering out-of-pocket costs for essential care. That change goes into effect April 1, 2027.

The rule also increases shared savings rates for eligible ACOs, provides new incentives for first-time participants and sets more predictable spending targets. And, the rule simplifies electronic health record reporting requirements, streamlines patient notice mandates and removes outdated administrative hurdles.

These Are the 13 Tactics Expert Whitetail Hunters Actually Use to Tag Mature Bucks

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 4, 2026 5:13 PM EST

Get everything from whitetail news and opinion pieces, to advanced hunting tactics and cutting-edge gear reviews.

The best deer hunters in the nation (known and unknown) all share a common thread. They are students of deer and the woods, and they never stop learning. As such, they continually experiment with new and advanced deer hunting tactics.

In order to learn some of these secrets (and to share them with you), I called a handful of the best deer hunters I know. Here’s how they find success season after season.

Mark Drury of Drury Outdoors has bagged more big deer than most. His approach is proven, and the process he uses includes saving and analyzing a lot of trail camera data, especially historical information. He has kept every single trail cam pic of a racked buck since 2007.

“Save trail monitoring pictures in an organized fashion for easy review in future years,” Drury says. “Bucks are incredibly habitual from year to year. Study your pics year-round to formulate patterns with individual bucks or areas. [Over time you’ll build] a database that helps lead to patterns in future years, as crop rotations, weather, and moon in future seasons mirror conditions that occurred in the past. It takes a lot of time to assemble and study but saves time in future seasons.”

Grant Woods of Growing Deer TV is one of the most renowned deer scientists in the country. He seems to get inside the mind of whitetails and understands what they do and why they do it. To that point, he stresses making hunting decisions based on what deer do, and not what we think they should do.

“An issue I’ve seen is hunters making setup and tactical decisions based on human thoughts,” Woods says. “The hunters I know that have repeated success filling tags in all types of conditions and habitats always think like a deer. They consider what deer need that day, and what will deer avoid.”

As Paul Beasley, co-host of “Canada in the Rough” TV, says, hunters are creatures of habit. “The longer we hunt a piece of ground, the more established our hunting setups and entry/access/travel points become,” he says. “We tend to rely more on what has worked before, and while deer in general also tend to gravitate to the same trails, food sources, and pinch points, it has been my experience that mature bucks need to be treated as individuals and hunted accordingly.

“The buck I shot this year helps illustrate my point. We have hunted this same 100-acre property for over 10 years, and have always taken deer off of it in the same 50-yard-by-80-yard area. Throughout the spring and into summer, this buck was working that area perfectly, but as hunting season approached, his behavior changed. His appearances on camera began to fall off. So, I scrambled to put up more cameras and found that he hadn’t gone anywhere. He was just using different parts of the property.”

Beasley killed that buck on the second afternoon of the season. He arrowed from a setup that his crew had never harvested a buck from before.

“It was through paying attention to this individual buck’s behavior, and not just doing the same old thing that has always worked, that allowed me to put my tag on him,” Beasley says.

Oftentimes deer hunters do things without thinking. We react, rather than making decisions based on logic. HeadHunters TV’s Nate Hosie agrees, and says we should hunt smarter and not harder.

“Growing up, I used to think the more I was out there the better my chances,” he says. “It’s not completely wrong, but as I’ve grown as a hunter, I’ve learned to pick my moments for when to strike. Wind, temperature, time of year, trail cam intel, [and more] now go into my hunt. The less I’m in and out, and the higher probability my attempts are, the more success I find.”

Rick White is a longtime staple in the outdoor industry and he suggests that hunters go all or nothing with scent management.

“One of the biggest things I try to instill in people is always going to the woods as scent-free as possible,” he says. “Always play the wind. Never intentionally hunt a stand on a bad wind. But also know that deer don’t always come in from where we want them to. So, if you’re not going to follow every step with scent elimination, then save your money.”

Read Next: 15 Scent Control Strategies That Actually Work

It sounds like rudimentary advice, but even experienced hunters make blunders that lead to being seen, heard, and smelled by deer. It’s something we must continually keep at the forefront of our mind. Will this entry route cause bedded deer to see me? Will this exit route blow scent into a bedding area?

Bone Collector Co-Host Nick Mundt knows deer hunting as well as anyone. As a former deer hunting guide and current big deer killer, he stresses the importance of going undetected when getting in and out of your hunting spots.

“Never think the wind will just work,” he says. “Always know that they’ll bust you if the wind is wrong. It dictates every sit.”

Aaron Gaines of Ani-Logics advises hunters to kill a target buck as early as possible. The early season and pre-rut offer opportunities to hunt consistent bucks.

“This is when he’s on a pattern and most predictable,” Gaines says. “To do this, I utilize most recent information from cameras. I’m very disciplined on hunting the right wind. If he’s daylighting, and I’ve got the right wind, things usually work out well.”

Read Next: Why the Last Week of October Is the Best Time to Kill a Big Buck

During the rut, whitetail bucks seem to travel via loops. That’s not hearsay or some hokey hack from your grandaddy’s hunting journal. That’s straight from a deer biologist. More specifically, former CEO of the Quality Deer Management Association and current HuntStand biologist, Brian Murphy.

“The biggest piece of advice I’ve learned over the years is to develop a plan and then stick with it long enough for it to have a reasonable chance of success,” Murphy says. “In other words, informed optimism. The hunters who have done their homework and keep a positive attitude, often after multiple dismal outings, are ultimately the most successful. Knowing from research that bucks often take a few days to complete loops within their home range, it’s not surprising that it takes persistence to increase the odds you will get an opportunity.”

Nathan Mrnak, COO of Base Camp Leasing, is a diehard deer hunter. He spends many days in the field each year. He stresses that it’s OK to move off your original plan and make a move.

“Picking out a stand location can be one of the toughest decisions while setting up on a mature whitetail,” he says. “I have had many first sits that ended with my target buck just out of bow range. Seeing his exact route provided the last bit of intel to moving a set just 50 yards to capitalize the next sit. This has worked time and time again in big timber.”

The most basic element of deer hunting is hunting deer where they are. To do that, you need current information.

Own the Season’s Art Helin knows this well. “Hunt where the bucks want to be during the correct phase of the season,” he says. “Food sources during early season, transition areas during the pre-rut (between field and bedding and staging areas), funnels and ridge edges during the rut (all day or mid-day sits especially), and back to food sources late season.”

During the rut, a lot of deer hunters choose to do all-day sits. Whitetail Properties Land Specialist Ben Harshyne does, too. However, he changes things up.

“I grew up hunting all-day sits in one stand,” he says. “Over time, I learned that deep timber and thick cover are action-packed in the morning and afternoon, yet often go cold by evening. In the afternoon, shift focus to hunting cover and edges near food sources or along travel lines leading to food. Not necessarily the actual food edge, but nearby. Does bed near food and still want to feed — and the bucks know it. Few stands are productive all day, so fine-tune your late-day move to target cover that relates to food.”

Big buck killer Jared Mills has arrowed a lot of big whitetails. He says don’t be afraid to be aggressive, but do it at the right time.

“I think most guys tend to be too conservative when hunting a target buck, staying on the fringes and only getting out when the conditions are perfect,” he says. “That’s fine if you have the entire season to hunt, and there’s little chance of him getting killed by a neighbor, but that’s a very rare scenario for most of us. While the windows of opportunity to encounter a target buck are already small enough, we make them even smaller by not getting in there after him. Be smart with your hunting, of course, but don’t sit around waiting for the absolute perfect time to move in, otherwise, you’ll likely miss a chance.”

Finally, just keep hunting. You won’t always kill a deer on opening day. It might just be closing day. So, keep hunting, and don’t give up.

“Have patience and persistence,” Helin concluded. “The entire season can change, and it can all happen in a short minute. Stay with it, and never give up, but also never get lazy. Always follow the same regimen from day No. 1 until the end.”
