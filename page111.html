Ali G: Who Iz I is available to stream with a Prime subscription, and as someone who grew up watching the original series over on HBO, I had to check it out. Unfortunately, it feels like this character hasn't aged appropriately with the times, and that's largely because of the plot.

The plot of the movie that recently hit the 2026 film schedule is that a broke Ali discovered a fax from decades ago that HBO actually wanted to renew Da Ali G Show for Season 3. He sets off to the United States in an attempt to reverse his fortunes, as he has a child on the way. Unfortunately, he faces obstacles from his past and tries to make up for past controversies. The problem is, the movie addresses so many things the wrong way.

Ali G: Who Iz I? runs with the narrative that people who dislike Sacha Baron Cohen's character believe that he's mocking the Black community. The movie then becomes a quest for the character to prove he's biracial, and for Nira, an alleged professor of African American studies, to prove he is not.

There are funny moments, as we wrote previously. The problem, however, is that Cohen once again leans on his exploitative style of subjecting unsuspecting subjects to anything from racial slurs to unexpected nudity. One particularly hard-to-watch scene is when Nira (Cohen in disguise) angers a group of BIPOC people by intentionally mispronouncing common phrases in Black culture.

It does little to combat criticisms lodged against Sacha Baron Cohen in recent years, in that he profits from making marginalized communities the butt of his jokes. While there are moments in which the comedian uses subjects' own biases and prejudices against them, he also intentionally riles up the people who may suffer the most from his stunts.

What's frustrating to me is that, from my perspective, Sacha Baron Cohen missed a golden opportunity to turn the lens back on Ali G fans. If the joke, as the movie suggests, was indeed that the character was a "white man acting Black," then wouldn't it have been a much more satisfying thing to suggest there was nothing ever wrong with the character in the first place?

It's strange to double down on Ali G when I felt Cohen made attempts to modernize the character of Borat in his sequel. Ali G: Who Iz I? almost felt like nothing had changed from when the show was canceled, including the character.

Maybe it shouldn't be surprising. Ali G: Indahouse wasn't a smash hit, so it shouldn't be a surprise that the follow-up didn't do well. I guess I was just hoping it would do better and give us something all to laugh about. Instead, I just kind of feel weird for watching and questioning whether I ever should have in the first place.

Others can watch and see if they have a different take. All I can say is that if I ever debated who the better Sacha Baron Cohen was between Ali G and Borat, I don't wonder that any longer.

‘Zi’ Review: Kogonada Rediscovers His Voice with a Microbudget Hong Kong Experiment Starring Michelle Mao and Haley Lu Richardson

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Editor’s Note: This review was originally published during the 2026 Sundance Film Festival. Grasshopper Film releases “Zi” in select theaters starting Friday, November 6.

A Hong Kong violinist who’s losing her grip on reality, young Zi (Michelle Mao) is haunted by visions of her future self. The doctors at the local neurology center suspect that she might be suffering from the early symptoms of a brain tumor, but Zi — a being of pure cinema, cobbled together from the half-formed memories of films like “La Jetée” and “Cléo from 5 to 7” — will have to wait a long and lonely 24 hours for the test results to come back.  Related Stories ‘To Hold a Mountain’ Took on the Government — Now It’s Montenegro’s Oscar Submission ‘Soul Patrol’ Review: The Vietnam War’s First Black Special Ops Team Shares Their Stories in An Enlightening Documentary

Well, maybe not so lonely. Crying on one of the innumerable concrete steps that climb Lantau Island, Zi is consoled by a sweet American expat with a strangely familiar face (and an aggressively unnatural blonde wig). Her name is L, she’s a disillusioned former dancer played by the luminous Haley Lu Richardson, and she can’t help but reciprocate the inexplicable closeness that Zi feels towards her. Over the course of a single night together, these two lost souls will help to relocate each other in time — to resynchronize with the world around them, reconnect with the infinite potential of the present, and return themselves to the creative purpose that once anchored them to it.

Even if seen without a shred of context, it would be obvious to anyone that “Zi” was made by someone who is — or hopefully was — desperate to spark a realignment of his own. To steal himself away from regret and anxiety and tether his perspective back to the now. Shot without a script or a budget in the immediate aftermath of Kogonada’s ill-received “A Big Bold Beautiful Journey” (the “After Yang” auteur’s studio debut, by all indications a soul-crushing experience for an artist whose two previous features retained the delicate grace of the video essays that he used to make), this searching and unformed wisp of an experiment hardly represents the first time that an ascendent director has scaled down after a misstep, but “Zi” takes the notion of “one for me” to much greater and more galvanizing extremes than usual.

This isn’t just a filmmaker trying to get back on track, it’s a filmmaker desperately, nakedly searching for a way to fall back in love with filmmaking itself. A way to prove that it might still love him back. (Kogonada described the project to IndieWire as “A retreat, as well as a pursuit.”)

While the prospect of watching Kogonada do that will naturally be of greater interest to those who have watched him get to this point, this movie’s palpably improvised nature — its stolen shots and recursive shape lend the impression of someone trying to cut their ultra-evocative vacation footage into a coherent narrative — would force anyone to wonder about the meta-textual aspects of its creation. “You feel like your sole purpose in life is something, and it doesn’t choose you back,” L sputters out at one point. “What do you do with that? I had to get away from it all. That’s why I came here. Wandering.”

Since arriving in Hong Kong, she’s made a habit of collecting sounds in much the same way as Kogonada has come there on the hunt for images. They both find a dissonance around Victoria Harbor that reflects their internal feelings of otherness and self-alienation. It’s China, but not. It’s a single city, but one split across two main areas by a stretch of the sea that some people never cross. It’s among the world’s most densely populated metropolises, but the vertical terrain seems fundamentally inhospitable to human civilization, and nature continues to assert itself between every skyscraper, shopping mansion, and crack in the sidewalk. Zi is the only native character in the film, but the history and design of Hong Kong reflects her disassociation as well — like her, the former British colony’s experience of the present is delayed.

That’s the closest thing to a diagnosis we get for what ails her, though it’s clear that Kogonada is leaning more towards soft science-fiction than the stuff of hard medicine. “Temoral relativism” is how L’s ex-fiancé Min (Jin Ha) describes it, though he’s slow to reveal how much he knows about Zi’s condition. Everything she perceives is already in the past. I suppose that’s true for all of us to some degree, if you factor in the nanoseconds that elapse between stimuli and our ability to perceive it, but the situation is more pronounced for Zi (who Mao plays with the effervescent helplessness of a paper airplane). Of course, that doesn’t explain why she believes she’s bearing witness to visions of her future self, but that confusion only exacerbates her feelings of distance from the present.

Even in its more platonic dimensions, the threadbare story that gradually allows Zi to close that gap is awash with the romance of loss, reconciliation, and belonging. Kogonada is falling back in love with the movies right before our eyes, and yet,  in characteristic fashion, that process isn’t garish or forced. His is a vision that is revealed through careful subtraction — by clearing away all of the dust and smudges that came to obscure it.

While Kogonada indulges in a few legible winks to his cinematic forebears (the most obvious of those being a step-printed homage to Wong Kar Wai, and my most far-fetched being a disoriented nod to José Luis Guerín’s “In the City of Sylvia”), he and cinematographer Benjamin Loeb almost completely eschew the neon exoticism that foreigners tend to look for in Hong Kong, with the vast majority of the film being captured in unfussy handheld shots that accentuate its here-and-now anxiety and delegate the emotional heavy lifting to an impeccably curated soundtrack of Ryuichi Sakamoto remixes and originals.

By the end, “Zi” engenders a certain emotional attachment toward and between its characters, all of it building to a gently unexpected gutpunch of a final minute. But even that last-minute reveal — not a plot twist so much as a sudden change in perspective — is rooted more in the movie’s form than its plot, as Kogonada’s characters remain largely symbolic of their function in the writer-director’s journey back to himself.

It’s a testament to Mao, Ha, and especially Richardson — who continues to be so good at grounding Kogonada’s most abstract ideas in a tender human place — that “Zi” retains even a soft dramatic pull as it searches for the present, though its recursive nature certainly wasn’t conceived with a casual audience in mind. If you’re hooked, which I wasn’t, or haunted by it, which I was, that will likely have less to do with an acute emotional connection to these characters than with the overflowing rewards of watching someone rediscover the sound of their own voice, and hear a way forward into the future in its echoes.

The Yangwang U9 Xtreme Just Set Two Guinness World Speed Records

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The electric Chinese supercar went more than 312 mph on a public road in the Abu Dhabi desert.

The fastest production car in the world. No, not necessarily an attribute that would be useful in your real life, unless your driveway is a section of unrestricted autobahn, but as an achievement, it's something automakers love to boast about. Porsche. Ferrari. Bugatti. And now Yangwang.

The luxury division of Chinese giant BYD (as Lexus is to Toyota), Yangwang's appropriately named U9 Xtreme hypercar just hit 312 mph on a public road in Abu Dhabi. This feat nets it two Guinness World Records at once: the single-direction highest speed for both a production car and for a production electric car.

At this point, perhaps the electric production speed record doesn't need its own subdivision, especially since the U9 was chiefly breaking its own previous record of 308 mph, set last year. The U9 Xtreme, or U9X, has four electric motors, one at each wheel, for a grand total of over 3000 hp. That's three Veyrons concentrated into a single car.

The battery architecture is also distinct from the standard U9, with a 1200-volt architecture and the ability to both send and receive huge amounts of power with greatly improved thermal efficiency. Even when nearly depleted, at 20 percent charge, it can still pump out 1800 kilowatts of power (2414 hp), or soak up 700 kilowatts through regenerative braking.

A motor at each corner means almost limitless fine-tuning for torque-vectoring control, meting out all that power with minimal wheelslip. The U9X also features electric dampers that clamp down on squat and dive under hard acceleration and braking, and active aerodynamic elements to maintain stability at speed. A tip of the hat also goes to Indonesia's Giti Tires, who developed the rubber capable of putting up with these tremendous speeds and forces.

Yangwang only plans to build thirty of these U9X models, and it's not like you can just slap on a license plate and bully 911 Turbos on the Autobahn. The 4.35-mile stretch of public road used for the record attempt was closed at the time, and also fastidiously cleaned of debris, including going back over the surface looking for any wires left by the street sweepers.

However, as a demonstration of just how advanced BYD's battery chemistry can be, this new speed record represents all kinds of bragging rights. Speed records come and go. For now, nothing can touch the Yangwang U9 Xtreme.

AI watchdog report indicates we may have less than 6 months before AI super intelligence arrives

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The estimate covers well-specified tasks; METR's judgment that AI drives most of the acceleration rests partly on redacted evidence.

Anthropic's AI models were likely improving on tasks with clear success criteria roughly twice as fast in October 2026 as they did between March 2024 and October 2025, according to a Nov. 5 assessment by METR, an independent model-evaluation group.

METR judges that AI assistance probably accounts for most of the recent acceleration. But Anthropic redacted much of the evidence behind that judgment, leaving outsiders with a clearer view of the faster benchmark trend than of what caused it.

The central published measurement uses AECI, Anthropic's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index. METR estimated that AECI's fitted growth rate after October 2025 was 1.9 times its earlier rate, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.5 to 2.8 times. The analysis includes snapshots of unreleased models, so it covers more than the products customers could access.

METR interprets that trend as roughly two years of historical benchmark progress achieved in the past year. The 1.9-times fit is distinct from its broader judgment of roughly twice the historical pace on well-specified tasks. That judgment carries substantial uncertainty, including whether the same acceleration extends to broader work.

Those limits matter because success on clean, easily graded tasks may translate unevenly into real-world usefulness. METR also cautions that an unusually slow historical baseline could make the acceleration appear larger. In the other direction, model results arrive after training, potentially concealing a more recent change in research speed.

METR says it confidently ruled out an acceleration in the growth of training compute as the explanation for the faster progress. The detailed evidence supporting that finding was redacted.

Beyond that exclusion, separating causes was less decisive. METR says non-AI factors likely played a significant role in the acceleration in late 2025, while judging that AI assistance now accounts for a majority of it.

The distinction also leaves people at the center of the most important training work. METR says AI was not yet making highly significant, fully autonomous contributions to those parts of Anthropic's model-training process. The reported speedup primarily involved researchers using AI assistance.

At the pace METR measured, six months could deliver roughly a year’s worth of progress on Anthropic’s benchmarks. The case for superintelligence within that window hinges on what the next models can do with those gains. If they can design training experiments, spot promising results, and help turn them into better models, each generation could shorten the time it takes to build the next.

For that to produce superintelligence within six months, the gains would have to carry over from tasks with clear answers to open-ended work where even experts have to figure out what to try next. The first sign might come from inside an AI lab, when a model runs an experiment its researchers hadn’t thought to try, and the result helps build a more capable successor.
