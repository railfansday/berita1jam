What is Ethereum actually for? A judge and Vitalik Buterin both left it open

the 16th of November, a federal judge acquitted Anton and James Peraire-Bueno in the “first of its kind” wire fraud prosecution over a $25 million Ethereum exploit. This judgement came about after the government failed to prove the brothers made any “material misrepresentation.”

The ruling, filed in the Southern District of New York (24-CR-293), came the same day as when Bitwise CEO Hunter Horsley pitched stablecoins as a backup for banks.

On a similar note, Ethereum [ETH] co-founder Vitalik Buterin argued that digital assets do not match up to investing in houses. These three developments, when taken together, compound the question of what crypto is actually for.

On the 2nd of April 2023, the brothers seemingly exploited a bug in the MEV-Boost relay software, due to which they could view transactions that sandwich traders believed were private.

The brothers’ alleged proposal for replacement transactions, in turn, resulted in massive losses of roughly $25 million within seconds.

Finally, on the 16th of November 2026, Judge Jessica G. L. Clarke, when granting an acquittal, wrote,

The question before the Court is not whether the Peraire-Buenos’ conduct was right or moral, or even whether it violated uncharged crimes.

It is simply whether the evidence at trial, construed in a light most favorable to the Government, could support wire fraud and related convictions in this case. Because the Government failed to prove that the Peraire-Buenos made any material misrepresentations in the execution of their scheme, the motion is granted.

The court analyzed the government’s theories – including the “lure transactions” and the so-called “false signature,” and found nothing that could support a wire fraud conviction.

A couple of days later, on the 18th of November, Horsley posted on X:

Rolling bank outages in the AI era – Consider having some stablecoins or crypto just in case.

The post came as thousands of Chase, Bank of America, and Wells Fargo customers reported being unable to access their accounts.

Vitalik Buterin, in a separate post on November 18, argued that younger generations have been investing in digital assets over real estate options; doing so, he noted, reduces –

Perverse incentives that drive people to push to keep housing expensive.

But he added a condition: housing had the advantage of being –

A culture and policy-encouraged default of investing in a safe non-crazy thing that you can’t easily liquidate.

Modern platforms encourage the opposite of this, leading to more irresponsible behavior… We need the equivalent of those good properties that housing investment has, for modern digital assets.

The three developments have been framing crypto’s developing role separately, but these assets have a long way to go before they can fully replace TradFi.

Vitalik Buterin himself leaves the question open, as he observed,

We need the equivalent of those good properties that housing investment has, for modern digital assets.

The ruling did not answer that question, nor did the outage.

If someone handed you $8,300 every second, what would you do? That’s how much pure profit Nvidia now makes. By the time you finish reading this paragraph, Nvidia will likely have cleared $45,000 and change.

It’s easy to look at Nvidia, a company that just lost its visionary founder CEO due to a series of scandals, and think that the company and maybe the entire AI industry are in terrible trouble. Perhaps so?

But I want you to understand: Nvidia isn’t your average semi-successful company that might tempt a new CEO to fix things that are broken. It is a company so staggeringly, mind-bogglingly successful it’ll do everything in its power not to rock the boat. There is so much money at stake.

It’s so much money that when I heard about Jensen Huang’s resignation, my first thought was: I need a way to visualize this jaw-dropping amount of money for you. I want to show you that Nvidia has risen so far, so fast, that rival chipmakers AMD, Intel, and Qualcomm barely qualify as competition now.

If you’re on a desktop OS, you could mouse over each dot in our chart above to see the staggering sums. But we built another way to visualize the rise of Nvidia I like even better. It’s a horizontal bar graph that not only puts Nvidia’s revenue growth in perspective, but shows you its monster profits right alongside them — plus the world events that made Nvidia a star.

TL;DR: The crypto boom and the great GPU shortage have nothing on the AI boom. Those earlier periods saw Nvidia’s profits briefly peak at $1.24 billion and $3 billion a quarter respectively, while the rise of data centers has driven them to $65 billion per quarter with no sign of stopping.

Yes, Jensen may have set a trillion dollars of Nvidia’s market cap on fire — from around $5 trillion at the start of November to “only” $3.6 trillion now — but the smoldering company is raking in $700 million in profit a day on average.

Put another way: Nvidia could give a $1 million bonus to every one of its 42,000 employees, every three months, without running out of cash. Or, you know, Nvidia could end world hunger or provide clean water for everyone, now that it’s making over $200 billion in pure profit a year.

Maybe that’d make people feel differently about data centers?

But even if we stick to self-serving capitalism, you’re probably still a happy investor if you bought Nvidia a decade ago. A $1,000 investment 10 years ago is now worth over $67,000. You’d have over $1 million if you invested $15,000 back then.

And make no mistake, Nvidia is sticking to capitalism. Nvidia interim CEO Colette Kress just promised to return as much money to investors as possible on the company’s November 17th earnings call. “Going forward, we intend to return all excess free cash flow net of strategic uses to shareholders,” she announced. “All” is a lot more than the 50 percent baseline that Jensen promised five months ago.

Nvidia isn’t the only company whose fortunes have risen this dramatically. RAMageddon revealed that just three companies — Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron — controlled nearly 90 percent of the entire global DRAM market, and those three have repeatedly signaled that they’d prefer to celebrate record profits instead of ramping up production too fast.

But while their revenues and profits have been skyrocketing even faster over the past year or so, even Big RAM hasn’t caught the AI wave quite the same way as Nvidia. Samsung, of course, doesn’t just make memory — its revenue numbers include phones, tablets, TVs, home appliances, chips, displays, camera sensors, audio, networking equipment, medical equipment, and more.

In the most recent quarter, Micron made $37.7 billion in profit, SK Hynix made $29.8 billion, and Samsung came in at around $55.3 billion, but they’re all shy of Nvidia’s $65.3 billion. Historically, RAM has also been a bit more cyclical: Both Micron and SK Hynix actually lost money in 2023 because they produced too much memory.

On its latest earnings call, the first since cofounder Jensen Huang’s resignation, interim CEO and CFO Colette Kress reinforced the idea that Nvidia expects the good times to keep on rolling.

Asked how things might change under new management, she suggested that they wouldn’t: “From a business perspective, it’s business as usual,” she said. She added that the company has secured the backing of customers and suppliers including TSMC, the all-important firm that actually fabricates Nvidia’s chips.

“Our momentum is real, our demand is accelerating, our supply is growing. Our customers and partners are with us, and employees are committed,” she claimed. “We’ve got this.”

Nvidia claims it’ll rake in even more money next quarter, with projected revenue of $130 billion. Profit may be proportionally smaller since gross margins are estimated to drop to 71–72 percent (compared to nearly 75 percent), but Kress said Nvidia intends to get them up as high as 73 percent next year.

In other words: This astounding gravy train isn’t going anywhere. At least that’s the plan.

GTA 6's Completely Unrealistic Mountains Had to Be in the Game, Rockstar Says

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GTA 6 takes some liberties with its world around Vice City

If you’ve ever spent time in and around Miami, Grand Theft Auto 6 is bound to delight you. Rockstar Games spared no expense in bringing the diverse world of Florida to life in digital form. Walk down the street in Vice City, and you’re sure to find an approximation of a bar you’ve actually been to. Head to the Leonida Keys, and you’ll find rows of houses on stilts. The attention to detail is impressive, but it also makes one odd detail about the map a little odd.

Go all the way north in Leonida, right up to Lummox County, and you’ll be able to go hiking and hunting in the mountains. As any Floridian will tell you, that’s not exactly reflective of the real Florida’s topography. Why take such a major creative liberty there when so much else about Leonida is grounded in realism? In an interview with Polygon, Rockstar North co-head Rob Nelson explained why GTA 6 takes players to the mountains.

Nelson said that one of the biggest challenges the team faced when designing the game was figuring out the map. Rockstar’s map team began working on the game in 2015, Nelson said, well before anyone else at the studio. While creating another city was easy enough for a team that has mastered that art, expanding to surrounding Florida locales was a tougher proposition. That’s because Florida is a very flat state.

The problem with depicting it as is, though, is that GTA players likely would have some expectations about what should be in a modern GTA map. GTA 5, for instance, built off of Los Angeles to create Los Santos. Because of that, the map has a lot of variety in elevation. That wouldn’t be possible if Leonida stuck too close to reality, so Rockstar ultimately decided to include some mountains to keep GTA 6 a little more consistent with GTA 5.

Though it may not be accurate, it’s a welcome addition to the map. As fun as it is to drive around Vice City, weaving around the same highways can get old. Miami artists’ Art Basel boycott campaign calls for alternatives to fair week’s commercial model

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Visitors to the 2025 edition of Art Basel Miami Beach interact with a sculpture by the collective MSCHF on the Perrotin stand Photo: Liliana Mora

As Art Basel Miami Beach prepares to return to the city, a group of local artists and organisers is asking the Miami art community to reconsider its relationship with the busy week of fairs that takes over the city every December. The grassroots collective Artists 4 Artists Miami announced plans for its “Art Basel Boycott” at a protest outside the Miami Beach Convention Center at the end of last year’s edition of the Switzerland-based fair. Now, after a year of preparation, the collective is launching its first campaign, inviting local artists, curators, gallerists, collectors and residents to reconsider their participation in Miami Art Week’s fairs while building alternatives rooted in the community.

In a city whose international cultural profile has become virtually inseparable from the Art Basel brand, the boycott poses a difficult question: can Miami’s arts economy sustain its local creative community without depending so heavily on the machinery of a global art fair? Oorganisers are not calling for a blanket rejection of Miami Art Week or even its tentpole event. Instead, organisers envision a longer-term effort to challenge the economic and political structures surrounding the fair and redirect attention toward local artists, independent galleries and experimental programming.

“We realise that in order to refuse to take part in something as intertwined and culturally impactful as Art Basel, you really have to start building an alternative,” says misael soto, an artist and founder of Artists 4 Artists Miami.

The campaign grew out of the collective’s broader efforts to connect cultural work with other grassroots struggles. After spending much of 2025 supporting campaigns led by other organisations, Artists 4 Artists Miami began considering how it could mobilise its own community. Its participation in protests organised by Jewish Voice for Peace and other groups opposing the war in Gaza helped crystallise the idea, soto says, prompting organisers to examine the connections between the art market, housing affordability, labour conditions, and the companies that benefit from the fair.

For soto, Art Basel has become a focal point for these overlapping concerns. The fair generates significant economic activity, but the group questions how much of that value reaches Miami’s working artists and residents, particularly as the city becomes increasingly expensive. The group also points to the environmental and infrastructural costs of hosting an international event, and to the political and corporate interests associated with some of its major partners and investors.

Those concerns extend to the fair’s relationship with the city itself; soto points to a recent Miami Beach Commission decision concerning Art Basel’s rental agreement for the Miami Beach Convention Center, which reduced the fair’s rent from nearly $1m to around $500,000 and committed the equivalent of an additional $300,000 in public funds toward first-responder and security costs. The group questions whether the benefits the fair brings to the community justify the public resources devoted to it.

And yet, as the Artists 4 Artists Miami organisers acknowledge, Art Basel is deeply embedded in Miami’s cultural identity and economy. For many artists and arts workers, Miami Art Week provides essential income through art handling, installation, production and other contract jobs, while offering a smaller number opportunities to exhibit their work. Local galleries rely on the annual influx of collectors, press and visitors, and cultural institutions benefit from the attention and potential donor relationships the week brings.

“Art Basel Miami Beach has been a committed partner to Miami Beach and its creative community for more than two decades, supporting local artists and organisations through cultural programming, educational initiatives and opportunities for artistic exchange,” a spokesperson for Art Basel tells The Art Newspaper. “The fair also generates significant economic activity across the region, benefiting local businesses and workers in a wide range of sectors.”

According to local officials, the 2025 edition of Art Basel Miami Beach generated nearly $60m in economic impact for the city of Miami Beach, and $570m in economic impact for Miami-Dade County. The fair has consistently included several South Florida galleries as exhibitors, and every year the city of Miami Beach acquires a work from the fair for its public art collection.

Ariel Lynch, an artist and educator who has lived in the city for four years, signed the Artists 4 Artists Miami pledge despite recognising the tensions inherent in the campaign. Lynch says the fair offers substantial economic benefits to established galleries and collectors while providing comparatively few exhibition opportunities for local artists.

“Miami deserves more than institutions like Art Basel Miami Beach,” Lynch says. She plans to spend Miami Art Week visiting local galleries, events and experimental exhibitions instead of attending the main fair, while introducing students to the city’s year-round art scene.

Still, Lynch acknowledges the difficult position facing those who depend on Art Basel for their livelihoods. For workers already struggling to make ends meet, declining paid work during Miami Art Week may not be financially feasible. That is a dilemma soto is also mindful of: the fairs have helped pay their bills and enabled them to travel internationally, but they believe the short-term benefits can obscure longer-term costs, including overwork, underappreciated labour and environmental strain.

The “Art Basel Boycott” pledge is designed to accommodate different levels of participation. Signatories must select at least one commitment: not attending Art Basel Miami Beach, not exhibiting work at the fair, not exhibiting during Miami Art Week or withholding their labour from the fair. They can also choose commitments aimed at supporting alternatives, allowing participants to assess what is realistic given their circumstances.

As of this writing, the campaign had collected 21 signatures through an online form hosted on the collective’s website. Organisers are contacting local cultural and political organisations, particularly grassroots groups, to seek endorsements and establish partnerships. One existing partner is Panamía, a local network connecting businesses, creatives and residents. Artists 4 Artists Miami is also exploring programming in conjunction with a Panamía gathering and art print fair planned for Miami Art Week.

Beyond events, the collective hopes to establish a mutual-support network that could help pledge signatories navigate the financial consequences of opting out of Miami Art Week. That would entail connecting artists and organisations with local resources, soto says, including food assistance and other forms of mutual aid.

For the boycott’s organisers and signatories, success will not necessarily be measured by a signature count or an immediate impact on the fair. Instead, they hope the campaign will encourage a gradual shift in how Miami’s cultural producers and workers understand their economic and political influence.

“If a year from now we see that much more alternative and locally focused programming happening outside of Art Week,” soto says, “that would be a huge victory.”
