Gap stock soars as retailer says Old Navy turnaround shows signs of improvement

📅 2026-11-19  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Gap Inc. on Thursday announced Ashley Kechter will be the next president and CEO of its Athleta brand, effective Dec. 14.

The retailer also said Old Navy's turnaround is showing signs of progress, sending shares up more than 12% in trading Friday.

Kechter comes to Gap from activewear retailer Vuori, where she was global president and oversaw the brand's global business strategy, marketing and financials. She was previously global brand president of Fabletics and began her career at Gap before that.

"I'm excited to return to Gap Inc. and partner with [Gap CEO Richard Dickson] and the team to strengthen our connection with customers and shape the brand's next chapter," Kechter said in a statement.

The announcement comes after Lululemon poached former Athleta CEO Maggie Gauger to be its chief product officer last month.

"Clearly, we're disappointed with Athleta's performance, and we recognize there's a lot of work ahead," Dickson told CNBC. "We had been in rebuild mode, but now we're really in turnaround mode."

Dickson said the company is focused on getting back to its fundamentals: fabrications, feature, form, function and fit. Dickson added that Kechter will be conducting a "comprehensive review" of the brand to reposition Athleta for future success.

While the execution of its strategy has thus far "fallen short," Dickson said this new approach will allow Athleta to fully realize its potential.

Gap also reported mixed fiscal third-quarter earnings on Thursday and posted flat overall comparable sales. Wall Street had been expecting a comparable sales increase of 1.3%, according to StreetAccount.

Net sales grew 2%, though demand softened in October and early November due to warmer weather, the company said. Store sales increased 1%, while online sales increased 3%.

Gap also said it's recorded a $420 million adjustment to cost of goods sold and a $21 million adjustment to interest income from tariff refunds so far this year.

The company lowered its full-year net sales outlook to be "roughly flat," down from previous guidance of up between 1% and 1.5%. Gap is also now expecting adjusted earnings per share of between $2.29 and $2.34, down from an earlier expectation of between $2.35 and $2.45 per share.

Dickson said in an interview that much of the lowered guidance was due to warmer weather in the first two weeks of the quarter, but he said Gap is expecting "meaningful acceleration" in shopping ahead of the peak holiday season.

"We're monitoring the trends, and we're going to remain agile as we respond to the changing business trends," Dickson said.

For the quarter, Gap reported net income of $311 million, or 86 cents per share, compared with $236 million, or 62 cents per share, the year prior. Sales rose slightly from $3.94 billion the year prior.

At Athleta, net sales were down 14% compared with last year, coming in at $220 million. Comparable sales for the banner sank 15%, which the company attributed to "limited traction" from efforts to modernize its merchandise and marketing. `

The company's namesake brand saw 9% comparable sales growth for the quarter, with net sales of $1.1 billion, marking an 11% increase compared with last year. Dickson said it was the brand's 12th consecutive quarter of positive comparable sales.

"Gap is really firing on all cylinders, and the consistency of the brand is really giving us confidence in the brand's ability to continue its momentum," Dickson said.

The Old Navy banner saw flat comparable sales for the quarter and net sales of $2.3 billion, up 1% compared with last year. It's the first earnings report since the brand, which has been experiencing sluggish sales, named Michael Francis as its new CEO last quarter. Francis began his role at Old Navy on Nov. 2.

Banana Republic saw its net sales sink 3% compared with last year, which the company attributed to delayed demand for cold-weather categories and a women's fall assortment that fell flat. Wall Street had been expecting growth of 1.5%, according to StreetAccount.

Dickson said the brand has since introduced new products and seen demand "rebound" as cooler weather has set in across the country.

"We've been encouraged by the early customer response, and we should see sequential improvement in the fourth quarter," he said. Fear of God Fall 2027 Ready-to-Wear

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

After spending almost a year with his design team in Paris, Jerry Lorenzo moved it back to Los Angeles, while continuing to develop Fear of God’s collections in Milan: “I just felt like—creatively, emotionally—I needed to be rooted in my city,” he said. The decision influenced TEN, his latest collection and the tenth numbered offering in thirteen years. “It doesn't have the cleanliness of Paris,” he acknowledged. The designer hadn't planned for the tenth collection to mark any particular milestone, but described its timing as “providential,” considering the spiritual significance of the number. Bringing together everything from tailoring to vintage-inspired sportswear, he left nothing out: Or, as he put it: “To say something that's unequivocally yours, you have to put all of you into it.”

There was no particular theme this season. Lorenzo cited everything from Keanu Reeves in flannel to Michael Jordan in a suit to Allen Iverson walking into a basketball game among the references that have long informed his work. Instead, the inspiration came from “knowing who we are,” he said. Having navigated what he described as a difficult year, he considers TEN one of his most emotional collections, shaped as much by setbacks as by successes. As a young brand, Fear of God had sometimes tried to “serve an industry,” rather than its own audience: “It's easy to get lost there,” Lorenzo said.

The tailoring was generous yet controlled: long black overcoats layered over grey waistcoats and wide trousers, cropped leather jackets paired with fluid suiting, and softly structured blazers with exaggerated shoulders. A belted black leather coat came with bright red gloves, while quilted leather jackets, faded denim, and washed workwear introduced more rugged textures. The palette moved between black, charcoal, olive, earthy browns and washed-out blue, punctuated by flashes of red. Alongside familiar wool-cashmere fabrics and American jersey, Lorenzo introduced velour, new leathers and “a lot of Japanese fabrics,” including denim and canvas. Bonded details and articulated sleeves added structure to the oversized silhouettes.

The American influence was particularly evident in Athletics, the category rooted in Lorenzo's personal approach to sportswear: “I don't ever put anything technical on,” he said of his gym wardrobe. The collection also introduces the Fear of God Trainer, the brand's first athletic shoe developed entirely in-house, with supercritical foam cushioning. Rather than relying on conspicuous branding, Lorenzo believes its identity is immediately recognizable through its design: “Shape and materials are undeniably ours.” After years of collaborations with sportswear partners, the experience brought less a lesson than a realization: “You kind of realize you could have done it on your own the whole time.”

Commerce approved sales of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies, Rep. Mast says

📅 2026-11-19  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The subagency of the Commerce Department that regulates and enforces exports approved the sale of more than 879,000 AI chips to Chinese companies including Alibaba and Tencent as of May 2026, according to documents released Thursday by the House Foreign Affairs Committee.

Some of those approvals, made by Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security, included sales to companies designated by the Department of Defense as "Chinese Military Companies," according to the documents. From December through May, companies had made 3.4 million requests for sales of U.S. AI chips to China, the committee reported, citing those made by Nvidia and Advanced Micro Devices.

"Over the past month, the administration's handling of chip export controls has put our national security and technology edge at risk. The Bureau of Industry and Security has failed to enforce the rules, and Congress must now conduct rigorous oversight of the administration's policies," Rep. Gregory Meeks, D-N.Y., the top Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, said at a meeting of the panel Thursday at which members voted to release the documents.

Spokespersons for Nvidia and AMD said their companies comply with U.S. export control laws.

Nvidia argued it was following the Commerce Department regulations that were in effect at the time.

A spokesperson from the Bureau of Industry and Security said it shared information with Congress on both approvals and denials of licenses for Nvidia's H200 and comparable chips. "Licensing decisions are the product of deliberation and consultation among multiple U.S. Government agencies," the spokesperson said.

"The review and licensing process spanned more than half a year, with approvals subject to strict conditions and limited volumes. Despite receiving some licenses, we have since stopped shipping H200s to China," Nvidia spokesperson John Rizzo told CNBC.

On Tuesday, Nvidia reported $2.4 billion in quarterly revenue from H200 and related chip sales in China. In total, customers based in China and Hong Kong made up about 9.5% of all revenue.

"AMD complies with applicable U.S. export control laws and regulations and maintains rigorous processes to ensure compliance across our global operations," AMD said through a spokesperson.

The approval of 879,000 AI chips is "not that many," Bernstein Senior Analyst Stacy Rasgon told CNBC, adding that this news is not surprising.

"It doesn't sound to me like they're approving everything they got willy nilly," Rasgon said of the Commerce Department. With 879,000 approvals out of 3.4 million requested AI chip sales, he added, "There was a process in place, and it sounds like they approved about one out of every four."

Despite a rapid succession of China export controls on AI chips that started under the Biden administration in 2022, Nvidia and AMD both re-engineered some chips to comply and be sold legally in China. And in December, Trump said Nvidia could ship H200s, its older generation Hopper chips, to "approved customers in China" if the U.S. got a 25% cut of sales.

The U.S. then licensed Nvidia H200 sales to Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and other Chinese companies, although sales were limited because Beijing didn't approve large-scale imports, chip analyst Patrick Moorhead of Moor Insights & Strategy told CNBC last week.

H200 sales numbers remained low due to those restrictions on China's end. Nvidia said H200 shipments to China made up less than 1% of data center revenue for the quarter that ended in July.

"In the grand context of the size of this market and how much they're shipping, it's a rounding error. It's tiny," Rasgon told CNBC.

The committee cited the Export Control Reform Act as justification for releasing the data. The panel said the law allows it to declassify and release export data if it "determines that the withholding of the following information is contrary to the national interest."

The license requests released by the Foreign Affairs Committee include requests to sell to major firms including ByteDance, Tesla (Shanghai) and Baidu.

The release of documents comes in the wake of Jensen Huang's resignation from the chip supplier Nvidia after news surfaced that he had several extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese spy that led to concerns about Nvidia chips being diverted to China.

In the weeks since, Republicans and Democrats in Congress have called for legislation to be passed that would impose stricter export controls on the sale of advanced AI chips to China and other adversaries.

"The license information we are disclosing today will help inform the American public about how BIS has handled licenses for Nvidia H200 and equivalent AMD chips to China. The public has the right to know how these decisions were made," Meeks said.

Mast said two of the firms listed in Nvidia license applications to BIS — Maginfra and New Matrix — are suspected "smuggling fronts." The New York Times reported in November that both companies had empty offices and had ties to companies under national security sanctions.

"Both are fake companies. If BIS approved the sale of these chips to these firms, obviously that is of tremendous concern to us — especially given that President Trump made clear last year that the sale of any AI chips to China will have very real national security conditions," Mast said at the Thursday meeting.

Maginfra and New Matrix did not reply to requests for comment.

Shares of AMD fell Thursday after the investment firm Hunterbrook Media issued a report alleging the company "has its own China problem." The report claims that "after the U.S. blacklisted Chinese server maker IEIT Systems, the company kept getting newer AMD firmware, which enables AMD chips to run in servers."

CNBC did not independently verify this information.

Fallout from the Huang scandal on Capitol Hill has intensified in the last week, as lawmakers question the degree to which the former head of Nvidia's relationship with the suspected Chinese spy could have jeopardized national security and American AI dominance.

Huang is due for a closed-door transcribed interview with the House Foreign Affairs panel and two other committees on Dec. 1. Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter is scheduled to appear before the panels Dec. 2.

Senate banking committee Chair Tim Scott, R-S.C., announced on CNBC's "Squawk Box" on Wednesday that Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had also agreed to testify.

Lutnick has come under fire over his relationship to Huang and for his failure to take action on Megaspeed, the Singaporean company where Alice Huang, with whom the former Nvidia CEO had an affair, was an executive until 2025. U.S. investigators suspect Megaspeed diverted Nvidia chips to China.

Amid that criticism, the Commerce Department on Monday issued an interim final rule to close a loophole that has allowed Chinese companies to lease Nvidia's advancing computing power from data centers in third countries.

The rule was "written by and at the direction of" the Treasury Department and the White House National Security Council, BIS said in a press release.

Uber, the popular ridesharing app, is testing rides for kids. Uber Kids will cater to youngsters ages 10 to 12, those awkward years before adolescence. The new program expands on the company’s other offering to underage passengers, Uber Teens, which launched in 2023 and provides rides to teenagers ages 13 to 17 in select cities.

The pilot program for Uber Kids starts in three US cities for a limited time: Austin, Texas; Nashville, Tennessee; and Madison, Wisconsin. Its expansion will depend on how well it performs in those regions. If successful, Uber Kids could bring a whole new influx of customers to the app, specifically parents and families needing help getting their kids from point A to point B.

On the surface, it feels like a questionable proposition, given all the potential harms and dangers to young people traveling alone with a stranger. But it seems like Uber took those concerns into account. The rideshare app has added a slew of mandatory requirements for both approved guardians and qualified drivers.

First off, kids won’t simply be able to order a ride for themselves. The adult user must add the child to their family profile and complete an onboarding session covering the service’s safety features. They’ll also need to set up PIN verification for every trip. The parent or guardian must be present at the pickup location and provide the driver with a note containing the drop-off instructions.

As any parent would likely wonder (myself included), what about the drivers? How are they vetted?

According to Uber, only the highest-rated drivers will be invited to take part in the program. They’ll need to pass thorough background checks and successfully complete an Uber Kids education curriculum. Moreover, they must agree to use live video streaming in their cars so that parents and guardians can watch the trip every step of the way.

Live videos of underage passengers bring up a bunch of potential red flags, but Uber states all recordings will be encrypted, and no driver will have access to the content. Furthermore, sensors and GPS data will track the ride if the trip is interrupted or veers off course.

Uber could face a tough time recruiting drivers for Uber Kids. Transporting minors adds extra responsibility and requires specific skills that many drivers may want to avoid. To sweeten the deal, Uber is offering eligible drivers a $20 bonus on their first ride and an extra $7 per trip after that.

An Uber spokesperson didn’t immediately reply to our request for comment.

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/consumers-believe-ai-will-be-key-making-healthcare-simpler-survey" hreflang="en">Consumers believe AI will be key in making healthcare simpler: survey</a>

📅 2026-11-19  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Consumers believe AI will be key in making healthcare simpler: survey" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Americans want a healthcare experience that's simpler and easier to understand, and artificial intelligence may be the key to getting there, a new survey shows.

The poll, conducted by the Harris Poll and commissioned by Elevance Health, found that 82% of American patients view at least some part of healthcare information as a "foreign language," and close to half (45%) said they made a healthcare decision they later regretted due to a lack of understanding.

The survey was conducted among 2,0001 adults in mid-July, and 74% said they have encountered a key barrier, such as understanding their health insurance or a medical bill. And only a quarter (27%) said they completely understand their own health coverage.

That said, most (79%) of those surveyed said they believe AI can help to improve the healthcare experience overall.

Ratnakar Lavu, chief digital information officer at Elevance Health, told Fierce in an email that because the tech has the ability to quickly surface and parse key data about a member or health need, it can serve as a central piece of the puzzle in making a more personalized experience.

"AI-enabled tools give us the ability to create a unique member context," he said. "Because of this digital technology, we can now truly 'know the person' and proactively work with them to help them manage their healthcare needs."

For example, 82% of those surveyed said they would like to receive answers about healthcare as quickly as they can use an app to order a food delivery, and 83% would like to easily access recommendations that pull from their health coverage, individual health needs and financial situation.

Seventy-two percent said they would trust AI for accuracy on at least one type of information, such as an explanation of benefits, cost estimates or details on medication.

However, while many Americans believe AI can play a leading role in driving a better health experience, just 20% said they currently use the tech regularly as a source of information. And while 72% said they trusted the tech to surface key health information, 33% said they would trust an AI platform to translate their insurance benefits, and 30% said they trust the tech to estimate care costs.

Just 13% said they would trust AI to make diagnoses.

Lavu said that healthcare organizations looking to roll out new tools need to avoid simply embedding AI into programs for the sake of it, and be transparent about both the use case as well as the role of the human in the loop.

The study also found that women were less likely than men to trust AI, and younger generations were more likely to use it as a source of healthcare information than older groups.

"We have to implement AI at the speed of trust, which means being thoughtful about where AI adds value, not just delivering technology for technology’s sake," he said. "Trust is also earned when AI-enabled programs truly make things easier for the consumer. "

For example, Elevance Health has deployed an AI-powered program to support utilization management reviews and help drive faster approvals where possible, though AI is not used to make claims denials. It is also leaning on AI for more proactive member engagement initiatives through its digital channels, identifying individuals that would benefit from extra support or who may face gaps in care.

"We start by listening to members and identifying where healthcare is creating the most confusion or friction," Lavu said. "From there, we determine the right solution, which may or may not involve AI. When AI is the right tool, it should fit naturally into familiar interactions and make it easier for members to get an answer or take the next step."

<a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/health-tech/hlth-2026-elsevier-upgrades-clinicalkey-ai-full-patient-ehr-context" hreflang="en">HLTH 2026: Elsevier upgrades ClinicalKey AI with full patient EHR context</a>

📅 2026-11-19  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
HLTH 2026: Elsevier upgrades ClinicalKey AI with full patient EHR context" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Elsevier unveiled this week a major upgrade to ClinicalKey AI, enabling the AI-powered clinical decision support tool to access a patient’s full electronic health record and evaluate treatment plans against the company’s evidence-based medical library.

As AI advances, Elsevier has strategically shifted from a traditional academic publisher—it’s one of the world's largest publishers of scientific, technical and medical content—to an AI-driven analytics information company, incorporating generative and agentic AI into its healthcare and academic research.

ClinicalKey AI is a HIPAA-ready point-of-care decision support tool that draws answers from full-text articles from approximately 1,000 world-leading journals. Elsevier’s upgraded ClinicalKey AI is already deployed at more than 1,200 hospitals, and the company says the solution integrates with all electronic health record providers. Elsevier recently struck a content agreement with the JAMA Network that brings full-text content and visuals from the JAMA Network Family of Journals to ClinicalKey AI.

The company has rapidly expanded its AI capabilities and features, including ClinicalKey Nursing AI, an AI-powered tool built specifically for nurses and nursing workflows. Elsevier has also developed specialized AI assistants, such as LeapSpace, an AI-assisted workspace for researchers that’s built on 15+ million peer-reviewed articles and book content and 100 million Scopus records, according to the company.

Three months ago, the company rolled out its "Ask AI" feature across more than 1,500 U.S. hospital sites using its flagship ClinicalKey platform. With the generative AI feature, clinicians can ask conversational clinical questions and receive direct, cited answers drawn from evidence-based sources, including premium journals and content from leading medical organizations. Unlike general-purpose chatbots, Ask AI does not pull from the open web and does not store, process or retain patient-identifiable information, according to the company.

In June, the company acquired Wellsheet, a health technology company that aggregates and synthesizes patient chart data—including notes, labs, imaging and medication orders—from EHRs to provide relevant clinical intelligence at the bedside. The integration of Wellsheet's patient-chart analysis enhances Elsevier’s clinical decision support with context-aware AI, according to executives.

“The problem we’re trying to solve here is that physicians are challenged by so many things when they’re taking care of patients; top among them is patient complexity. Patients have more and more conditions, and they’re taking more and more medications. It can be hard to really understand the full picture and determine how to proceed,” Dr. Claudine Lott, physician executive for clinical solutions at Elsevier, told Fierce Healthcare during the HLTH conference this week.

“Our original ClinicalKey AI tool has been a place where the clinician can go, and they can ask a question, and it searches this curated database of evidence-based content we’ve given it and gives them an answer. But there was a bit of a missing piece in that it wasn’t capturing that patient context. If you wanted to get an answer, that’s really tailored to a patient, it was limited to what you can type in,” she said.

She added, “Wellsheet has 10 years of experience with looking at the entirety of the patient chart and helping to surface that to the clinician.”

There is a growing market for AI-powered clinical decision support products, but Elsevier contends its advantage lies in its depth of patient context and its large, curated content base, with a wholly owned, large proprietary library of peer-reviewed STM literature, including content from millions of articles, journals and books.

San Juan Regional Medical Center in Farmington, New Mexico, is using ClinicalKey AI’s new patient context functionality to provide quality care with fewer resources. With smaller clinical and quality teams than large academic health systems, and in a state where nearly 40% of residents rely on Medicaid, the medical center, which serves the Four Corners area, has found this support especially valuable, according to Carlo Hallak, M.D., physician executive, information services, at San Juan Regional Medical Center.

A non-profit, community-owned acute care hospital and Level III trauma center, San Juan Regional Medical Center operates outpatient and specialty clinics alongside its main hospital campus, with associated facilities such as San Juan Regional Rehabilitation Hospital and San Juan Health Partners clinics.

Hallak emphasized that Elsevier is a primary source of medical knowledge rather than a “wrapper” for other AI platforms, and that was a key distinction for the organization, Hallak noted. And ClinicalKey AI is designed for the entire care team. “The hospital is run by nurses, mid-level providers, other caregiver teams,” he told Fierce Healthcare at the HLTH conference.

The medical center aims to move clinicians to enterprise LLMs integrated with the electronic health record that can provide the full patient context, Hallak said.

"We're already seeing how ClinicalKey AI can be instrumental at the bedside and help improve care for our patients. As a rural healthcare center, we are proud to say that by using ClinicalKey AI to check each patient’s care against the same evidence base that major health systems rely on, we’re able to deliver the standard of care patients expect in any major city or leading academic center," Hallak said.

He added, "ClinicalKey AI reads the whole chart, checks it against the evidence and shows exactly where each finding comes from. That support is invaluable for a hospital like ours, and we’re already seeing strong adoption of ClinicalKey AI among our clinicians."

Hallak cited an example of a physician who used ClinicalKey AI to correctly identify viral meningitis, preventing an aggressive and unnecessary treatment course for bacterial meningitis.

ClinicalKey AI supports the physician’s judgment rather than replacing it, helping the clinician get to relevant evidence faster, he said, noting that, in the case of the viral meningitis patient, the physician’s clinical judgment was critical in recognizing that something was unusual and then consulting colleagues. The AI tool then helped accelerate the next step by quickly surfacing information the clinician otherwise would have found by searching the medical literature.

Physicians also use the tool during weekly complex case discussions to quickly review trending medications, images and test results in one summarized sheet, he noted.

“It’s going to expedite a lot of the work they do when it comes to these complex cases,” he noted.

The AI solution can quickly summarize a complex patient history at the point of care, and this can help care teams in the emergency department understand a frequent ED patient, Hallak said.

One patient has visited their emergency department 95 times in less than a year, in addition to making numerous visits to neighboring health systems, Hallak said. When that patient arrives, clinicians who aren't familiar with him would traditionally have to dig through the chart to understand his history. With Elsevier's ClinicalKey AI tool, they can ask for a summary of the patient's recent visits and care and get that information within seconds, he noted.

Because patients who frequently visit hospital EDs typically receive care at multiple organizations, San Juan Regional Medical Center is also working with other local health systems on interoperability efforts so clinicians can get a more complete picture of what happened during the patient's visits across different facilities.

As a rural hospital with staffing constraints, technology is essential to filling workforce gaps and supporting care delivery, Hallak contends.

"We don't have enough physicians, enough nurses, enough transporters, enough caregivers. We will never be able to have enough of those," he said.

Hallak envisions ClinicalKey AI will be particularly valuable for discharging patients from the hospital faster.

Generative AI tools can help speed up patient discharges by identifying barriers, assessing patients’ care needs and surfacing relevant information from both structured and unstructured data. Because the platform is designed for the broader care team, case managers and physicians can access the same information, while push notifications can alert clinicians when test results are available so they can act quickly, Hallak said.

"From an operational perspective, it allows for this care team coordination of care, and also from a physician perspective, it provides the context whenever there's something hanging and they're trying to understand more what's happening with the patient['s] discharge. You could be a hospitalist, and you've been off shift for like seven days, and then you come to the hospital and now the patient is on their tenth day, but you inherit that discharge. Going through, reading every progress note for every single day, reading every consult for every subspecialty that looked at this patient usually would take two to three days to actually get the patient out of the building safely," he said.

Rather than manually compiling information for a discharge summary, clinicians can use ClinicalKey AI to aggregate relevant information from across the patient’s record, trace it back to its source and review the AI-generated summary before signing off on the discharge, according to Hallak.

"It is a game changer, in my opinion. One of our hospitals said it used to take me three days to discharge a patient; now it takes me less than three hours," he said.

Going forward, the system expects to measure operational outcomes such as length of stay, safe discharge and multidisciplinary-rounds use, according to Hallak. The medical center also plans to evaluate clinicians' use of the tool and how it changes care decisions.

The medical center has also deployed other AI tools such as Oracle Health's clinical AI agent for ambient documentation.

"These things are crucial. We're hoping to bring in a clinical AI agent for nurses as well to help the whole care team be able to eliminate the noise, the friction that typically EHRs or IT would bring to the conversation," Hallak said.

The medical center is taking a risk-based approach to AI governance, using source verification and peer review for AI-generated summaries while applying greater scrutiny to tools that are predictive or suggestive, Hallak noted. That oversight involves clinical experts as well as compliance, legal and cybersecurity teams along with traditional vendor security and product-vetting requirements.

AI governance should become stricter as the technology moves from summarizing information to recommending or taking actions, he said.

Elsevier designed ClinicalKey AI with security and trust in mind, including HIPAA compliance, citations that show where information comes from and safeguards that keep the tool from prescribing what clinicians should do. Instead, it surfaces relevant information to help clinicians make their own decisions, Lott said.

"Health systems tell us two things stand in the way of adopting AI: tools that don’t work with their existing EHR partners, and concerns about security, compliance and responsible use," Omry Bigger, president of Clinical Solutions at Elsevier, said. "ClinicalKey AI was designed to address both. As an EHR-supported and HIPAA-compliant ready solution, ClinicalKey AI integrates seamlessly across health systems to provide clinicians with trusted evidence and patient context that gives them tailored clinical insights for each patient at the point of care."

Switching GLP-1 medications or undergoing bariatric surgery may help people maintain greater weight loss than stopping treatment entirely, according to a new study presented at ObesityWeek and published in eClinicalMedicine on November 14.

The Cleveland Clinic study compared five strategies for managing weight after treatment with GLP-1 medications, analyzing data from 20,486 adults with obesity or type 2 diabetes.

People who stop taking GLP-1 medications often regain weight. Researchers examined whether continuing treatment, switching medications, or undergoing bariatric surgery could help limit weight regain or support further weight loss.

The findings showed that people who underwent bariatric surgery or switched between semaglutide (Ozempic) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) experienced the greatest overall weight loss.

The study participants had an average age of 56.7 years, an average weight of 109 kg (240.3 lbs), and an average body mass index (BMI) of 38. All had been taking either semaglutide or tirzepatide for at least 90 days.

After 2 years, those who underwent bariatric surgery saw an average loss of 28.2% of their body weight or about 31.5 kg (69.4 lbs).

“For patients with severe obesity (typically a BMI of 35 or higher, or 30+ with metabolic conditions like type 2 diabetes), bariatric surgery remains the most durable, cost-effective intervention available,” he added. “Using a GLP-1 beforehand can safely reduce liver volume and lower surgical risk, while surgery provides the permanent anatomical and hormonal reset needed to maintain that weight loss long-term without relying indefinitely on weekly injections.”

A Florida federal court has tossed Tampa General Hospital's lawsuit against Eli Lilly over the 340B drug discount program.

Florida's Middle District court dismissed the case on a motion from the drugmaker on Wednesday. If Tampa General Hospital wants to file an amended complaint, it must do so within 30 days.

The hospital initially sued the pharmaceutical company over a policy it implemented that requires providers to submit claims-level data on pharmacy and medical dispensing if they participate in 340B. Tampa General said the decision is "unconscionable and immoral" in the initial complaint, filed in July.
