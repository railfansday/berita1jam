Suzanne Farwell Bets on a Big-Screen Comeback for the Rom-Com With ‘Otherwise Engaged’

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The longtime Nancy Meyers collaborator is preparing to make her directorial debut with the buzzy AFM project, which co-stars Halle Berry, Zoë Kravitz, Colin Firth and Jack Whitehall.

Suzanne Farwell couldn’t have asked for a better education in the creation of the contemporary romantic comedy. As a longtime producing partner to Nancy Meyers, she had a front-row seat during the development and production of some of the genre’s most indelible hits of the past quarter century — Something’s Gotta Give (2003), The Holiday (2006), It’s Complicated (2009) and The Intern (2015). Now, after more than 25 years in the business, Farwell is preparing to make her own carefully plotted first attempt at the increasingly elusive rom-com breakthrough as a writer and director.“I love producing, but from working with Nancy all those years, I absolutely knew that I wanted to direct,” Farwell says. “I don’t know that I’ll ever be able to achieve what Nancy has, but I certainly aspire to.”As a first-time feature director but longtime student of the genre, Farwell has assembled precisely the sort of premise and potently lovable cast that could spell rom-com magic.The project also amounts to a bet on an audience Farwell believes Hollywood is underserving: adults who still want to see an original romantic comedy in a theater. “It’s hard to do, to get the marketing spend, to get people’s attention,” she says. But with recognizable stars and an escapist script built around them, she believes there’s still room to break through.Otherwise Engaged stars English comedian Jack Whitehall as Charlie, a New Yorker whose plans to propose to Gabby (Zoë Kravitz) are upended when his estranged father, fallen business tycoon Jasper (Colin Firth), turns up at his door after an early release from prison. When Jasper falls for Diana (Halle Berry), Gabby’s fiercely independent mother, their whirlwind romance threatens to throw the younger couple’s wedding plans into chaos.Farwell co-wrote the screenplay with Gabriel Mizrahi and is producing alongside Susan Cartsonis for Resonate Entertainment, with Laura Rister producing for Esme Grace Pictures. Farwell co-founded Resonate in 2016 with Cartsonis and Brent Emery, her producing partners on the indie feature Carrie Pilby, with a focus on commercial films for female audiences and a commitment to representation and inclusion.Mister Smith Entertainment launched international sales on Otherwise Engaged at the Toronto International Film Festival in September and has brought the project to AFM. CAA Media Finance is handling U.S. rights. Production is slated to begin on the streets of New York in spring 2027.Ahead of AFM, The Hollywood Reporter connected with Farwell to discuss the creative origins of Otherwise Engaged, the current state of the romantic comedy in a consolidating Hollywood and how her project will reveal a fun new side of rom-com mainstay Colin Firth.Where did the inspiration for this project come from?It really started with Jack Whitehall. I had met with Jack on another project, and that project ended up not going. But I came away from meeting with him and getting to know his work a little more intimately just really enamored of him and his energy and his physical comedy and self-deprecating humor. He fills stadiums, and I could see why. And I just felt inspired to do something with him. So, I went to my writing partner, Gabe Mizrahi, who was also a big fan of Jack’s, and we ended up building the story around him and came up with the premise and the story for Otherwise Engaged. So, we built the movie around him, but then to get Colin Firth to play his father and Halle Berry to play his future mother-in-law and Zoë Kravitz to play opposite him — it’s literally the exact cast that we had in mind when we wrote the script. So to have all these actors on board — and two Oscar winners — it’s a little bit surreal and incredible, and I am very well aware of that and feel really fortunate. We very much had his physical comedy and the self-deprecating thing that he does. And Colin Firth is one of my favorite actors of all time. And I guess with just the way the business has changed, the only movies that are in movie theaters are generally superhero movies or tentpoles or horror. It’s a lot about second chances, starting over again in midlife. I’m very interested in that. I’m in midlife.I feel you.Whether it’s professionally, romantically, whatever that is. And then having that exist and complicate matters with a young couple who are just starting out in their lives and have every opportunity ahead of them, I think, is a very interesting combination. You have a lot of different perspectives, and it’s a lot of fun.It leaves a lot of room for comedy and for real heart — the father-son relationship between Colin’s character and Jack’s character, the mother-daughter relationship between Halle Berry and Zoë. And when they do the thing that they’re not supposed to do, which is get together, just watching the explosive consequences of that — I think it will be a blast.You’ve had a very distinguished career as a producer. What was it about this moment, or this project, that made you finally want to write and direct?After The Intern, I produced my first independent film called Carrie Pilby, with Bel Powley, Nathan Lane and Gabriel Byrne. And it started to really become real for me, thinking about directing. And I thought, “If I wait to have a script sent to me, it’ll never happen.” So I thought, “Why not me?” So I partnered with my writing partner, Gabe Mizrahi, and we just had this sort of instant simpatico kind of connection and wrote two films. This is one of them. So I don’t know if that answers your question, but I just thought, “I’m not a first-time filmmaker. I’ve made a lot of films.” And my experience alongside Nancy — I mean, there’s no better training. She’s sort of singular. So there’s no better training than to learn from the best and be inspired to plunge in. She’s been a great mentor and a great supporter of me, and just the way that she trusted me through our working relationship for 18 years. And watching her process and how fiercely she defends her films has always been very inspiring.It really sounds like this project is a culmination of a lot of ambitions for you. How are you feeling about it now that you’re pretty close to production?I’m really excited. I think there would be something wrong if I wasn’t also a little nervous. I think there’s an amount of nerves that are appropriate here. But I think it’s because I’m so excited, and I know the script inside and out. I can’t wait to get going. It feels like a great gift to have this cast, and I have amazing producers supporting me. I’m going to surround myself with incredible heads of department. And yeah, I can’t wait to make the movie. I want to see this movie.

TikTok Shop testing $10 Amazon Prime-style subscription service for shoppers

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TikTok Shop is testing a new paid subscription service that offers shoppers exclusive discounts, free shipping, and other benefits similar to Amazon Prime.

The new membership, called TikTok Shop Plus, is being introduced to select customers in the US as the social media platform prepares for the holiday shopping season.

TikTok Shop launched in the US in 2023 and has since expanded its presence in online retail. The subscription pilot comes as the company looks to attract more shoppers and compete with established retailers like Amazon and Walmart.

TikTok Shop began offering its new membership program over the weekend to more than 1 million US customers identified as some of its most loyal shoppers.

The service costs $9.99 per month following a 30-day free trial, making it cheaper than Amazon Prime’s $14.99 monthly subscription and Walmart+’s $12.95 monthly membership.

Members also receive three coupons each month, including $5 off with no minimum purchase, $10 off orders of $59 or more, and $30 off purchases totaling at least $119.

“TikTok Shop Plus is currently being piloted with an expanding group of eligible U.S. users,” Patrick Nommensen, president of strategic initiatives at TikTok Shop, told Modern Retail.

She Trained Through Alaskan Winters to Run a Sub-3 Marathon. These Strategies Helped Her Fight the Cold and Stay Consistent.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Snow, ice, and short days didn’t stop Michelle Baxter from regular runs outdoors.

We earn a commission for products purchased through some links in this article. Why Trust Us?

When winter arrives, it’s tempting to put your running goals on hold until the temperatures warm up, the days get longer, and the roads clear. Cold temperatures make it harder to leave a warm bed, while snow and ice can turn a familiar route into a much slower—and more slippery—run.

For months at a time, Michelle Baxter’s running routes in Anchorage, Alaska, were covered in packed snow and ice. After a snowstorm, plows could take days to reach every neighborhood, and by then, cars had compressed the fallen snow onto the roads. Even after the plows came through, the slick surface often remained.

Baxter—now an RRCA- and V.02-certified run coach—spent 15 years living in Alaska and training through winters like these, figuring out how to keep running through harsh, unmotivating conditions.

She trained for 12 marathons in that time, continuing to build fitness year after year. Eventually, she even achieved her most ambitious running goal: breaking three hours in the marathon. After eight years of attempting to break the barrier—and a heartbreakingly close 3:01:14 at Humpy’s Marathon in Anchorage in August 2021—Baxter finally ran a 2:58:24 at the California International Marathon in December 2021. She has since lowered her PR to 2:56:57 at the Indianapolis Monumental Marathon.

Your winter running routes may not be covered in snow and ice for six months straight, but chances are you’ll face your own challenges as the temperature drops in the coming weeks and months. The good news: Baxter’s experience can help you navigate tough terrain and make the most of your training.

Here’s how you can put her lessons to work this winter so when the ice thaws, you’re ready to run your fastest race yet.

What you wear can make or break a winter run, Baxter says. When you’re too cold, too warm, or worried about taking a spill around every tight corner, it’s hard to focus on any other aspect of the run. Baxter spent years figuring out what works, and her gear advice starts with two essentials: finding reliable footwear and appropriate layers for the conditions.

In Anchorage, finding reliable footing was Baxter’s biggest winter challenge. She initially resisted buying studded shoes, assuming they wouldn’t make much difference. But once she tried them, she was surprised by how much more confident she felt in every step.

For packed snow, Baxter often wore regular trainers fitted with screws in the outsoles, which she added herself.

On sheer ice, Baxter preferred shoes made with built-in carbide studs; she swears by the Icebug brand. “You can wear these shoes and make a 90-degree turn and feel so confident running on ice—like sheer ice,” Baxter says. “They were a game-changer for me.”

You may not encounter sheets of ice on your regular running routes, but consider the conditions you encounter most often and look for an option suited to them.

Now living in Minnesota, Baxter says she reaches for studded shoes if she expects at least half of a run to cover potentially snowy or icy ground. She notes that while the shoes may be slightly less comfortable than your normal trainers, protecting your body from harm is the number-one priority during the winter months of training.

“I just think it’s worth it to feel confident running on the slippery surfaces and not risk injuring yourself,” she says.

One of the most common winter gear mistakes Baxter sees runners make is overdressing. It’s easy to look out the window, see cold weather, and pull on every layer you own in an effort to stay warm on the run. But remember you’ll warm up once you start moving.

Baxter’s rule of thumb echoes the advice of other run coaches: Start your run feeling a little chilly. After about 10 minutes, you should warm up and feel comfortable for the rest of the run.

Don’t forget to consider the wind, too; Baxter generally recommends dressing for the wind chill temperature. She recalls learning this lesson during one particularly windy Alaska run when she dressed for the ambient temperature rather than the wind chill temperature and ended up feeling sick from potential hypothermia.

If you’re unsure where to start when it comes to gearing up for a winter run, use our temperature-based layering guide, then pay attention to how you feel after each run. Were you still cold after warming up? Did you get sweaty and uncomfortable? Use your responses to adjust your layers on your next run.

For Baxter, keeping her legs warm as temperatures approached subzero was a recurring problem. She first turned to wearing multiple pairs of tights, which helped with the cold, but the layers restricted her movement. Eventually, she found a thick, fleece-lined pair of running tights that solved the problem, recommending them to other runners for temperatures ranging from 15 all the way down to minus 10 degrees Fahrenheit.

She made a similar change on top: Instead of wearing a base layer, fleece layer, and wind layer, she found a high-quality warm jacket to wear over a base layer.

Your biggest frustration might be cold hands or wet feet. For these issues, Baxter suggests putting hand warmers in your gloves, running-specific wool socks, and a waterproof shoe.

For visibility during the winter months, Baxter wears a lighted vest. She began treating it as a non-negotiable in her running wardrobe after noticing, as a driver, how much easier it was to see runners wearing blinking lights rather than simple reflective clothing items.

“Investing in a few quality pieces [of winter gear], I think you’re just going to enjoy your run more,” Baxter says. “You’re going to be safer and more comfortable.”

Even with the right layers, leaving your warm house and getting out the door often proves difficult when it’s cold and dark.

Baxter understands the struggle better than most. Anchorage gets less than six hours of daylight during the darkest winter months, and the city’s winter high temps hover around 25 degrees Fahrenheit.

So, how did she keep getting out there? “Running just became a habit of mine,” she says. “It’s how I start my day. I get up, I go for a run.” Even during peak winter in Alaska, Baxter often ran seven days a week simply because it was a part of her daily routine.

Baxter says each individual needs to find what helps them keep going, whether that’s a future goal, a training partner to run alongside, or a plan that lets you check off workouts.

For Baxter, her motivation and discipline to make running a habit often came from looking ahead.

“I just know how important those base miles are during the winter,” she says. “So I know I need to keep putting in the work, keep doing some workouts, because it’s only going to pay off down the road.”

If you have a spring marathon, a summer 10K, or another goal in mind, remind yourself how your winter routine fits into that bigger picture. You can also adjust your expectations for the season: Baxter says it’s okay to scale back during winter if that’s what works for you. A simple maintenance routine can help you arrive at your next training cycle ready to build.

The point is, doing something is better than doing nothing.

A goal months away isn’t always enough to get you out the door on a cold morning. Baxter suggests finding something closer to look forward to, too.

Consider signing up for a winter race, even if it isn’t one of your main events for the year. A Turkey Trot, holiday run, or another low-pressure race with friends can provide a checkpoint and make winter training more fun.

Soon after moving to Anchorage, Baxter joined a training group that held workouts at The Dome, an indoor athletic facility featuring an indoor track. The group helped her settle into the running community while giving her a reason to show up for a workout each week.

Once she developed those friendships, other runners expected her to show up, and those weekly track workouts were no longer optional.

If you’re looking for a similar boost, check for a local run club or training group. A standing workout appointment can give your week structure, while supportive company can make a difficult session feel less daunting.

Baxter generally followed the workouts on her schedule, but she changed where she ran to make them possible. The day after a substantial snowfall, she would head to the treadmill rather than plodding through fresh snow, for example. She also took many of her harder workouts to the treadmill or indoor track, where she felt more comfortable running fast.

Her easy and long runs, meanwhile, almost always happened outside.

You can take the same approach by considering the purpose of the day’s run. If you have a faster workout planned and the outdoors only offers slippery conditions, an indoor option may help you complete it with more confidence. If you’re running easy outside, give yourself room to slow down when conditions make your usual pace unrealistic.

Most of all, remember that one winter is part of a much longer running journey. Baxter’s progress—and eventual sub-three breakthrough—came from conquering multiple training cycles and years of running. The work you do this winter can be another step in that process.

“You simply can’t deny the power of consistently stacking marathon block upon marathon block or just years and years and years of running,” Baxter says. “Endurance running is a long game.”

Stop Hunting for Pricey Antiques—Designers Say These 9 Humble Finds Give a Home So Much Character

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

They’re affordable, widely available, and stylish.

It’s any avid thrifter’s dream to stumble upon an extremely valuable piece of decor while browsing the shelves of their favorite antique store. But while you’re hunting for rare treasures, don’t discount the less-expensive finds—they can be the secret to a home that looks instantly filled with character.

“To me, a collected home is much more interesting when everything isn’t perfect or chosen for its monetary value,” designer Lauren Sullivan of Well by Design tells House Beautiful. “Often, it’s the less precious pieces that give a room its personality.”

Even as older generations are continuing to downsize and offload unwanted things to local thrift stores, it still might be hard to find the diamonds in the rough. Keep reading to find out what Sullivan, and a handful of other designers, say you should be on the lookout for on your next thrifting visit.

The most valuable antiques are the ones that have been passed down through generations. In Sullivan’s own bathroom, the designer keeps her great-grandmother’s vanity set on the countertop, its aged finish providing beautiful contrast with the more contemporary marble and finishes. In her bedroom, the same great-grandmother’s small bedside clock found a place on Sullivan’s nightstand.

“Neither is particularly valuable monetarily, but I love incorporating pieces that have been passed down through generations into a home filled with both old and new,” Sullivan says.

Designers love incorporating artwork into homes to add personality and dimension, but Sullivan, as well as designer Becca Meyer of BB Meyer Design, believe that antique shops are where you can really find some hidden gems.

“Some of my favorite pieces of art have come from flea markets or antique shops for very little money,” Meyer says. “I much prefer these to mass produced prints.” You’re more likely to find pieces with aged textures at these types of shops, and that patinaed quality creates an entirely unique look.

Sullivan explains that she’s actually particularly drawn to prints, as well as smaller paintings and silhouettes. She even has an inexpensive print of The Skating Minister in a vintage frame in one of her guest rooms, pictured above. “These smaller, or sometimes quirky, pieces can bring so much personality to a space without requiring a significant investment,” she adds. They may not be originals, but they’re definitely unique.

Old pottery and ceramics are widely available, and they are usually affordable as well. Designer Brooke Snyder of Design Hutch loves how they make any place feel more curated.

The imperfections these handmade pieces carry, from uneven shapes, variations in glaze, or evidence of years of use, are what help these items bring a warmth to a room, particularly when placed near more refined materials, according to Sullivan. They feel humble, maybe even utilitarian, especially when displayed in a kitchen. Or, better yet, used in a kitchen (as long as they’re safe).

“They're beautiful on their own but also so functional for holding utensils or branches or flowers,” Meyer adds. “They make any space feel a little more lived-in and loved and help tell a story—and they don't break the bank!”

“I love incorporating vintage plates, old copper pots, and woven baskets into a home,” explains designer Joe Waroquier. Though these are run-of-the-mill, everyday objects, they can take on and provide an entirely different life when displayed in the home as decor. Mismatched plates on a wall, a collection of copper pots hanging on a rod or resting on shelves in the kitchen, an old crock filled with branches—these items invite in warmth, texture, and more personality.

“I’m particularly drawn to pieces that show their age, whether it’s a little patina, a worn finish, or an imperfect glaze,” he adds. “I love mixing these finds with more contemporary furnishings because that contrast makes a room feel collected and personal rather than overly designed.”

Mirrors can be intriguing focal points of a room, or they can add dimension to a gallery wall. However you want to style them, Snyder loves finding these reflective pieces at antique malls and vintage shops.

“Purchasing something that you don’t see every day from a retailer allows this unique piece to stand out on its own, without feeling like something you see everywhere or feels too simple,” she explains.

As Snyder puts it, antique table lamps provide high visual impact with low investment. Adding one to your accent table or on a bookshelf is an easy way to make your space feel more curated while inviting in another level of ambience with the lighting.

These vintage lamps in the above space, for instance, were bought from a collector and make the primary bedroom feel much more charming and curated. And unlike antique sconces, there’s no need to hardwire these into the wall.

Besides pottery and lamps, designer Ryann Swan Hackett of Ryann Swan Design loves to find and collect other small tabletop decor for her projects. Think candleholders, vintage coffee table books, and trays—things that can layer a little history and individuality into a room or simple vignette.

“If you’re lucky,” Swan Hackett says of the vintage coffee table books, “you’ll find an old inscription inside, which gives the book a story of its own.”

A larger piece of furniture is likely always going to be more expensive than a smaller one, but designer Yena Jung of By Yena Designs thinks vintage hutches are the versatile antiques people should pay more attention to. They come in many different shapes, styles, and sizes, and can easily make a home in any room of the house.

“A beautiful vintage hutch can add warmth, charm, and a sense of history to a space,” Jung says. “I especially love how their imperfections and natural patina make them feel collected over time.”

Inside the First Competition Between Historic, Vintage Timepieces

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Want more insider watch coverage? Get Box + Papers, GQ's newsletter devoted to the watch world, sent to your inbox every Friday. Sign up here to get it free.

In the automotive world, high-end cars have gone head-to-head since the early 1920s. Judges didn’t evaluate them based on their speed, though—in a Concours d’Élégance, the prize goes to the best-looking vehicles. The tradition continues today in picturesque sites around the world where collectors submit their often very high-end vintage, classic cars. Surprisingly, there has never been an event for vintage timepieces.

Aurel Bacs, the famous auctioneer whose longstanding association with Phillips has shaped the watch collecting world, endeavored to change that. “It began with a meeting where I said, ‘I have a dream,’” he said in a press release. For the very first iteration of this competition, Bacs and Phillips partnered with Swiss heavyweight Vacheron Constantin. They expected to receive maybe 100 to 200 submissions, but the jury was forced to whittle down 550 of them to 34 finalist watches, whose owners were flown out from across the world for the Concours held at the Hôtel Président in Geneva.

“It was the most beautiful evening in the field ever,” Bacs said. At the Hôtel Président, around 200 attendees gathered, along with a jury of 14 watch-industry experts including Bacs himself, Ben Clymer of Hodinkee, writer and historian Nick Foulkes, longtime watch journalist Roberta Naas, and others. Watches could only be submitted for consideration in one category (Chronographs, Métiers d’Art, Chronomètre Royal, Astronomical Complications, Multiple Complications, Design, and Chiming Mechanisms) and were judged based on nine criteria: authenticity, elegance, rarity, impact on the history of Vacheron Constantin and/or on watchmaking in general, provenance, technicality, métiers d’art (artistic crafts), state of preservation, and emotional dimension.

The jurors deliberated over several hours until the winners of the seven categories and a Best of Show were finally selected. Following a cocktail reception, everyone gathered on the lower floor of the Hôtel Président to see the results from the watch world’s first Concours d'Élégance.

The first category featured a smattering of some of the maison’s most important wristwatches, including a highly rare ref. 4072 in stainless steel from 1940. However, it was a yellow gold pocket-watch chronograph from 1929 that won the day. The result was such a surprise that the audience, intimately familiar with the historical importance of pocket watches, audibly gasped inside the room. It made me wonder if these old-fashioned timepieces could regain their lost position as the pinnacle of watch collecting. This piece certainly warranted the love it received: in excellent condition and featuring a white enamel dial with dual registers in an “up-down” configuration, it’s a beautiful timepiece.

This category celebrates handcrafts such as enameling and gem-setting, and it featured a smattering of truly spectacular pieces—mostly sub-40mm wristwatches in precious metals with wild cloisonné or Grand Feu champlevé enamel dials depicting landmasses or animals.

However, a stunning pocket watch set with blue sapphires throughout the bezel and Roman indices won out.

When an older American gentleman stepped on stage to collect his prize, he explained to a stunned crowd that not only had his great-grandfather, Dr. Otto von Frangeš, been given the watch by His Majesty Alexander I of Yugoslavia, its original owner, but that he wore it while sitting in the motorcade during the king’s assassination in France in 1934. Otto’s middle son, Nikola, eventually smuggled the watch out of Yugoslavia by sewing it into the lining of his wife’s jacket as they fled the Iron Curtain in the late 1940s.

In 1907, Vacheron launched the Chronomètre Royal sub-brand, specifically built to endure a life sailing the seas. The model’s parts were anti-magnetic and resisted rusting. The dials were made out of enamel, which could also stand up to the moist atmosphere without rusting. Because they were critical to maritime navigation, timing precision was of the utmost importance, and models from this line frequently won observatory trials (which tested for a watch’s accuracy) that ran from the 1800s to the mid-1900s. Given all that information, the winner is a bit of a shocker: a ref. 2215 from 1976 that exemplifies the qualities of the line but modernizes them in many ways. It doesn’t even feature a full set of numerals, trading them out for a few diamond-set indices. In his acceptance speech, the owner said he wears it on the weekends with jeans.

The Astronomical Complications category is dear to the heart of anyone who appreciates a good moonphase display and also includes timepieces equipped with a triple-calendar, a complete calendar, or a perpetual calendar. Though more modern pieces, such as a skeletonized QP wristwatch from 1987, made it to the final round, it was a wildly neat (and unique) platinum-cased “Cioccolatone” from 1957 that won out. Housed in a 34mm x 43mm rectangular case and equipped with a complete calendar, it’s owned by a dapper Italian collector, who beamed as he collected the prize with his grown son. This would not be this pair’s first time at the podium…

This is a loaded category for Vacheron, which famously makes the most complicated pocket watch and wristwatch in the world, with 57 and 41 complications, respectively. To be eligible in this category, watches were required to have at least two complications—child’s play for VC.

Spanning everything from an enamel-dialed, minute-repeating, split-seconds chronograph pocket watch from 1901 to several Patrimony watches from the 1990s, the winner was the “Don Pancho.” Delivered in 1940, this diminutive yellow-gold timepiece with minute repeater, day, and retrograde date indications was once the world’s most complicated wristwatch. Double-signed by Brooking in Madrid, it sold for roughly $740,000 at auction back in 2019.

A more subjective category, perhaps, than the others, Design included watches “featuring Vacheron Constantin’s signature design, either in the case or through the time display.” One entrant, for example—a wild time-only ref. 4695 dress watch—featured incredibly intricate lugs and a Maltese Cross-type dial. But the winner was nothing short of revelatory. Once again owned by the aforementioned tall Italian gentleman, the possibly unique ref. 6213 is an automatic world timer from 1957 in yellow gold. Based upon Louis Cottier’s famous system, it is so rare that even Clymer admitted he had never heard of it before the competition. The owner’s son described his father returning home with it one day when he was a boy, excited beyond belief that he had finally tracked it down after years of searching.

This category included pocket watches or wristwatches fitted with a minute repeater, quarter repeater, half-quarter repeater, or grand sonnerie—a mechanism that automatically strikes the hours and quarter-hours every 15 minutes. Incredibly, among the entrants was a red gold quarter-repeater pocket watch from the early 1820s with a stunning guilloché dial. However, the winner was another pocket watch dating to 1926 and cased in platinum. Its ultra-thin design, black Breguet numerals, Breguet blued steel handset, and simple silver dial belied the complex mechanism within. The visibly emotional American gentleman who owns it recalled how, as a boy, he would open a chest of drawers, remove his grandfather’s pocket watch, and listen to its minute repeater.

A bonus category, Best of Show went to a truly spectacular wristwatch: a ref. 6448 in platinum with diamond-set indices. Seemingly a simple time-only dress watch, this tremendous piece—which was produced in just three examples in the early 1960s—boasts a hand-wound, 13-ligne minute-repeater movement. Housed in a brushed-finish case with incredible teardrop lugs, it’s the only known example in this metal, and it was notably the last minute-repeating reference produced by the brand for nearly 30 years.

Nominally, the crowd at the awards ceremony was there to judge the merits of a watch, but I left emotionally moved by the stories each watch owner shared about their timepieces. As the very first concours for watches, the format will no doubt be repeated in the future—either by Vacheron, adopted by other brands, or done as a more traditional multi-brand event like the Concours d’Élégance at Pebble Beach.

New Sensor Tech Could Replace Bayer Color Filter and Transform Mobile Photography

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New Sensor Tech Could Replace Bayer Color Filter and Transform Mobile Photography

Metalenz has acquired Israeli startup PxE Imaging. PxE’s claim to fame is its Holocoder technology, a replacement for the Bayer color filter found on traditional image sensors.

As Metalenz explains in its press release, the Holocoder “eliminates the need for the Bayer filter and sorts light without absorption, increasing the light delivered to a standard image sensor by 2.5x.”

As expected, significantly increasing the light delivered to an image sensor will, all else equal, dramatically improve camera performance and image quality across the board—in dark scenes and bright ones alike.

Like PxE Imaging, Metalenz has been focused on technologies that adhere directly to an image sensor. A few years ago, Metalenz brought its groundbreaking metasurface optics to market, which are ultra-thin optics that can be printed like semiconductor wafers. The key is how the thin lens bends light.

That’s true, too, of Holocoder technology. The Holocoder “harnesses the wave nature of light to physically decode information such as wavelength and depth, enabling simultaneous capture of color and depth information in the same image with a single camera.”

A Bayer filter, which is how traditional digital image sensors capture and reconstruct color, also absorbs about 60% of the light coming into a sensor, Metalenz says. With its acquisition of PxE, Metalenz can now add sophisticated color and depth information to the material and surface data its products already capture, all without significantly increasing manufacturing complexity or computational demands.

“Metalenz has strategically expanded its IP from unique hardware to full-stack solutions co-designed with our metasurfaces,” says Rob Devlin, Co-founder and CEO of Metalenz.

“We’ve deployed into the most demanding customer devices, built a supply chain at mass-market scale, and built a software stack that takes information delivered to a sensor all the way through AI decisions. With PxE’s technology and team, we’re expanding our capabilities into visible sensing, addressing a longstanding problem of low light performance while solving the new physical AI problem of obtaining richer information beyond conventional RGB imagery,” Devlin continues.

“Owning hardware that brings new information to sensors is the real moat for differentiated AI models. Our combined capability with PxE extends that same principle across all of machine vision, so people and machines can see and understand more about the world without relying on heavy computation.”

“We developed the Holocoder to capture information in light that conventional sensors discard or cannot see, giving cameras greater light sensitivity and the ability to capture color and depth together,” adds Yoav Berlatzky, Co-founder and CEO of PxE Imaging.

“Joining Metalenz gives us the manufacturing platform and commercial reach to bring this technology to everyday devices. Together, we can give people better images and machines a richer understanding of the world.”

The terms of the transaction have not been disclosed.

Devlin spoke to WIRED about the acquisition, shedding more light, pun intended, on PxE Imaging’s technology.

“Apple, Samsung, Google — everyone is fighting to improve the visible camera every year, right?” Devlin tells WIRED.

“A lot of what they’re doing is investing hundreds of millions of dollars into software to try to somehow improve it, but the downside of that is that once information is lost when you’re absorbing all of that light, you can’t really buy it back, even if you’re throwing all of the software into the world at it.”

As of right now, Metalenz cannot directly combine PxE’s Holocoder technology with its commercial Metalenz optics. However, it is absolutely in the cards for the long term. PxE’s technology itself could hit the market as soon as the end of next year, thanks to Metalenz’s manufacturing capacity.

The potential use cases here are manifold. In scenarios where tiny cameras are very important, like autonomous vehicles for example, it’s obvious that a smaller camera that can absorb more light is beneficial.

Another obvious situation is smartphone cameras. As Devlin notes, there is a physical problem that image sensors of all sizes must try to overcome: getting more, better light data. The more light that reaches the sensor, the better. The Bayer color filter is an incredible technological achievement, but it’s getting very long in the tooth. Perhaps, if PxE is correct, there’s a better way.

‘The Rings of Power’ Just Dropped a Major Connection to Tolkien’s Deepest Middle-earth Lore

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Editor's note: The below contains spoilers for The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power Season 3 Episode 3.

Anyone who has read J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings or watched the films may recall that Gandalf names himself a "servant of the Secret Fire." It's a mysterious line, one that is never truly unpacked in Tolkien's epic trilogy. However, this season of The Rings of Power expands on the idea by leading Gandalf (Daniel Weyman) to the Flame Imperishable itself in a “burning bush” moment reminiscent of the biblical story of Moses — and the particulars of this scene offer the best understanding of what this spiritual flame truly is.

Although the Secret Fire (voiced by Allan Corduner) never appears as an actual burning bush in Tolkien's story, Gandalf's encounter in The Rings of Power Season 3 isn't far off from the source material, as the cosmic power originally manifests as a seemingly eternal tongue of fire in the desert. "I am that which burns within all things yet still remains unseen," the Flame reveals. "I built the heavens and stars, and all of Middle-earth is cradled within my flame." Although Tolkien was careful not to insert overt religious allegory into his work, he also maintained (as recorded in The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien) that "The Lord of the Rings is, of course, a fundamentally religious and Catholic work; unconsciously so at first, but consciously in the revision." Many of these undertones exist through certain characters, but on occasion, those ideas are more overt. This is where the Secret Fire comes into play.

As explained in The Silmarillion, the Fire — also called the Flame of Anor in The Fellowship of the Ring or the Flame Imperishable in other sources — is the literal divine power of Eru Ilúvatar, the creator of Tolkien’s fantasy world. It’s not a simple Force-like power that Ilúvatar commands, however, but rather a living extension of himself. As recorded in The Silmarillion, "Ilúvatar gave to their vision Being, and set it amid the Void, and the Secret Fire was sent to burn at the heart of the World; and it was called Eä." In essence, the Secret Fire is the living word and will of Ilúvatar, an aspect of him that exists in all creation itself. The Secret Fire is what gives life to all of Middle-earth, and the desire to attain it is what led Melkor to rebel and become Morgoth in the first place. Since only Ilúvatar can command the Fire, the two are mysteriously but divinely linked.

The cast and creators break down the show's most personal storyline yet for Tolkien's most iconic wizard.

Considering Tolkien's faith, one can draw parallels between the Secret Fire and the Holy Spirit, the third figure of the Trinity, as described in the Bible. In his book, Tolkien and The Silmarillion, Clyde S. Kilby recalled, "Professor Tolkien talked to me at some length about the use of the word 'holy' in The Silmarillion. Very specifically he told me that the 'Secret Fire sent to burn at the heart of the World' in the beginning was the Holy Spirit." Of course, the Holy Spirit is often compared to fire in both the Old and New Testaments, notably during Pentecost, when "tongues of fire" appeared on Jesus Christ's disciples when baptized by the Spirit in Acts 2:3-4, while Hebrews 12:29 describes God metaphorically as a "consuming fire." No doubt, Tolkien had this in mind.

While previous seasons of The Rings of Power have hinted at Eru Ilúvatar's existence, this is the first actual depiction of his presence within the story. Indeed, Tolkien didn't write much about how Ilúvatar directly inserted himself into the rest of creation. While the Secret Fire reveals the truth about why Gandalf was sent to Middle-earth, this is certainly not a commonplace experience. Just as the Valar themselves are still clothed with mystery, so is Ilúvatar — though Tolkien has always asserted that he has been intimately involved in the form of providence. Indeed, according to the author, Gollum's most tragic defeat and the One Ring's destruction during The Lord of the Rings was itself an act of divine intervention by "The Other Power [who] then took over: the Writer of the Story (by which I do not mean myself)," per The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien.

Nevertheless, most interactions with the Secret Fire (and Ilúvatar himself) throughout Middle-earth come in the form of visions, providence, and power rather than "burning bush" moments. Just as Gandalf considers himself a "servant" of the Flame Imperishable, so too do the Faithful in Númenor. This is part of their tension with the King's Men, as the Faithful still follow the teachings of the Valar, themselves angelic spirits working in Ilúvatar's service, whereas the King's Men rebelled against those traditional ways. It's no wonder that Lloyd Owen, who plays Elendil, once described his character as a "combination of Noah and Job from the Old Testament." As The Rings of Power Season 3 continues to expand to the far reaches of Tolkien's world, the advent of the Secret Fire honors the author who first brought Middle-earth to life.

Manna: ‘Napoli will be ready in January, did not spend wisely last season’

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Napoli director Giovanni Manna assures they have ‘clear ideas’ on how to improve Max Allegri’s squad in January, but admits ‘perhaps we didn’t spend our money wisely’ last season.

The Partenopei are struggling badly both in Serie A and the Champions League, with calls for the coach to be sacked, perhaps rescued only by the injury crisis decimating his team.

President Aurelio De Laurentiis seems determined to stick with Allegri and that means giving him more alternatives in the January transfer window.

“We have clear ideas on how to improve, correct and complete the squad, and we will ensure that we are ready in January,” director of sport Manna told Radio Kiss Kiss.

It is very likely that several players will be moved on, above all Lorenzo Lucca and Noa Lang, who have failed to live up to expectations following big money moves.

“Last season we spent a lot, but perhaps we didn’t spend our money wisely, and I say that being fully aware of the criticism I deserve for that. The President and I never held back when it came to investments, but we did have to respect certain financial parameters, so over the summer opted for more of an austerity approach.

“Nobody banned us from making moves, but some situations were stalled and that blocked others in the transfer market. We’ll be ready in January.”

Manna was slightly reassured by the 1-1 draw with Lazio that saw Napoli go into the break for international duty.

“I saw an encouraging reaction in the second half, showing the kind of energy required to get out of the current situation, so we are hopeful things will be better once we get a few players recovered from injury.

“We are well aware of the situation, and we are not happy with how the season has started. Aside from injuries, if we did something wrong or made incorrect evaluations, we are here to work and fix it.

“It’s important to find a sense of calm and not try to experience every game as if it’s the last, as that doesn’t help.”

There have been reports that Napoli are pursuing Torino winger Alessio Cacciamani, who had already been targeted by Roma over the summer.

“He is certainly an interesting prospect who impressed at Juve Stabia and is showing his potential, but that’s a long way from saying he’ll come to Napoli,” replied Manna.

“There are many players we are keeping an eye on, and he is one of the most interesting young Italian players right now.”

Monza centre-forward Gustavo Varela is another name mentioned with regards to a potential January swoop from Napoli.

“He is a good player, but right now we have Lucca, who is young and can cover that role. Then we’ll see, based on who leaves, what will happen. He is certainly doing well, but I doubt Monza will want to sell a player like that in January, seeing as they are fighting for their Serie A safety.

“Napoli have not made any steps for Varela so far, we’ve done nothing.”
