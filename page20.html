Why West Ham fans should welcome - and be wary of - Scott Parker appointment

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Scott Parker is the frontrunner to succeed Nuno Espirito Santo, who was sacked as West Ham head coach on Tuesday morning with the club’s promotion hopes hanging in the balance.

West Ham were widely considered favourites to bounce straight back to the Premier League this season, but after six wins from their first 13 Championship games, they are down in eighth place.

Having recorded a £104.2million loss in the last financial year, the club are acutely aware of the financial imperative of returning to the top flight at the first opportunity and have moved swiftly to make a change in the dugout following successive home defeats to Southampton and West Brom.

Parker, a club legend who won three successive Player of the Year awards during a four-year stint in east London, is seen as someone who can not only get supporters back onside but has experience getting teams promoted from the Championship.

The 46-year-old has earned promotion to the Premier League on three separate occasions across spells with Fulham, Bournemouth and Burnley, but his success has not come without criticism.

His time at Fulham, who offered him his first senior managerial role in May 2019, was dominated by tensions with the board, and Parker openly criticised the club’s long-term structure and recruitment.

Despite taking Fulham up from the Championship, Parker then struggled to build a competitive Premier League side and presided over a dispiriting relegation campaign, with his pragmatic style of play proving increasingly unpopular with supporters.

Parker left Fulham by mutual consent after their relegation in 2021 before swiftly taking charge at Bournemouth. While not direct rivals, the move left a bitter taste for some, with Bournemouth in direct competition with Fulham for promotion from the Championship.

Both teams went up automatically during the 2021/22 season. But while Fulham were able to stabilise under the guidance of Marco Silva, similar issues reared their head for Parker, and he was sacked four games into the season after questioning the direction and commitment of the club following a 9-0 defeat to Liverpool.

Parker’s comments, in which he said his team were "ill-equipped at this level", led Bournemouth’s then-owner Maxim Demin to hit back, stating that for the club to progress, everyone must “show belief in and respect for one another.”

Scott Parker is a manager with pedigree at this level but also plenty of baggage

West Ham will be aware of Parker’s history, including an ill-fated 67-day spell at Belgian side Club Brugge, and they will have made it clear during negotiations that he must secure promotion to secure his future.

After a toxic end to Nuno’s tenure, there will be a fair amount of goodwill for the former England international given his cult status at the club and his knowledge of the league.

The Championship is relentless, though, and results need to change quickly if West Ham are to keep pace with the top of the table and stop the chasing pack from gaining encouragement.

In Parker, West Ham believe they have a safe pair of hands who knows the club and knows how to get out of the Championship. Supporters are, however, bound to be more sceptical.

Parker’s vision for West Ham will almost certainly be built on defensive solidity, and with good reason. West Ham have conceded 18 goals in 13 league games this season, a rate of 1.4 goals per game.

Given the quality of the squad at his disposal, though, there will also be an expectation to entertain. West Ham, for all their struggles, have at times blown teams away this season, scoring in every game this term.

Bosch gives chips their own division, eyeing €5bn in sales by 2030

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The car-parts giant will open its fabs to outside customers and chase robotics demand from January 2028.

The Bosch Parkhaus near Stuttgart Airport

Bosch will move its semiconductor business into a dedicated division from January 2028, as the German supplier looks to expand chip sales beyond the automotive industry.

The company is targeting annual semiconductor revenue of around €5bn by 2030, it announced today. The new unit, which will be called Semiconductors, will remain part of Bosch rather than becoming a separate company.

Its chip business currently sits within the Mobility Electronics division, which will continue to handle the production of electronic control units for vehicles.

The restructuring reflects Bosch’s ambition to find more customers for its chips outside the automotive sector.

The company plans to open its manufacturing facilities to external clients and expand its foundry business, producing semiconductors for other companies.

Its target markets include robotics, factory automation and AI data centres, alongside consumer devices such as phones and wearables. Bosch says its components already play a role in controlling humanoid robots.

“With the founding of a dedicated unit, we are opening a new chapter in our more than 60-year semiconductor history,” said Erik Rein, who heads Bosch’s chip business.

Bosch has several established positions in specialised semiconductor markets. It is a leading supplier of MEMS sensors, the miniature motion and pressure sensors used in devices such as smartphones and robots.

The company also produces silicon carbide chips and custom circuits for power systems.

Its manufacturing footprint spans around 40 chip sites worldwide. Bosch has invested approximately €3bn in its wafer plants in Dresden and Reutlingen, and is spending around $2bn to convert its Roseville, California facility to silicon carbide production.

The company also plans to expand its test centre in Penang, Malaysia, through the mid-2030s. It holds a 10% stake in ESMC, the TSMC-led semiconductor joint venture building a factory in Dresden.

The move comes as Europe’s automotive industry continues to grapple with chip supply risks.

Carmakers spent part of the autumn managing shortages during the Nexperia dispute, which exposed the industry’s dependence on a limited number of suppliers.

By offering manufacturing capacity to outside customers, Bosch could help diversify Europe’s chip supply while seeking new markets for its own technology.

Bosch deputy chairman Markus Heyn said the company’s sensors are “the key” to its products and “the technological basis” for its customers’ innovations.

Prediction: This Will Be D-Wave Quantum's Stock Price by the End of 2030

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The quantum computing pure play has its sights on a potentially profitable niche of this nascent technology.

Experts believe it could take years or even decades for commercially viable quantum computers to be useful at scale. But that isn't stopping early movers from trying their luck at the opportunity, despite the high risk of failure for those that attempt to pioneer new technologies.

D-Wave Quantum (QBTS -5.26%) is one of the ambitious pure plays in the space. And its rapidly growing business suggests it has already developed something that customers may be willing to pay for. But can its current efforts justify the sky-high valuation of its shares?

Quantum computing is a technology that many of us have heard about, but that few truly understand. At its core, it aims to bring an entirely new type of tool to bear on extremely complex computational problems. Classical computers (including whatever digital device you're reading this on) store and process data as bits, which can only have two possible values: one or zero. Quantum computers use quantum bits (qubits) that -- thanks to some peculiar but fundamental aspects of physics -- can temporarily have values that are neither one nor zero, but are instead complex probability vectors.

Think of a bit as a coin that has always either landed on heads or tails, and a qubit as a coin that (while it's in the midst of a problem) is spinning through the air. When it's spinning, it's in superposition of both possibilities, and the angles and speed of that spin influence the probability that it will land on one side versus the other.

If all of that sounds radical and head-achingly complex, that's because it is. It's also completely unnecessary for most common computing workloads.

But if quantum computing technology can ever be made to work as hoped, it could be used to rapidly solve types of problems that would take the world's most powerful classical supercomputers years or even eons to solve, and lead to revolutionary advancements in areas like drug discovery, materials science, and logistics, among others.

Scientists have been working on quantum computing for decades. And the reality is that we still don't know for sure when the technology will deliver on the lofty hopes they have for it. That said, investors have been encouraged by industry leader IBM, which has said it believes it can produce fault-tolerant devices by 2029. For its part, D-Wave Quantum has also begun early commercialization of its own quantum computing devices.

While D-Wave's second-quarter revenue was nearly flat year over year, the company did see a 1,120% jump in bookings for the first half of the year. Bookings refer to the value of client orders that were received during the period, even if they have not yet been fulfilled. That may provide a better view of how D-Wave's business is performing, considering its small scale and the challenges involved in manufacturing and developing its devices. It's also necessary to note that this growth came off an extremely small base. Its actual first-half bookings were just $35.5 million. (Compare that to IBM's Q2 revenue of $17.2 billion.)

D-Wave's flagship product is its Advantage2, a refrigerator-sized machine that is built around an unusual niche technology within the quantum computing realm: quantum annealing. That subset of the technology can be used to find some of the lowest-energy solutions to complex problems. It's important to note that it's quite different from the gate-based quantum computing being pioneered by its rivals, which are aiming for broad-use machines that are analogous to classical computers.

For the foreseeable future, clients will mostly use D-Wave's devices for research rather than full-scale commercial applications.

Over the next four years, D-Wave's stock may experience substantial gains at times driven by positive political developments or breakthroughs in the industry as a whole. But as it stands, I believe these are unlikely to lead to sustainable investor returns over the long term.

Right now, the stock trades at a price-to-sales (P/S) multiple of 307 compared to the S&P 500's average of 3.7. In other words, D-Wave Quantum is priced at a highly speculative level. That limits the returns investors can expect, even under a best-case scenario. Meanwhile, investors can anticipate that D-Wave's cash burn and losses will remain high as it strives to maintain a technical edge over much larger, more-established rivals like Alphabet and IBM.

For these reasons, I don't expect the stock to gain any value over the next four years. And shares will probably still be worth around $10.65 by the end of 2030. Investors who are optimistic about quantum technology should probably wait for a better entry point before considering a position in the stock.

7 Famous James Bond Auditions That Almost Changed 007 Forever

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Given the popularity of the suave super spy and his adventures, it's par for the course that many talented actors have gone for and just missed out on playing James Bond - with some even making it to the audition stage. With major legacy roles like James Bond or Batman, the matter of casting is usually an intensive-sounding one, with those involved in picking these iconic heroes generally wanting to test the waters with a fair range of actors to make sure the right one is selected.

As specific visions for movies are often crafted before casting begins, plenty of actors who could logically nail specific characters can miss out on parts like that of 007 because they don't fit the exact parameters required, rather than not being good enough at their craft. This also means plenty of actors can audition for the role and not get it even if they seemed like solid picks for the opportunity, because the nuance of what's being looked for may contrast with said actor's understanding of what's being asked for.

While this is a shame for those who narrowly missed out on the chance to play James Bond, it does make learning the behind the scenes stories about who almost took on specific eras of 007's story endlessly more engrossing. This is doubly true given that all of these actors would've brought about a different overall landscape for the world of James Bond movies in their own ways.

Years before he would become known as the DCEU's Superman - and later Geralt of Rivia in Netflix's The Witcher - Henry Cavill auditioned for the role of James Bond, hoping to be the actor picked for the era of 007 that would start with Casino Royale.

Though Casino Royale's James Bond role would go to Daniel Craig instead, Cavill's audition has been noted to be a promising one. Director Martin Campbell stated that Cavill's performance was solid, and that he was passed on for the role more because he was slightly too young for the version of Bond that was envisioned (as per The Hollywood Reporter), rather than this seeming to be a matter of acting talent.

Had Cavill been cast for the role, this would have changed two major franchises in a big way. Not only would the 007 series have gotten a Bond whose physicality and history with action stories would have no doubt been reflected in his on-screen performance, the DCEU also would seemingly have had to cast a different Superman too, to avoid filming conflicts.

Sam Neill's audition for 007 came after Roger Moore's time as Bond came to a conclusion, with Neill being one of the viable candidates for his replacement in The Living Daylights. Interestingly enough, it seems as though Neill was very much a frontrunner for the film, with director John Glen reportedly having been inclined towards picking the actor for the role (as per The Hollywood Reporter), though Timothy Dalton would be cast instead.

Given Neill was and is still best known for his role as Dr. Alan Grant in the Jurassic Park series, it would have been interesting to see whether broader audiences struggled more to see him as the kind of more ruthless and no-holds-barred killer that we saw with Dalton's Bond, though Neill certainly did have a look that would have meshed well with the usual debonair getup of the secret agent.

Indeed, while Neill's acting talents suggest he would have been more than capable in this kind of role, it also bears to reason his casting could have influenced the film to divert from the path it took with Timothy Dalton's own Bond to begin with. Given how this set up the path to the modern Bond films we have today, it's not impossible that this move alone could have totally transformed the James Bond timeline from the 80s up until today.

The Bond series has flirted on and off with the idea of an American actor playing the role for a long time now. James Brolin was one of the earlier efforts to consider an American actor for the role, following then-current James Bond actor Roger Moore considering retiring after 1981's For Your Eyes Only.

Brolin's screen test for the role displayed his ability to do some convincing secret spy fight scenes, and it seemed for a short while as though he was set to be the next Bond. However, these plans failed to come to fruition due to Moore returning to the role for 1983's Octopussy.

With that said, it's fascinating to consider what might have been the case if Brolin had indeed gotten the role. Brolin's capacity for comedy, and his decidedly varied filmography in terms of genre means he could have well been the most all-rounded Bond casting to date had things become reality. Similarly, the actor's history with sci-fi before the 1980s might have lent itself nicely to a 007 who was very much at home with using larger-than-life spy gadgets in a casual and cool manner.

Having made a name for himself in the world of crime dramas thanks to his famous performance in The Wire as Jimmy McNulty, it's easy to see how the same skillset that made West a believable Baltimore detective could have also made him a solid 007 casting.

This concept is furthered by West being a standout actor more generally, as is in some ways symbolically reflected in his story about showing up in plain clothes for his audition, when other actors appeared in Bond-styled tuxes (as per Esquire).

The actor's comments that James Bond is "pretty much a superhero, and I think he should be more of a soldier" (via The Scotsman) do suggest West might have played his version of 007 in a different manner. While we've had no shortage of more high-octane Bonds, the idea of a "soldier Bond" speaks to a performance that could have been the spy's most grounded yet, which could have worked both for and against West and the franchise overall.

Michael Gambon's personal perspective on the matter of his James Bond audition is one that doesn't hold anything back. The actor stated to Ryan Tubridy on The Late Late Show that, when he was one of several stage actors interviewed for the role, he told them he didn't believe he was right for the role, commenting: "I can’t play James Bond, because I’m bald, I’ve got a double chin and I’ve got girl’s t*ts."

Given Michael Gambon's perspective on the matter, it's likely any more powerful James Bond performance from Gambon would've required either some major convincing of the actor, or otherwise a move to a less combat-based and perhaps more cerebral or cunning Bond, to allow Gambon to lean into the intellectual side of things in his performance.

Now that Anthony Starr is best known for playing a flying, superhuman villain who can shoot laser beams from his eyes in the form of The Boys' Homelander, it might seem surreal to consider him in the less fantastical - if still often over-the-top - world of spies as a prospective candidate for 007. However, this was indeed the case long before the actor appeared in The Boys, with Starr being one of the candidates for Casino Royale's Bond.

With Anthony Starr's audition for James Bond being one that has since leaked online, we have a direct vantage point into what Starr's spy could have looked like. Starr's smooth delivery and careful use of expression in this tape certainly suggest the actor could have nailed the role, and his filmography only supports the idea, as Anthony Starr has historically given his all to his roles.

Given Starr's greatest strength is his ability to imbue his characters with layered personalities that carry considerable depth, it does also seem more likely than not that his James Bond would have been one of the most three-dimensional feeling in the franchise's history, and may have felt far more individual in some senses than even other similar Bonds, crafting a distinct era for the hero.

Sam Worthington is another actor whose filmography makes it surreal to consider them as James Bond today. Worthington is now instead associated with the Avatar franchise and a decidedly different story of the blue Na'vi finding their way on their home planet of Pandora, rather than the human-centered stories James Bond naturally hinges around.

Regardless, Worthington was also another notable candidate for the role of Casino Royale's James Bond, presenting a very different story as to how his movie timeline could have unfurled. As it stands, Sam Worthington's history playing hardened roles looks as though it could have made him an engrossing Bond, with a kind of believable grit, and an everyman charm that could also make him a convincing spy in this sense.

Interestingly enough, though, it seems the real-world timeline is the actor's preferred one. Worthington noted in an interview with Australian radio station Nova’s Fitzy and Wippa (via Metro) that he was unsure of his delivery of James Bond's British accent, stating he "sounded like Dick Van Dyke from Mary Poppins", and also noted he felt he "just couldn’t get the charm" of the role down. Ultimately, this may mean his later success in the Avatar series instead was perhaps a better fit for the actor and franchise overall.

59 Years Later, Peanuts' Best Quote On Happiness Remains Its Greatest Line Not From Charles Schulz

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Across nearly 50 years and 17,897 daily comic strips between 1950 and 2000, Charles M. Schulz gave Peanuts fans no shortage of memorable lines. Some captured the awkwardness and anxiety of growing up, while others delivered the kind of simple wisdom that sticks with readers long after the punchline. Schulz was famously hands-on with the strip, writing, drawing, and lettering every panel himself. So it's easy to assume that every famous Peanuts quote came directly from him.

When it comes to happiness, Schulz certainly left his mark. His April 1960 comic strip gave readers the now-famous line, "Happiness is a warm puppy," which later became the title of his bestselling 1962 picture book. It's one of the most recognisable quotes in the franchise, but it isn't the only line worth remembering. In fact, Peanuts' best take on happiness came from someone other than Schulz.

That line appears at the very end of You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown, the stage musical that premiered Off-Broadway on March 7, 1967, at Theatre 80 St. Marks in New York City. With Charlie Brown, Snoopy, and the rest of the gang brought to life on stage, the production captured much of the charm and dry humor that made the newspaper strips so popular. It went on to run for 1,597 performances, but its greatest contribution might just be its final line. Nearly 59 years later, that simple thought about happiness still feels right at home alongside Schulz's best writing.

Charles M. Schulz may have created Peanuts, but its best line about happiness came from songwriter Clark Gesner. Gesner wrote the book, music, and lyrics for You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown, though the musical wasn't exactly what he had in mind when he started. Back in the early 1960s, he was simply writing songs about his favourite newspaper comic strip.

He eventually sent a demo tape to Schulz, who liked the songs enough to give Gesner his personal blessing to release them as a 1966 concept album. That record opened the door to the 1967 Off-Broadway musical, with Gary Burghoff (yes, the future Radar from MASH*) playing Charlie Brown, Bob Balaban as Linus, and Bill Hinnant as Snoopy.

The musical's closing number is called "Happiness", and it's pretty much everything that makes Peanuts work packed into one song. Charlie Brown and his friends take turns singing about the little things that make them happy: learning to whistle, finally tying a shoe, having five different crayons, sharing a sandwich, or catching a firefly just to let it go. There's no grand adventure or big life lesson here. It's just the gang enjoying the sort of small victories that Charlie Brown's world is full of. And then Gesner saves the best line for last:

"For happiness is anyone and anything at all, that's loved by you."

That's the line that gets to the heart of Peanuts. Schulz's "Happiness is a warm puppy" is wonderful, and honestly, it's hard to argue with Snoopy-sized doses of puppy love. But Gesner's lyric takes that same idea and gives it a little more room. Happiness can be a pet, a friend, a favourite crayon, or someone who makes even a miserable day feel a bit better.

Anyone who has spent time with Charlie Brown knows that his life isn't exactly short on disappointments. His baseball team keeps losing, his kites keep meeting trees, and Lucy is always ready to pull the football away at the worst possible moment. Yet Peanuts never feels completely miserable.

There's Snoopy on his doghouse, Linus with his blanket, and a group of kids who always find reasons to spend time together. That's the magic of the strip: happiness isn't about winning every game. Sometimes, it's just about having someone to share the day with.

A truly unforgettable sendoff to a legendary comic strip still hits home 26 years later with the final Peanuts Sunday strip.

That's also why You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown gets the characters so right. Snoopy turns dinner into a Broadway showstopper in "Suppertime", Linus celebrates his blanket in "My Blanket and Me", and Sally steals the show with "My New Philosophy", added for the 1999 Broadway revival. The musical understands what made Peanuts special without changing what fans loved about it. And perhaps that's why Gesner's final line still feels so right nearly six decades later.
