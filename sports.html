Football Association chair Debbie Hewitt is set to inspect documentation in person regarding Fifa’s deeply contentious World Cup private investment proposal, it is understood.

The global governing body dispatched a letter on 2 November to members of the Fifa Council – where Hewitt serves as a Fifa vice-president – offering them the opportunity to examine the files at its headquarters in Zurich.

Hewitt is believed to have taken up the offer, though a firm date for the trip remains unconfirmed. Uefa president Aleksander Ceferin is likewise thought to have agreed to attend.

Relations between Uefa and Fifa president Gianni Infantino broke down over the project, which involved creating a new entity named Fifa Forward Enterprise (FFE) to manage commercial and operational activities for both the men’s and women’s World Cups alongside other Fifa tournaments.

Under the blueprint, a minority shareholding in FFE was to be acquired by private investors, led by Thrive Capital, an entity established by Joshua Kushner, whose brother is the son-in-law of United States President Donald Trump.

The initiative was ultimately scrapped following opposition from Uefa and a pair of other continental confederations.

Hewitt’s career experience in private equity provides her with direct insight into the standard documentation and financial details expected in proposals of this nature.

Fifa issued the invitation after European representatives on the Fifa Council sent a formal letter on 19 October requesting clarity on several matters, including the FFE arrangement and disciplinary proceedings involving Folarin Balogun.

Provisions regarding non-disclosure agreements and the specific terms governing document inspection in Zurich have yet to be finalised.

Earlier on Tuesday, the French Football Federation formally endorsed Philippe Diallo’s bid for the Fifa presidency.

On Monday, Uefa confirmed that the 63-year-old had secured the support of national association delegates across its executive committee and the Fifa Council to challenge Infantino.

Disquiet over the FFE proposals prompted European football’s governing body to promise in August that it would put forward a credible challenger to Infantino.

Diallo’s domestic federation has now unanimously backed his campaign, stating: "World football is going through a pivotal period in its history. The challenges affecting its governance demand clear thinking and responsibility.

"Restoring trust, preserving the universality of football, rebuilding Fifa’s credibility, and placing the development of football in all regions of the world at the heart of its actions are essential issues for the entire international football community.

"By accepting this invitation, Philippe Diallo is responding to a desire to open a new chapter for the governance of world football."

Figures within Uefa had previously hoped that former Arsenal manager Arsene Wenger, who currently serves as Fifa’s chief of global football development, might enter the race, though he is believed to have declined the prospect last week.

There is a natural temptation to keep extending deals and retain a successful group. The danger is that a squad can age faster than expected. What appears sensible in 2026 can look problematic by 2029 if too many key players are on expensive long-term contracts at the same stage of their careers.

Elite clubs constantly refresh, they evolve while remaining competitive. Such an approach requires difficult judgment calls.

For now, Arsenal’s contract activity should be viewed as a sign of strength rather than concern. Clubs in decline rarely spend their time trying to protect the futures of their most important players. Arsenal are doing so because they remain convinced that the current project has room to grow.

The more intriguing question is what happens next.

Arteta has already built a team capable of winning the Premier League and challenging for the Champions League. Sustaining that level for another five years may require a different skill altogether. It will require the ability to refresh the squad without weakening it, identify the next generation before the current one begins to decline and make unpopular decisions before they become obvious.

Those decisions are rarely celebrated when they are made – they are usually only appreciated years later; if ever.

Arsenal are entering that phase now. Keeping Rice, Raya and Timber appears straightforward. The real challenge will be determining which members of this successful squad remain part of the next cycle, and which make way for it.

Sean Connery Wasn’t the First James Bond: Meet Barry Nelson in the ‘Casino Royale’ You’ve Never Seen

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ever since George Lazenby first replaced Sean Connery as James Bond in 1969’s “On Her Majesty’s Secret Service,” fans have argued over who makes the best 007 — and those arguments are likely to resume once Robert Pattinson takes the screen as the latest Bond in director Denis Villeneuve’s new iteration. Although Craig has his partisans, Connery tends to be the consensus choice for the best Bond, though I’ve argued here for the merits of 1970s and ’80s Roger Moore. All the Bonds have their virtues, but Connery benefited from being there first — for anyone who discovered the series in its initial heyday, Connery simply is James Bond. Related Stories ‘The Other Bennet Sister’ Christmas Special Trailer: Can Mary Bennet Still Be Herself and ‘the Perfect Wife’? ‘Madden’ Review: David O. Russell Fumbles Again with an Unfunny and Purposeless Football Biopic

Yet Connery actually wasn’t the first actor to play Bond. That honor goes to journeyman MGM contract player Barry Nelson, who played Bond in a “Casino Royale” adaptation eight years before Connery made the character famous in “Dr. No.” Connery may have been the first 007 to grace the big screen, but Nelson established Bond on the small screen in an episode of the CBS anthology series “Climax!” opposite Peter Lorre in the Le Chiffre role that would later be inhabited by Orson Welles (in the goofy 1967 “Casino Royale”) and Mads Mikkelsen (in the 2006 adaptation that introduced Craig to the series).

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is a fascinating artifact that includes elements that would go on to characterize future James Bond movies, while also going in a few other very different directions. The key difference is in the scale, stripped down by necessity for television production. The hour-long “Climax!” episode is all interiors, taking place entirely in the hotel and casino of the title. It’s more a James Bond play than a movie, with Bond and Le Chiffre facing off in a series of verbal — and ultimately physical — jousts in confined spaces, and it’s surprisingly effective.

It’s also a very strange beginning for a franchise that would come to be known for its globetrotting locales and elaborate action set pieces. Not that this “Casino Royale” was officially part of the James Bond franchise as we know it. In fact, although there have been three adaptations of Ian Fleming’s first James Bond novel, only the third incarnation with Daniel Craig was from EON Productions, the company that controlled the character from “Dr. No” through the Craig era before selling the rights to Amazon.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” came about when producer Gregory Ratoff optioned the rights to Fleming’s novel in May of that year. The novel wasn’t yet a hit, and Fleming was sorely in need of cash, which led him to make one of the worst deals in movie-rights history when he sold the book outright to Ratoff for $6,000 after the TV episode aired. This left “Casino Royale” out of the deal when EON secured the screen rights to James Bond as a character, so Bond producers Albert Broccoli and Harry Saltzman could only sit back and grit their teeth when Ratoff turned “Casino Royale” into a comedy in 1967 and cast Peter Sellers, David Niven, Woody Allen and others as rotating Bonds.

EON ultimately made a deal to bring “Casino Royale” under their umbrella and used the novel to reboot the franchise with Craig, but in 1954 it served as the source for a much more modest affair. The biggest similarities between the 1954 “Climax!” episode and the Daniel Craig movie come in the time spent on extended gambling sequences, and the centering of a brutal torture scene. Given censorship restrictions of the time, Nelson’s torture in a bathtub is considerably less graphic than the mallet to the testicles inflicted on Craig, but it’s rough enough stuff for a 1950s TV production.

Certain aspects of Nelson’s characterization, like Bond’s mix of sexual swagger and icy indifference, would later be adopted by most future 007s, to varying degrees. The biggest difference between Nelson and future Bonds is a simple one: He’s American. Although Broccoli and Saltzman flirted with the idea of an American Bond throughout the series’ history (going so far as to cast James Brolin in “Octopussy” before Moore decided to return at the last minute), to date they’ve never actually pulled the trigger on casting outside the UK.

Nelson is probably best known to modern audiences as the affable hotel manager Stuart Ullman in Stanley Kubrick’s “The Shining,” but in the 1950s he was a sturdy character actor and occasional leading man who bounced around television after an initial sojourn as a studio contract player. As a young man, he appeared in several important MGM films, including “Shadow of the Thin Man,” “Bataan,” and “A Guy Named Joe” (the source material for Steven Spielberg’s “Always”). Later on, he popped up in “Airport” and had a successful Broadway career. He was nominated for a Tony acting opposite Liza Minnelli in Martin Scorsese’s 1977 production “The Act.”

In 1954, though, he was in the middle of a prolific and productive era as a TV stalwart, both as a series regular (on “The Hunter” and “My Favorite Husband”) and a frequent guest star. He doesn’t exhibit a ton of range as American “Combined Intelligence” agent “Jimmy” Bond, but that too is in keeping with the overall conception of the character, at least until Craig tried to bring a little more emotional depth to the role.

In Americanizing Bond, the “Climax!” edition of “Casino Royale” ends up making a number of related changes that are small but make the film feel just a bit off for fans of the EON productions. American Felix Leiter (later played by Jeffrey Wright) becomes Brit Clarence Leiter, for example. Although there’s none of the Bond gadgetry that would become famous later, there are hints of what’s to come in the form of props like a villain’s cane that hides a gun, and the opening credits for the “Climax!” series itself feature a gun barrel pointed at the viewer that predicts the iconic graphics of later Bond title sequences.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is noteworthy for another reason: its composer, Jerry Goldsmith, who would ultimately score hundreds of major films, including “Planet of the Apes,” “Chinatown,” “Basic Instinct,” and most of Joe Dante’s work. Yet in some ways what’s fascinating about “Casino Royale” is how unremarkable it is — it’s a decent, solid piece of entertainment from an era when proliferating anthology shows provided similar programs every week.

If Fleming had his way, it would have only been the start for 007 on television; he wrote several outlines for episodes of a James Bond series before CBS canceled the project. Luckily, he repurposed most of what he wrote as short stories for his 1960 book “For Your Eyes Only,” and television’s loss was cinema’s gain. By 1962, James Bond belonged to the movies — and to Sean Connery.

After 3 Marathons Without a Plan, I Finally Followed One. Here’s What Happened in NYC.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

My 3:45 goal didn’t go to plan, but the NYC Marathon still delivered a win.

The first time I entered the New York City Marathon drawing, I got in. I’m not sure what possessed me that day to apply, having never run another race in my life. I figured I’d never get in, so why not? I played soccer growing up, and when the world shut down during COVID-19, running became the easiest way to get out of the house and see my friends. That’s where my running journey began.

My NYC Marathon training also happened during my senior year of college, which brought its own challenges. While I was excited to run, I also didn’t want to miss out on any of the socializing that happened that year. I did my best to make it to every party, every club soccer game, and anything in between while running as much as possible. I was scared that running was going to infringe on my everyday life and I wouldn’t have time to keep having fun. Despite all of that, I had caught the marathon bug and after finishing the New York City Marathon in 2022, I went on to run the Paris and Chicago Marathons in 2024.

While training for those first few marathons, I didn’t have any real plan, except to not let it interfere with my social calendar. My strategy consisted of building up mileage in order to reach 20 miles about four weeks out from race day. In between, I pieced together training information from influencers on Instagram and Google searches (this was pre-ChatGPT!). Now that I’m a Runner’s World+ member, I have all those resources right at my fingertips.

Although I caught the marathon bug almost instantly, I also felt defeated after New York. I knew I could have done better. Between New York City in 2022 and Paris in 2024, I never ran more than 10 miles at a time, but I was running enough that I knew I could do it again if I really wanted. I followed a similar approach for Chicago in 2024: Build up to one long run about a month out, then fill the rest of the weeks with shorter runs and one medium-length run. It wasn’t much of a plan, but it worked for me. My times kept improving, and I broke four hours for the first time in Chicago.

Looking back, I realize that my no-plan approach was partly a way to protect myself. If I never said my goal out loud, I couldn’t feel disappointed if I missed it. Breaking four hours in Chicago changed that. For the first time, I wanted to see what I could do with a real plan.

I was now living in the city I had expected to call home ever since I left college, so I really wanted this race to feel meaningful. For the first time in my marathon career, I downloaded a Runner’s World training plan, the 16-week Break 3:45 plan to be exact, printed it out, and started tracking all of my runs. I wanted to beat my time in Chicago and get as close to 3 hours and 45 minutes as possible.

In the beginning, this felt super daunting. I had never done an interval run in my life nor had I ever intentionally sought out hills to make my runs feel harder than they already did. Nonetheless, I found my old Garmin Forerunner 55 and learned how to upload intervals to run up and down the West Side Highway. When the plan called for hills, I made my way back to the Queensboro Bridge for the first time since 2022, ready to face it with excitement instead of fear. Even while pretending to be excited, the incline is no joke!

For a while, I really felt like I had gotten the hang of things. I actually started to recognize what different paces felt like during the intervals, instead of sprinting and pulling back and looking at my watch while I ran. And, I could now confidently run over many of the bridges in New York City, notably the Williamsburg and Manhattan, even though they aren’t even part of the marathon course. I wasn’t following the plan exactly, but I was able to reorganize the days of the week to hit all the workouts.

Near the end of August (roughly week eight of the plan), it became hot all the time, I was constantly traveling for weekend trips, and I had started to lose the excitement of running. So while I was making sure to hit all of the long runs, the interval training fell off completely. I was a little disappointed in myself because the interval training runs were workouts that made me feel like I was getting stronger each week. I had never done this type of workout before, so I could tell I was getting better each time.

To find the joy of running again, I really started to lean on my friends. Every week, I’d send out a few texts to see who was available and wanted to go for a run with me. I ran the loop in Central Park almost every week and met up with my friends in Brooklyn to run every Friday morning. I started to explore parts of the city I had never been to before, which helped ground me in my training and remember that running is supposed to be fun and is an exciting way to explore the city.

From my previous experience, I knew that running the long runs was the most important thing for me to feel confident on race day. So much of marathon running is mental, so knowing that I could run 20 miles (and have run 26.2 before), was enough for me. So, while I started with a plan, I started reverting back to my tried-and-true method—the just make sure you run the long runs plan.

Outside of the training plan itself, I leaned on Runner’s World a lot. I didn’t have access to this in the past so I didn’t feel confident about planning for prerun meals or even how to take gels during the race. I also found that being able to read runner stories online made me feel more connected to the community, which helped me mentally with my training.

Plan or no plan, I’m proud of myself! My official time was 3:57:39, so while I did not achieve my goal of breaking 3:45, I did run a sub-4 race, and following the Runner’s World plan helped me get there. The last time I ran this race, I was a senior in college and thought I had it all figured out. I was going to move to New York City, get a real adult job, and start the life I had been imagining. Similar to my experience following the Runner’s World plan, this is not how everything went. Instead, I moved to a different city after graduating, eventually found my way back to New York, and my life is so much more full than I ever expected because of it. Maybe things aren’t always supposed to go to plan, and that’s a good thing!

'I didn't want to look back on my career with regret' - the journey of Aaron Connolly

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Aaron Connolly speaks to Sky Sports about his breakthrough at Brighton and the pitfalls and setbacks that followed on his journey in the game. He is now rebuilding in League One at Leyton Orient and determined to forge a career he can be proud of.

It is easy to forget that Aaron Connolly is still only 26, considering everything he has already experienced.

Just over seven years have passed since that magical full Premier League debut, scoring twice for Brighton against Spurs at the age of 19 in 2019.

In the time since, there have been injuries that have damaged his progress in football and obstacles in his life beyond the pitch.

"It's been tough," Connolly - now at Leyton Orient - tells Sky Sports. "It's probably been well documented that I haven't dealt with setbacks very well before.

"I love playing football. It's what I've done my whole life. So when I can't, it's obviously difficult.

"I've had a spell of injuries and interruptions. It seems to have been the way for the last three or four years of my career.

"At Hull, I was doing well, scoring goals, then got an injury and didn't really get back in.

"Then I left Hull, went to Sunderland, started one of my first games against Millwall, scored and then picked up a little injury and missed the next game. It's just that momentum I never seemed to get.

"Then obviously last year [at Orient] I had that momentum for about 15 to 20 games. I started scoring and assisting, and then tore my quad. I was in a bit of a rush to come back and then obviously did my shoulder and needed surgery again.

"Hopefully, I'm doing everything I can to stay injury-free right now, but with football you never know. There could just be that one late tackle or slip or something. Hopefully, fingers crossed, I'm past that stage now."

Connolly knows, injuries aside, he hasn't always helped himself. Temptations and distractions away from the pitch halted the progress in his late teens and early 20s.

A reputation was built that he is now working hard to shake off.

"There's always going to be football fans who have a certain opinion of you without knowing you," he admits. "I burst onto the scene a few years ago, and it feels like a long time ago now.

"There are people at the highest level because they live and breathe football and do everything properly. I didn't do that.

"I probably worked as hard as I could to get to that position to score those goals against Tottenham. After that, I kind of came away from everything I'd done to get myself in that position.

"Now, obviously, I've realised, and I'm hoping it's not too late to have realised, that kind of lifestyle and that attitude towards the game isn't going to get me very far.

"I probably believed the hype straight away and thought I'd scored two on my full debut, so I'm going to score 30 in the Premier League without dedicating myself to it, being in the gym, being first in and last to leave.

"I let myself down a lot, and that's probably why I ended up getting injured.

"I'd be late into training, be the first one to leave, never really stay and work on my craft or anything. I kind of thought I'd signed a long-term deal here now, so I've got five years to play with. That five years just went, and every footballer will tell you it goes quickly. It does.

"I've realised now that I need to dedicate myself to football. Sometimes when I'm at the training ground now, I come in first and leave last, and I'm sitting there thinking, 'why has it only clicked for me now that that's what you need to do in football?'

"Probably from 23 to 25 years old, I was just looking back on when I was 19, thinking, 'what did I do? Why did I do it for so long?'

"Even when I was injured, I still wanted to do the same thing because when I was at Brighton, if I got a little injury, that injury would take six weeks when it should have been two because of the way I was doing things. I wasn't doing everything properly.

"But listen, that's just the journey. If every player had the same journey, it would be a boring sport.

"I can never look back and say I wish I hadn't scored those two goals straight away, because I might not have ended up at Sunderland, for example, without that kind of CV behind me because of the impact I had early on at Brighton.

"It is difficult to think that I wish it hadn't happened. I do agree it probably was the worst thing that could have happened, looking back now, but at the time it felt like the best thing ever. And some of the issues probably would have come about eventually anyway."

Part of Connolly's journey has been overcoming an alcohol addiction, something he opened up about a couple of years ago on World Mental Health Day, while he was with Sunderland.

"I don't want to be that kind of player that's known for these types of issues, but also the reason I need to remember why I did the interview a few years ago was just to help as many people as possible," he says. "I know in my family my mum's dad struggled a lot with it, so I'd always happily speak about it.

"I'm in a good place with all of it. Obviously, it is a difficult thing to wake up and realise I can't do what a lot of my team-mates can do, which is go out on a Saturday, have maybe a couple of sociable drinks and go home. I know that's not me.

"Trying to find different things at the weekend to entertain myself when all I knew before was Saturday game, Saturday out, Sunday out. That was kind of what I'd do.

"I don't miss it, that's for sure. I have a better life and better relationships with people now without it.

"And my football, if I was drinking and going out, I wouldn't be able to do any of it, at any level.

"I wouldn't have the relationships I have with people. I always have time for everybody now, whereas before I was quite selfish and didn't have time for many people.

"I was probably miserable a lot in different clubs and different scenarios. It would be the only thing on my mind.

"I can't think about whether people like me or like what I've done or opened up about that situation. But for me, it helped, and all the messages and responses I received helped a lot of other people as well. That's what I've done it for."

Connolly is keen to now try and help younger players learn from his own pathway, even if those at Orient aren't quite exposed to what he was in the Premier League.

"Everyone needs to make mistakes," he says. "I'm not going to know all the answers straight away when I was 19 and getting paid all this money and playing in front of 50,000 every week.

"People are texting you saying, 'I can sort you this, I can do this for you', the minute you start playing in the Premier League.

"It took a lot of adjusting and getting used to that. I'm probably still not used to that kind of stuff because when you're growing up in Ireland, you don't get that kind of special treatment with things like that.

"But I always sit with the academy players in the physio room, and if they ever want to come and ask me something, they know they can. I'd like to think I'd give them as much advice as I can.

"They all want to play in the first team, but they're currently on loan in the Isthmian Premier, and they're thinking it's the end of the world. Why am I here at 18 or 19?

"I'll remind them that some journeys are completely different. I was in the Premier League at 19 and now I'm in League One. You might be in the Isthmian at 19 and could be in the Premier League by 26. There are different journeys.

"I always used to have a little bit of back and forth with Adam Webster at Brighton when I'd say, 'where were you at 19?' He was playing for Portsmouth in League One and Two. He'd say, 'well, I'm here at 26. Let's see where you are at 26'.

"He was right. They probably saw it coming before I did.

"I always try to give them that kind of advice. I know they do come in and sit with me and ask me questions, so I just want to be that kind of open space for them as well."

For Connolly, there is a lot of time to get back to where he believes he can be. Right now, he just wants to get a run of fitness and form with Leyton Orient.

Most importantly, he is determined to forge a career with no more regrets.

"A lot of people when I was 19 probably wouldn't have thought I'd be in League One, but that was probably one of the reasons why I'd end up dropping down the leagues to where I am now." he says.

"Like I said before, if this is going to be my level and this is where I end up at 35 or 36, then that's fine. At least I know I've given it everything, and then it's down to whether there are better players than me and better players in my position going ahead of me. I can accept that.

"But if I'd kept living the way I was and not appreciating the game, I'd probably look back when I'm 34 or 35 thinking, I wish I'd realised this 10 years earlier.

"I don't want to look back on my career with regret. You always hear that when I was 18, there used to be people coming into the training ground for educational talks and saying that.

"I'm just looking forward to seeing what I can do at 100 per cent fitness."

Palace chairman Steve Parish said Frank was the "perfect fit for our club".

But how big is the challenge at Selhurst Park that awaits the man who made his name at Brentford?

"It's going to be a bit of a shock for Thomas Frank," former West Ham and England goalkeeper Rob Green tells BBC Sport.

Newcomers to the ballot are in bold.

As a reminder, players must receive votes on at least 75% of the ballots cast to be inducted into the Hall of Fame with the 2027 class. Those who get at least 5% of the vote can remain on the ballot the following year, and so on, for a maximum of 10 voting cycles. Players who are on the ballot for 10 years without getting 75% of the vote, in addition to players not getting 5% of the vote, fall off the ballot in ensuing years, though that doesn't mean the doors to the Hall are permanently shut.

The results will be announced on Jan. 19, with the induction set for Cooperstown over the summer.

Here's everything else you need to know about the Baseball Hall of Fame this offseason.

As alluded to earlier, there are two different votes each offseason. The BBWAA vote is for former players who have been retired from Major League Baseball for at least five years. It's the most common method in mainstream Hall of Fame ballot discussions. When you hear stuff like "first-ballot Hall of Famer," it means via the BBWAA vote.

Players who don't make it via the BBWAA vote can get extra chances via committees. That's how, for example, Jeff Kent made it last year. Formerly called the Veterans Committee, the Hall of Fame now uses Era Committees, which are divided into two categories: contemporary baseball (1980 to present) and classic (prior to 1980). The contemporary ballot is then split into two, one for players and one for non-players like umpires, executives, pioneers and managers.

Sure enough, this time around, it is a non-player Contemporary Baseball Committee, which looks at "managers, umpires and executives whose most significant career impact was realized since 1980."

We'll obviously do a deeper dive in the coming weeks, but even without the longevity component (Posey only played in parts of 12 MLB seasons), I think he's in.

Then you're looking at Brett Gardner, Ryan Zimmerman, Kyle Seager, the short peak of Jake Arrieta and relief work of Andrew Miller. Good players worth mentioning, of course, but they won't sniff the Hall and it seems likely all behind Posey and Lester are one-and-dones on this ballot, as they'll fall short of 5% of the vote.

This is the sixth year for Alex Rodriguez on the ballot, which means it's the sixth time that I point out that we don't really need much time to go over his case. His numbers say he's one of the 10 best position players of all time, but his ties to PEDs are keeping him out to this point.

Spain vs England was a game that had almost everything, and may yet mean more; perhaps the most significant moment might have come before it had even started.

The hosts took the opportunity of a first match back in Madrid since the World Cup to showcase the trophy, in what almost felt another homecoming.

The mood was very much of revelling in their embossed status, something that would have had a particular edge for this specific nation.

No country that has won the World Cup has waited as long for another trophy as England.

And, as the 2010 winners Iker Casillas, Fernando Torres and Fernando Llorente presented Unai Simon that with that precious piece of brilliant gold, it was almost literally in England’s faces.

Thomas Tuchel’s side had to watch the goalkeeper wave the World Cup about right in front of them.

It had been noted on the Spanish side that Jude Bellingham was the only player who clapped, this now being the city that he calls home with Real Madrid. He then played like he was demanding the same appreciation, and he was not the only one.

Seeing the World Cup so close and yet so far, with more than three years until the next edition, only deepened a mood that had been felt in the build-up to this game.

England, in the words of both Declan Rice and Thomas Tuchel, knew they were going to win.

Just as importantly, they displayed a new level of their own, even beyond Tuchel describing it as the best performance of his time.

This wasn’t, after all, the usual counter-attacking approach to playing Spain - at least not for when England actually got so far ahead.

It was England imposing their game, an evolving ideology, on Spain.

“We are well aware which steps we took after the World Cup,” Tuchel said, conspicuously repeating this more than once.

The manager was deeply stung after all the criticism following the Argentina defeat.

And yet, much as he might bristle about it in public, it does feel as if some of that criticism has been acknowledged.

England look much more technical, but still with pace.

This was something much more considered and coherent.

“We wanted to get better in the deep build-up, we wanted to get better at defending in a low block and on top of it, we wanted always to impose our identity and the way we want to play on every opponent,” Tuchel remarked.

Tuchel clearly wants to change the very focus of this team.

They’re a team that know how to beat them, that can carry that through to a home Euros.

It might even create a bit of consideration on the Spanish side, even allowing for how assured they are in their own approach.

It might be the beginning of a proper modern rivalry.

Might, of course. England have to go and use this to do what Spain have made customary, and actually win one of those trophies.
