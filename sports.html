Who is Joe Milton III? Eagles turn to QB with 'cannon' arm strength

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Jordan Mailata might have been speaking for the rest of his Philadelphia Eagles teammates – and the public at large – when discussing his initial impression of quarterback Joe Milton.

"Have you seen his arm?" Mailata asked reporters on Wednesday, Nov. 4. "It is a (expletive) cannon, oh my God."

After taking a moment to apologize for his language, the left tackle went on to describe what made the passer such a singular player at the position.

"You see (the arm strength) on TV. You hear about it when he was going through the draft. But then you see it, and it's a work of art, man," Mailata said.

Since he was a high school football standout, Milton has been known for physical capabilities that differentiate him from even his most gifted peers. But after spending the bulk of his three-year pro career on the fringes of the league, the 6-5, 230-pound quarterback will get his chance to step into the spotlight Sunday, when he's set for his first pro start against the New York Giants.

Milton takes over for Tanner McKee, who started the past three games after Jalen Hurts was sidelined by blood clots that later moved to his lungs. Under McKee, an already shorthanded and struggling Eagles offense continued to flounder. The attack ranks last in yards per game (265.3) and 30th in scoring (17.1 points per game).

With the team's losing streak extending to six games, Milton is being called on to provide some kind of spark.

"With everything going on, he provides a good light to it," Mailata said of Milton.

From the time Milton started emerging as a highly sought-after high school recruit in Orlando, word began spreading about his arm strength.

As a 16-year-old, he threw a pass 75 yards to win a high-school competition, he said in 2024. At the Manning Passing Academy in 2023, Milton launched a pass more than 70 yards, with a clip of the feat quickly going viral.

For as easy as he made his throws look, though, college was anything but that for the quarterback. He lost his starting job twice – first at Michigan, then again at Tennessee after an injury.

But Milton put himself back into the NFL conversation with his final season with the Volunteers, during which he threw for 2,769 yards and 20 touchdowns with five interceptions. After again showcasing his unique physical tools during the pre-draft process, he was selected in the sixth round of the 2024 NFL Draft by the New England Patriots, who took fellow quarterback Drake Maye with the No. 4 overall pick.

Stashed as a developmental option, Maye didn't see any action until Week 18 of his rookie year, when he took over for Maye after one series and threw for 241 yards in a win over the Buffalo Bills. He delivered a signature moment when he repeatedly evaded rushers as he darted left before uncorking a 48-yard touchdown strike to Kayshon Boutte.

That performance caught the attention of Dallas Cowboys owner Jerry Jones, who three months later acquired Milton in a trade with the Patriots.

"I really have to pinch myself that we got him after the last game that he had up there, starting there for New England," Jones told 105.3 The Fan in Dallas in August 2025. "I'm just excited that we had a chance to get him. And he hasn't disappointed. ... We're just more and more encouraged by what we're seeing."

But Milton lost the role as Dak Prescott's backup to Sam Howell prior to this season and was waived in late August as part of the team's cutdowns to a 53-man roster. He rejoined the Cowboys two days later, however, when he re-signed to the practice squad.

When the Eagles signed Milton to their practice squad on Oct. 16, one day before Hurts was placed on injured reserve, the move might have largely been an afterthought to some.

Less than a month later, however, he's taking on a starring role for the team at a crucial juncture.

Eagles coach Nick Sirianni initially said on Monday, Nov. 2, that he was not entertaining benching McKee. Two days later, news broke of the switch.

Sirianni later chalked up the mislead to a matter of trying to establish a competitive advantage.

"Every second that goes by in a situation like that, that you can hold onto that information as long as you possibly can, is a little less time for the opponent to get ready for the game," Sirianni said Friday, Nov. 6.

With the news out, however, Sirianni went to bat for his new starter.

"He's talented and been here for a couple weeks now," Sirianni said of Milton. "Very talented arm, very talented athlete, can process and make decisions out there. Lot of good qualities he has. I like his confidence and everything that he brings, and it's been fun getting to know him and working with him."

Matt Mercer's Best Critical Role Quote Is Still the Most Inspiring Line in D&D History

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If you’re still on the fence about being a DM, listen to Mercer

Matthew Mercer is one of the world's most popular Dungeon Masters. Alongside other incredible DMs like Deborah Ann Woll, Brennan Lee Mulligan, and Aabria Iyengar, Mercer has undeniably shaped how players worldwide approach tabletop role-playing games. As Critical Role, the multimedia entertainment company Mercer co-founded, has grown and evolved over the years, one thing has remained constant: Mercer’s willingness to help other tabletop players. He is especially eager to support aspiring Dungeon Masters who hope to develop their skills and become as accomplished as he is, even appearing on the official Dungeons & Dragons YouTube page to provide assistance.

Mercer’s wisdom is well documented across multiple interviews, but only recently, as a fellow DM, did I find the most inspiring quote that everyone (regardless of whether you're a Dungeon Master or a player) should internalize as soon as humanly possible.

In an interview with Variety published in January 2026, Mercer and Mulligan sat down for a sincere conversation about everything and anything D&D. They reflected on their respective work as Dungeon Masters on Critical Role and Dimension 20, discussing the experiences and paths that shaped their careers.

One topic that came up was preparing a tabletop session. Mulligan asked Mercer what his philosophy is for preparing a tabletop session, and Mercer’s answer perfectly defines one of the core beliefs that we think should inspire players on either side of the table in their future games.

“Like many things, it comes with experience, and sometimes you have to fail to learn better.”

After decades of playing tabletop games and building a successful career from them, Mercer’s words capture the spirit of what makes role-playing tabletop games, particularly D&D, so engaging — our desire to perfect how we tell stories through interactive play.

We’ve all heard similar sayings about the benefits of failure, such as Henry Ford’s “Failure is only the opportunity to begin again, this time more intelligently,” or Chris Bradford’s “There is no failure except in no longer trying.”

Giving yourself permission to fail in D&D, however, is one of the most essential lessons a player needs to learn. Without it, the striving for perfection puts tremendous pressure on not just you, but everyone at the table with you.

For example: if you’re the DM, you’ll constantly be assessing whether your players are having fun, or feeling like you have to nudge them in a specific direction, and getting more and more alarmed when that doesn’t turn out to be the case. If you’re a player, you’ll be so busy overthinking whether your decisions will work out that you'll bring the game to a halt.

When I first started DMing, I scripted scenarios that I thought would completely derail the campaign and the story I wanted to tell if the players didn’t follow them to the letter. But that mindset was selfish: this wasn’t just my story, and I wasn't the only player at the table. It led me to make terrible, sometimes unfair calls as I tried to get everything back on track. Half the time, it didn’t work and only made things harder. Why didn’t I stop and pause to tell my players they’d thrown me a curveball once again — and that I, a Brit with only a passing knowledge of American sports, had fumbled it so badly?

The truth was that my anxiety about not doing things exactly as I’d planned in my head was making games feel far more terrifying than they needed to. I didn’t want to be wrong, but being wrong, and having people accept that about you and still want to sit at your table because they want to be part of the story you’re telling together, is one of life’s greatest pleasures.

Regardless of which side of the table you’re on, the fear of failure and the pursuit of perfection can gradually take over and distract from what matters most. When you try so hard to make every decision, encounter, or moment just right, it’s easy to lose sight of the reason everyone gathered to play in the first place: to have fun.

That’s why it’s worth keeping Mercer’s advice in mind. After all, failure is a gift that keeps on giving.

Per the posting agreement with MLB, posted NPB and CPBL players get a 45-day window to negotiate and sign with an MLB team. KBO players get a 30-day window. Players who do not sign during that posting window return to their previous team in NPB, CPBL, or KBO. The MLB team that signs a posted player pays a fee to the player's former NPB, CPBL, or KBO team.

$5M plus 17.5% of contract value over $25M

$9.375M plus 15% of contract value over $50M

For example, the White Sox paid Munetaka Murakami's former team, the Yakult Swallows, a $6.575 million posting fee on his $34 million contract last offseason. That's $5 million on the first $25 million plus 17.5% of the remaining $9 million, which is $1.575 million. The $5 million plus $1.575 million equals the $6.575 million posting fee paid to the Swallows.

Why Footballers Wear Tiny Shin Pads

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Watch any professional football match closely, and you may notice something odd about some players' legs: socks rolled down to the calf, with holes cut out of the back and, tucked inside them, a shin-pad barely bigger than a credit card. Jack Grealish made the look famous, but the trend has since spread from the elite game down to Sunday league and junior pitches.

Shin-pads are compulsory under football's laws, so how are players getting away with protecting so little of their shins? And why would they want to flout a rule made to protect them and their most prized asset?

Shin-pads became mandatory in 1990 due to the increasing physical brutality and injury rates seen in Europe's elite leagues.

According to Law 4 of IFAB's (International Football Association Board) Laws of the Game, shin pads must be made of a 'suitable material' and must be 'of an appropriate size to provide reasonable protection'. However, it also states that players are responsible for the 'size and suitability' of their shin-pads, meaning that, ultimately, the decision is up to the individual.

IFAB told BBC Sport that referees are only required to check that some form of shin pads are being worn, meaning that even match officials are not able to force a player to change into bigger, more protective equipment.

An explanation has emerged after players have been spotted with cut holes in the back of their football boots

The majority of professionals who have been quizzed on their shin-guards cite comfort and freedom of movement as the main factor in opting for micro-pads. Given the speed and intensity that the modern game is played at, every gram less that their equipment weighs gives a minute boost to their range of motion and explosiveness.

Another reason is the recent trend of low socks that sit around the calf. According to Law 4, the shin pads must be covered by the players' socks. Therefore, if they were to use the more traditional, bigger shin-pads, players would have to wear longer socks.

The reason Jack Grealish wears his socks rolled down to his ankles and the smallest shin pads you're ever likely to see

Whilst there has not yet been an in-depth study into the effectiveness of mini-shin-pads compared to traditional ones, guidance written by the FA, and the actions of various amateur and semi-professional clubs, offer a damning verdict.

FA guidance written in 2024 states that "any part of the shin that is not covered is not as well protected", before adding that micro shin pads may "increase the risk of injury due to the smaller amount of shin covered." The statement continues by advising that "safety is prioritised by ensuring a good proportion of the shin area is covered and therefore protected."

Various amateur and non-league clubs have been keen to ban mini-shin-pads, including Ilfracombe Town, who prohibited the use of such equipment across all age groups and squads after forward Riley Malin suffered a double-leg break (tibia and fibula) in a 50/50 challenge.

According to the BBC, in August 2024, Penistone Church took similar action after teenager Alfie Collins also suffered a double-leg break, having switched his bigger shin-pads at halftime for a pair that were "no bigger than a credit card."

Josh O’Connor Should Be James Bond

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Challengers star would bring a new energy—and a new brand of modern masculinity—to the world’s most famous spy.

WHEN THE NAMES of the rumored final contenders to play the next James Bond were floated in the press a couple of weeks ago—seemingly Callum Turner, Jack Lowden, Josh O’Connor, and a mystery third candidate, according to Deadline—I heard a lot of the same sentiment afterward. In more or less words, a lot of people seemed to say “I love Josh O’Connor, but he shouldn’t be Bond.” When I dropped the link into a group chat full of people who aren’t inhaling movie news like I am, there was another probably common response: “I don’t know who that guy is, but he doesn’t look like Bond.”

To me, that sentiment is exactly why O’Connor is the best—and, in some ways, the only—choice to be the next James Bond.

For those not familiar, O’Connor, a 36-year-old Brit who grew up in Cheltenham, has been a working actor for more than a decade, but his star has only really begun to shine in the 2020s. He won an Emmy for playing Prince Charles in The Crown, and more recently has made splashes in movies like the thrilling and sexy sports film Challengers, Rian Johnson’s third Knives Out film Wake Up Dead Man (which, ironically, he appeared in alongside previous Bond Daniel Craig), and this summer’s Steven Spielberg blockbuster Disclosure Day.

It’s not hard to understand why some people’s minds might naturally push against the idea of O’Connor as Bond. Where 007 is typically a classically handsome, devilishly smooth gentleman who almost looks like a video game simulation—seriously, look back at Sean Connery and Pierce Brosnan for just a second—O’Connor is a bit off-kilter. His hair is wavy; his ears are prominent; his smile is asymmetrical. There’s no beating around the bush—he’s been compared more often to Linguini from Pixar’s Ratatouille than the idea most people have of what a James Bond should be.

But it’s through those eccentricities that O’Connor shines. If you take the time to watch his performances, you’ll find the constant image of someone who brings an undeniable charisma to every project, and somehow manages to mold that into something different each time. In Challengers, O’Connor is the kind of guy you want to root against—he’s sleazy, not a particularly good friend, and unrefined. But he brings the perfect bit of steamy, compelling energy to a love triangle that wouldn’t be the same without him. Co-stars Mike Faist and Zendaya are deserving of credit (as are composers Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross), but O’Connor’s charm is what makes the whole movie work.

He pulls a similar magic trick with just about every other performance. In Wake Up Dead Man, he plays a former boxer-turned-priest who once accidentally killed a man in the ring. Throughout the course of the film, O’Connor goes from someone who wrestles with guilt, shame, and intrigue to someone who can believably talk someone struggling with grief off a ledge—all while both trying to solve a murder and possibly being suspected of that same murder at the same time. In Kelly Reichardt’s The Mastermind, O’Connor plays a less-than-competent, hardly aspirational art thief who stumbles his way through charged political times. Despite this, O’Connor is so magnetic on screen that it’s just about impossible not to like him.

But it’s not just the way he elevates sleazeballs and losers into undeniably watchable men (although that doesn’t hurt). O’Connor has shown a propensity to play traditionally enchanting as well. In La Chimera, from Italian filmmaker Alice Rohrwacher, he’s cool, sleek, and morally gray in a movie that may be the blueprint for what his Bond could potentially look like. He’s got a likable way about him that’s different from some of the manly men (like Connery), sleek operators (like Brosnan), or rugged action stars (like Craig) who’ve played the role before; His charm is distinctly more modern, oozing from nothing other than being a guy who knows how to talk to people, and do it really well.

O’Connor’s quirks and oddities, may, at first glance, be what seems to make him an unfit choice to play Bond. But the key is to look at O’Connor’s unique look and gifts as a feature rather than a bug. People might say Bond needs to look this way, and sound that way, and otherwise it doesn’t work. But for anyone who feels so strongly about that, there’s good news: There are 25 other canonical James Bond movies (plus 1967’s Casino Royale and 1983’s Never Say Never Again) where he almost always looks and behaves just like that. When better than now to try something new? Daniel Craig’s blond hair and untraditional look were also questioned at first, and it’s safe to say that risk turned out pretty well in the end.

The forthcoming Bond movie has Denis Villeneuve signed on as director, and Villeneuve has pretty much never failed when it comes to casting his movies. Can you imagine anyone other than Timothée Chalamet as Paul Atreides in his Dune movies? It seems like an obvious pick now, but the 30-year-old Oscar nominee had only just begun to break out when he was cast in the role back in 2018. Villeneuve has a clear eye for talent, and so it won’t be much of a stretch to trust in whoever ultimately lands the job—but it’s hard to not be excited about a director of his caliber linking up with an actor as ambitious and high-reaching as O’Connor.

Plus, it’s worth noting that the Bond franchise isn’t quite like other mega-series out there. Since the Craig era began with 2006’s Casino Royale, Bond movies have come out on average every three or four years. That’s spread out enough for O’Connor to continue taking on fun, exciting projects even outside of the role as everyone’s favorite super spy.

The other choices aren’t bad. Callum Turner gets a bad rap after his appearance in One Night Only, but he’s shown promise in projects like Green Room and Masters of the Air, and Apple TV’s new Neuromancer looks good. Jack Lowden has only played one of television’s most beloved spies for six seasons on Slow Horses—the fit would be seamless. Whoever Villeneuve picks (even if it’s the unnamed mystery actor) will be afforded a huge amount of goodwill.

But isn’t the fact that O’Connor elicits such a reaction—consistently in his on-screen roles and through the mere idea of him becoming James Bond—proof that he’s the right pick?
