'I didn't want to look back on my career with regret' - the journey of Aaron Connolly

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Aaron Connolly speaks to Sky Sports about his breakthrough at Brighton and the pitfalls and setbacks that followed on his journey in the game. He is now rebuilding in League One at Leyton Orient and determined to forge a career he can be proud of.

It is easy to forget that Aaron Connolly is still only 26, considering everything he has already experienced.

Just over seven years have passed since that magical full Premier League debut, scoring twice for Brighton against Spurs at the age of 19 in 2019.

In the time since, there have been injuries that have damaged his progress in football and obstacles in his life beyond the pitch.

"It's been tough," Connolly - now at Leyton Orient - tells Sky Sports. "It's probably been well documented that I haven't dealt with setbacks very well before.

"I love playing football. It's what I've done my whole life. So when I can't, it's obviously difficult.

"I've had a spell of injuries and interruptions. It seems to have been the way for the last three or four years of my career.

"At Hull, I was doing well, scoring goals, then got an injury and didn't really get back in.

"Then I left Hull, went to Sunderland, started one of my first games against Millwall, scored and then picked up a little injury and missed the next game. It's just that momentum I never seemed to get.

"Then obviously last year [at Orient] I had that momentum for about 15 to 20 games. I started scoring and assisting, and then tore my quad. I was in a bit of a rush to come back and then obviously did my shoulder and needed surgery again.

"Hopefully, I'm doing everything I can to stay injury-free right now, but with football you never know. There could just be that one late tackle or slip or something. Hopefully, fingers crossed, I'm past that stage now."

Connolly knows, injuries aside, he hasn't always helped himself. Temptations and distractions away from the pitch halted the progress in his late teens and early 20s.

A reputation was built that he is now working hard to shake off.

"There's always going to be football fans who have a certain opinion of you without knowing you," he admits. "I burst onto the scene a few years ago, and it feels like a long time ago now.

"There are people at the highest level because they live and breathe football and do everything properly. I didn't do that.

"I probably worked as hard as I could to get to that position to score those goals against Tottenham. After that, I kind of came away from everything I'd done to get myself in that position.

"Now, obviously, I've realised, and I'm hoping it's not too late to have realised, that kind of lifestyle and that attitude towards the game isn't going to get me very far.

"I probably believed the hype straight away and thought I'd scored two on my full debut, so I'm going to score 30 in the Premier League without dedicating myself to it, being in the gym, being first in and last to leave.

"I let myself down a lot, and that's probably why I ended up getting injured.

"I'd be late into training, be the first one to leave, never really stay and work on my craft or anything. I kind of thought I'd signed a long-term deal here now, so I've got five years to play with. That five years just went, and every footballer will tell you it goes quickly. It does.

"I've realised now that I need to dedicate myself to football. Sometimes when I'm at the training ground now, I come in first and leave last, and I'm sitting there thinking, 'why has it only clicked for me now that that's what you need to do in football?'

"Probably from 23 to 25 years old, I was just looking back on when I was 19, thinking, 'what did I do? Why did I do it for so long?'

"Even when I was injured, I still wanted to do the same thing because when I was at Brighton, if I got a little injury, that injury would take six weeks when it should have been two because of the way I was doing things. I wasn't doing everything properly.

"But listen, that's just the journey. If every player had the same journey, it would be a boring sport.

"I can never look back and say I wish I hadn't scored those two goals straight away, because I might not have ended up at Sunderland, for example, without that kind of CV behind me because of the impact I had early on at Brighton.

"It is difficult to think that I wish it hadn't happened. I do agree it probably was the worst thing that could have happened, looking back now, but at the time it felt like the best thing ever. And some of the issues probably would have come about eventually anyway."

Part of Connolly's journey has been overcoming an alcohol addiction, something he opened up about a couple of years ago on World Mental Health Day, while he was with Sunderland.

"I don't want to be that kind of player that's known for these types of issues, but also the reason I need to remember why I did the interview a few years ago was just to help as many people as possible," he says. "I know in my family my mum's dad struggled a lot with it, so I'd always happily speak about it.

"I'm in a good place with all of it. Obviously, it is a difficult thing to wake up and realise I can't do what a lot of my team-mates can do, which is go out on a Saturday, have maybe a couple of sociable drinks and go home. I know that's not me.

"Trying to find different things at the weekend to entertain myself when all I knew before was Saturday game, Saturday out, Sunday out. That was kind of what I'd do.

"I don't miss it, that's for sure. I have a better life and better relationships with people now without it.

"And my football, if I was drinking and going out, I wouldn't be able to do any of it, at any level.

"I wouldn't have the relationships I have with people. I always have time for everybody now, whereas before I was quite selfish and didn't have time for many people.

"I was probably miserable a lot in different clubs and different scenarios. It would be the only thing on my mind.

"I can't think about whether people like me or like what I've done or opened up about that situation. But for me, it helped, and all the messages and responses I received helped a lot of other people as well. That's what I've done it for."

Connolly is keen to now try and help younger players learn from his own pathway, even if those at Orient aren't quite exposed to what he was in the Premier League.

"Everyone needs to make mistakes," he says. "I'm not going to know all the answers straight away when I was 19 and getting paid all this money and playing in front of 50,000 every week.

"People are texting you saying, 'I can sort you this, I can do this for you', the minute you start playing in the Premier League.

"It took a lot of adjusting and getting used to that. I'm probably still not used to that kind of stuff because when you're growing up in Ireland, you don't get that kind of special treatment with things like that.

"But I always sit with the academy players in the physio room, and if they ever want to come and ask me something, they know they can. I'd like to think I'd give them as much advice as I can.

"They all want to play in the first team, but they're currently on loan in the Isthmian Premier, and they're thinking it's the end of the world. Why am I here at 18 or 19?

"I'll remind them that some journeys are completely different. I was in the Premier League at 19 and now I'm in League One. You might be in the Isthmian at 19 and could be in the Premier League by 26. There are different journeys.

"I always used to have a little bit of back and forth with Adam Webster at Brighton when I'd say, 'where were you at 19?' He was playing for Portsmouth in League One and Two. He'd say, 'well, I'm here at 26. Let's see where you are at 26'.

"He was right. They probably saw it coming before I did.

"I always try to give them that kind of advice. I know they do come in and sit with me and ask me questions, so I just want to be that kind of open space for them as well."

For Connolly, there is a lot of time to get back to where he believes he can be. Right now, he just wants to get a run of fitness and form with Leyton Orient.

Most importantly, he is determined to forge a career with no more regrets.

"A lot of people when I was 19 probably wouldn't have thought I'd be in League One, but that was probably one of the reasons why I'd end up dropping down the leagues to where I am now." he says.

"Like I said before, if this is going to be my level and this is where I end up at 35 or 36, then that's fine. At least I know I've given it everything, and then it's down to whether there are better players than me and better players in my position going ahead of me. I can accept that.

"But if I'd kept living the way I was and not appreciating the game, I'd probably look back when I'm 34 or 35 thinking, I wish I'd realised this 10 years earlier.

"I don't want to look back on my career with regret. You always hear that when I was 18, there used to be people coming into the training ground for educational talks and saying that.

"I'm just looking forward to seeing what I can do at 100 per cent fitness."

Germany 5-0 Netherlands: Jurgen Klopp sends clear message that spells trouble for Nations League rivals

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Oliver Thomas | 17 Nov 2026 09:46

Jurgen Klopp is in dreamland after witnessing a “truly extraordinary” performance from his Germany side in a statement 5-0 victory over the Netherlands in the UEFA Nations League on Monday.

The four-time world champions began the night sitting third in League A Group 2 and knew that they had to claim maximum points against the Oranje at the summit to secure top spot and qualify for the quarter-finals.

With so much at stake heading into the sixth and final matchday, Die Mannschaft rose to the occasion in prolific fashion, claiming their biggest win against their Dutch neighbours since 1959 when they won 7-0.

Derry Scherhant opened the scoring just before half time with a sensational maiden international goal, setting the tone for a dominant second-half display.

Overwhelmed by Germany’s relentless high press, the Netherlands had no answer as two goals from Florian Wirtz, and strikes from Nico Schlotterbeck and Brajan Gruda, sealed a statement triumph for Klopp’s side.

“What we’ve just witnessed was truly extraordinary,” the former Liverpool manager told reporters post-match. “The game was extraordinary, and incredibly difficult.”

“Against a really good opponent, you have to find a way to win. We had to adapt and make a few adjustments, some fairly significant ones, but we made them. We took away a lot of their strengths, and then we scored some incredible goals. That was unbelievable.

“It was a fantastic football match and a wonderful experience. I wouldn’t have ever dreamed of having the chance to do this in Berlin in front of a sold-out crowd. In the final international game of the year, it’s amazing.”

In addition to progressing to the quarter-finals, the result served as compelling evidence of Germany's renaissance and progress under Klopp, with his arrival fostering renewed optimism around his youthful squad.

Germany failed to win their opening two Nations League matches, but they ended their group campaign with 11 points (W3 D2 L1) and have conceded a competition-low three goals in six games.

"The moment I arrived here, I fell in love with this team at first sight,” said Klopp. “I'm at the beginning of this journey.

“I simply feel that we have a fantastic football team and a very good footballing idea that can work extremely well. If we can get these highly-talented players firing, then we're going to be a very difficult team to play against.

“We scored some incredible goals and, with increasing confidence, put in a great performance in front of a fantastic crowd. It’s been a lot of fun to work with this team, and we feel like we have a very special group of players here.

“But this is still only just the beginning – after six games, we can’t say that we’ve arrived where we want to be yet, as there are still lots of things we can improve on.”

"Everyone who was here today is going home with a smile, and won't be questioning the German national team for the next three or four months. That's a good thing," Klopp added when referring to the negativity that lingered previously around the national team.

Looking ahead to the quarter-final draw on Friday, Klopp added: "The lads can head home now with a smile on their faces as the group winners. We’ll watch the draw later this week and wait and see what sort of crunch match we’ll get, as there are only strong teams left.”

Germany, who have risen to 11th in the FIFA World Rankings, will play their two-legged quarter-final tie in March 2027, with a first-placed finish in League A rewarded with a second leg on home soil.

Freiburg’s Derry Scherhant celebrates dream Berlin homecoming

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

SC Freiburg attacker Derry Scherhant enjoyed an unforgettable evening in his hometown of Berlin on Monday, scoring Germany’s opening goal in a commanding 5-0 Nations League victory over the Netherlands. The 24-year-old subsequently reflected on his unlikely journey to the national team and took particular satisfaction in proving those who had once questioned his ability to become a professional footballer wrong.

Scherhant had arranged approximately 50 tickets for family members and friends ahead of the encounter at the Olympiastadion. The former Hertha BSC youngster, who grew up just five minutes from the venue, marked the occasion with an outstanding individual performance and a spectacular first-half goal.

Scherhant opened the scoring in the 41st minute after receiving a pass from Joshua Kimmich, cutting inside and dispatching a powerful effort into the upper-right corner. The Freiburg professional had already struck the outside of the post earlier in the half and subsequently played a decisive role in Germany’s fifth goal.

The performance represented another significant milestone in a remarkable international breakthrough that has gathered momentum since Jürgen Klopp took charge of the German national team.

As Get German Football News detailed in its comprehensive October feature on Scherhant’s unusual career trajectory, the winger’s development was anything but straightforward. Scherhant struggled to establish himself at Viktoria Berlin and Tennis Borussia before eventually finding his way into Hertha BSC’s academy in 2020. His early difficulties included repeated questions about his physical development and whether he possessed the necessary qualities to succeed at the professional level.

He ultimately earned a Bundesliga debut with Hertha, although regular opportunities remained elusive during the club’s turbulent relegation campaign in 2022/23. The subsequent spell in the 2. Bundesliga proved important to his development, and SC Freiburg eventually secured his services in the summer of 2025. The move to Breisgau provided Scherhant with a more stable environment, even if initially displacing established attacker Vincenzo Grifo presented another considerable challenge.

GGFN‘s 2026/27 tactical preview of SC Freiburg identified Scherhant as one of the club’s most impressive performers during preseason preparations. The former Hertha attacker subsequently consolidated his place in Julian Schuster’s starting XI.

A particularly impressive Bundesliga performance against Eintracht Frankfurt followed his first senior Germany nomination in September. Scherhant contributed a goal and an assist in the 2-2 draw, providing further evidence of his rapid development.

The remarkable progression continued at international level. Scherhant earned his senior Germany debut during Klopp’s first international window and impressed sufficiently to retain his place when the Bundestrainer reduced his squad ahead of November’s fixtures. Get German Football News subsequently reported on Scherhant and Freiburg teammate Philipp Treu’s appreciation of the supportive environment at their Bundesliga club. Both players credited Freiburg’s collective mentality and stable working conditions with helping them establish themselves at international level.

Scherhant also spoke enthusiastically about the prospect of representing Germany in Berlin. The winger celebrated his 24th birthday during the November international break and regarded the opportunity to return to the Olympiastadion as an extraordinary personal occasion. His performance against the Netherlands ultimately exceeded even those expectations.

The Freiburg attacker became only the third Berlin-born player to score for Germany in an international match staged in the capital, following Thomas Häßler against Bulgaria in November 1995 and Hans Ruch against Scotland in June 1929.

Klopp was particularly impressed by the contributions of Scherhant and fellow attacker Brajan Gruda. The Bundestrainer praised not only their technical abilities but also the considerable defensive work and intensity they contributed to Germany’s convincing victory.

Speaking after Monday’s victory, Scherhant recalled how frequently his chances of reaching professional football had been dismissed during his youth.

“A few years ago, people told me that I wouldn’t make it as a professional,” Scherhant explained. “Then I became a professional. And even then, some people were still saying that it wasn’t enough, that the Regionalliga was the highest level I’d ever reach. It’s simply unbelievable that I’m now standing here, with the satisfaction of having proved those people wrong. 99 percent would have said never. One percent might have said perhaps, if he works really hard.”

Klopp offered his own assessment of Scherhant’s contribution alongside Gruda.

“Derry Scherhant and Brajan Gruda, the way those two play football — my goodness,” Klopp remarked afterwards. “But the amount of work they put in and had to put in today was extraordinary. That’s what ultimately makes a team. Because there are other players who can play football too. But it’s the commitment and passion you bring to it that ultimately matter.”

Scherhant himself struggled to contain his delight after scoring in the stadium he once called home.

Newcomers to the ballot are in bold.

As a reminder, players must receive votes on at least 75% of the ballots cast to be inducted into the Hall of Fame with the 2027 class. Those who get at least 5% of the vote can remain on the ballot the following year, and so on, for a maximum of 10 voting cycles. Players who are on the ballot for 10 years without getting 75% of the vote, in addition to players not getting 5% of the vote, fall off the ballot in ensuing years, though that doesn't mean the doors to the Hall are permanently shut.

The results will be announced on Jan. 19, with the induction set for Cooperstown over the summer.

Here's everything else you need to know about the Baseball Hall of Fame this offseason.

As alluded to earlier, there are two different votes each offseason. The BBWAA vote is for former players who have been retired from Major League Baseball for at least five years. It's the most common method in mainstream Hall of Fame ballot discussions. When you hear stuff like "first-ballot Hall of Famer," it means via the BBWAA vote.

Players who don't make it via the BBWAA vote can get extra chances via committees. That's how, for example, Jeff Kent made it last year. Formerly called the Veterans Committee, the Hall of Fame now uses Era Committees, which are divided into two categories: contemporary baseball (1980 to present) and classic (prior to 1980). The contemporary ballot is then split into two, one for players and one for non-players like umpires, executives, pioneers and managers.

Sure enough, this time around, it is a non-player Contemporary Baseball Committee, which looks at "managers, umpires and executives whose most significant career impact was realized since 1980."

We'll obviously do a deeper dive in the coming weeks, but even without the longevity component (Posey only played in parts of 12 MLB seasons), I think he's in.

As for the rest of the first-timers, the best case would be Jon Lester. Given the way a lot of similar starting pitchers have been treated in voting in recent years, my hunch is he'll be able to hang around on the ballot, but he has a major uphill battle in order to get in.

Then you're looking at Brett Gardner, Ryan Zimmerman, Kyle Seager, the short peak of Jake Arrieta and relief work of Andrew Miller. This is the sixth year for Alex Rodriguez on the ballot, which means it's the sixth time that I point out that we don't really need much time to go over his case. His numbers say he's one of the 10 best position players of all time, but his ties to PEDs are keeping him out to this point.

Spain vs England was a game that had almost everything, and may yet mean more; perhaps the most significant moment might have come before it had even started.

The hosts took the opportunity of a first match back in Madrid since the World Cup to showcase the trophy, in what almost felt another homecoming.

The mood was very much of revelling in their embossed status, something that would have had a particular edge for this specific nation.

No country that has won the World Cup has waited as long for another trophy as England.

And, as the 2010 winners Iker Casillas, Fernando Torres and Fernando Llorente presented Unai Simon that with that precious piece of brilliant gold, it was almost literally in England’s faces.

Thomas Tuchel’s side had to watch the goalkeeper wave the World Cup about right in front of them.

It had been noted on the Spanish side that Jude Bellingham was the only player who clapped, this now being the city that he calls home with Real Madrid. He then played like he was demanding the same appreciation, and he was not the only one.

As one figure with knowledge of the England camp said: “They will have been thinking ‘that’s what we want’.”

Seeing the World Cup so close and yet so far, with more than three years until the next edition, only deepened a mood that had been felt in the build-up to this game.

England, in the words of both Declan Rice and Thomas Tuchel, knew they were going to win.

“I could feel the true belief in the dressing room before the match that this is exactly the moment where we want to prove ourselves and push our limits,” the manager said.

They had that conviction to show what they were really about, and to show they would be the only team in the world not cowed by Spain. It was that mentality that comes when you feel ready to beat a rival that has long had it over you; that has been a benchmark.

A pace was set just moments after seeing that trophy, as a ferocious England subjected Simon to a flurry.

Just as importantly, they displayed a new level of their own, even beyond Tuchel describing it as the best performance of his time.

This wasn’t, after all, the usual counter-attacking approach to playing Spain - at least not for when England actually got so far ahead.

It was England imposing their game, an evolving ideology, on Spain.

“We are well aware which steps we took after the World Cup,” Tuchel said, conspicuously repeating this more than once.

The manager was deeply stung after all the criticism following the Argentina defeat.

And yet, much as he might bristle about it in public, it does feel as if some of that criticism has been acknowledged.

England look much more technical, but still with pace.

Throughout the World Cup, after all, there was a definite sense of his team just seeking to force their way through.

This was something much more considered and coherent.

“We wanted to get better in the deep build-up, we wanted to get better at defending in a low block and on top of it, we wanted always to impose our identity and the way we want to play on every opponent,” Tuchel remarked.

This similarly feeds into another theme of the night, which was how James Trafford’s performance may start a path that sees him become England’s No 1.

It was conspicuous that, when asked about the goalkeeper’s brilliant late double-save, Tuchel immediately pointed to his pure football effect.

“But there was a lot to do in the deep build-up as well, to overcome the first line of press, which he did very well with everyone around him,” he said. “So there was a lot to do, never lose the concentration, never lose the focus, never lose the vision, to find the free man to overcome the press and put the game into the opponent’s half.”

Tuchel clearly wants to change the very focus of this team.

And, in doing so, he wants to change its entire outlook.

There was naturally a lot of talk about “confidence” after a victory like this, which Tuchel was only too happy to immerse himself in.

“You want confidence, not only through results but confidence through performance and the result follows,” Tuchel said. “That’s the ideal process and that’s what we got.”

When Spain beat France in the World Cup semi-final with what was the performance of the tournament - “the statement performance” as Tuchel would have it - some close to Didier Deschamps’s squad felt the reason for the win had actually come much earlier.

Spain had beaten France in a series of high-profile games, above all the Euro 2024 and World Cup 2026 semi-finals. Lamine Yamal had been right to emphasise that, because he knew it played on French minds.

It gave Spain an edge. They knew they could beat France, but France were unsure if they could beat Spain.

That’s also why the frantic ending to this game in Madrid, so at odds with the first hour, may have been so important.

Had Spain equalised, or potentially won, England would have just been another team to fail to beat them. It could similarly have been the deepening of England’s own complex about Spain, since this would have been three high-profile set-backs in succession.

Even if this had just been a draw, after all, it would have felt like a defeat given it was a 3-0 lead.

It was a win, and a first defeat that Spain have endured in 44 games over two and a half years.

As such, England are not just another team the Spanish are reigning over.

They’re a team that know how to beat them, that can carry that through to a home Euros.

It might even create a bit of consideration on the Spanish side, even allowing for how assured they are in their own approach.

It might be the beginning of a proper modern rivalry.

Might, of course. England have to go and use this to do what Spain have made customary, and actually win one of those trophies.

Why do birds flap before landing? It may be about more than slowing down

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

New calculations suggest birds flap during landing not just to control speed, but to stop within limited space.

Most birds flap their wings as they come in to land, but those final wingbeats may do more than slow them down. New calculations suggest they also help birds stop within a tight space.

A bird the size of a pigeon could glide to a complete stop with its wings held still.

To manage it, though, the bird would have to begin its landing maneuver at about 80 mph (36 meters per second). It would also have to climb roughly 65 feet (20 meters), the height of a six-story building.

Flapping, the researchers propose, lets a bird stop inside the space it has.

Tom Rottier carried out the study at the University of Manchester and the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies. He worked with Philippe Lavoie of Toronto and Ben Parslew of Manchester.

No birds were flown for the work. Instead, equations were written for a bird gliding on fixed wings.

Only gravity, lift, and air resistance acted on this model bird. The team then solved the equations for the slowest possible touchdown.

The forces on a wing depend on how fast the wing moves through the air. Those forces shrink as a bird slows down. Its control over where it goes shrinks with them.

Arriving fast is no better. Whatever speed is left at touchdown is usually absorbed by the legs. A bad landing can damage muscle and bone, or it can kill the bird.

The difficulty, said Parslew, an aerospace engineering lecturer at Manchester, is that “landing isn’t as simple as just slowing down.”

A wing also has a minimum working speed. In level flight, the bird’s whole weight has to be carried by lift. A wing that moves too slowly cannot make enough of it. Engineers call that minimum the stall speed.

The stall speed is not fixed, however, as the team’s equations show. A bird that climbs more steeply puts less of its weight on its wings. The stall speed drops as a result. In a climb straight up, it is zero.

A climb that ends straight up is how a gliding bird could stop without flapping. The bird comes in fast and pulls up. It then climbs more and more steeply while gravity and air resistance slow it down.

At the top, the bird is pointed straight up and has no speed left.

More lift also means more drag, and drag takes away the speed the climb depends on. For that reason, the tilt of the wings has to keep changing on the way up. By the end, the wings are turned broadside to the air.

The team calculated what such a landing would take for a rock pigeon weighing 14 ounces (400 grams).

Starting from level flight, the bird would need that 80 mph speed. This is more than double a pigeon’s typical cruising speed.

The bird would travel about 200 feet (60 meters) horizontally, roughly two-thirds of a football field, while climbing 65 feet.

City pigeons fly between buildings. A landing of that size is not feasible in many of those places, the team wrote. Bigger birds would need even more room.

The researchers therefore propose that flapping is not simply a way to slow down. A glide can do that. Flapping does it in a small space.

A perch can be approached from below, but the ground cannot. That changes the answer.

The researchers then tested landings in which the model bird could not climb at any point. No path brought it to a stop.

The slowest landing allowed by the equations came in at an angle of about 34 degrees below level.

A bird that glides down to the ground therefore arrives with some speed left. That speed has to be removed by flapping, by braking with the legs, or by both.

The legs need enough muscle and bone to absorb the impact. But that adds weight the bird must carry in flight.

The researchers suggest this trade-off may have shaped how birds evolved. The need to withstand landing impacts may have set a minimum strength for their legs.

Leftover speed may also help explain why ducks, geese, and swans land differently on water than on solid ground.

More of a bird’s energy is absorbed by water than by the ground. The bird can therefore arrive faster.

Some birds of prey do finish a landing without flapping.

A 2022 study observed Harris’s hawks flying between two perches. They learned to dive low and then glide up to the perch.

A wing that is tilted very steeply is stalled, meaning air no longer flows smoothly over it.

The authors of that study proposed an explanation for the swoop. In their view, it keeps the distance a hawk flies with stalled wings as short as possible.

Rottier’s team tested the idea by giving their model bird one goal at a time. For this test, the model bird was allowed to flap during the dive, as the hawks did.

First, it was told only to spend as little time stalled as possible. The model bird climbed and then came down onto the perch. It arrived at speeds that would harm a real hawk, the team wrote.

Next, the only goal was to land as slowly as possible. This time the model bird swooped deep.

A mix of the two goals generally matched the real hawks’ paths best. Touchdown speed did more than stall time to produce the swoop.

Diving builds speed, and speed allows a steeper climb. A steeper climb ends in a slower arrival.

The pigeon figures come from equations, not from a bird in the air. In its main form, the model has no wind, no body or tail, and no flapping.

A wing gets a brief boost in lift and drag when it tilts up quickly. The wings of landing birds do this, yet the boost is also left out of the model. To see how much it could matter, the team doubled the model’s maximum lift.

For a slow touchdown, the starting speed the bird needed fell by 44%. The forward distance fell by 65% and the climb by 55%. When the maximum drag was doubled instead, the speed needed went up.

A living bird may therefore be able to glide to a stop in less room than the pigeon numbers suggest. How much less cannot be said from the model.

The equations were written so that wind can be added later. The team also described the model as a base for studies of single species.

The same physics may apply to animals that never flap, such as flying squirrels and flying snakes. It may also apply to drones built to land on perches.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society A.
