Louisville Kings begin title defense at 2027 UFL Draft. Meet the picks

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Louisville Kings' quest for back-to-back championships begins Nov. 18 in St. Louis, with the first two rounds of the 2027 United Football League Draft.

With the eighth and final pick of Round 1, coach Chris Redman & Co. selected 6-foot-8, 320-pound former Arkansas offensive tackle Dalton Wagner.

"He's got a lot of toughness, length, speed," Redman told the NFL Network. "He's going to be a huge asset for our offense. We were hoping this guy would fall to us, so really glad he did."

The Kings also hold the final pick of the second round Wednesday.

Rounds 3-15 of the UFL draft will be held Nov. 19, beginning at 8:15 a.m. on the league's official YouTube page. The draft pool includes all 2026 NFL Draft-eligible players and players from the 2023, 2024 and 2025 draft classes who were on an NFL roster on or after June 14.

As the reigning champions, the Kings will kick off the 2027 UFL season March 26 at Lynn Family Stadium. Their opponent has not yet been announced, but the game is set for an 8 p.m. kickoff and will be broadcast on Fox.

Prior to the draft, the Kings protected 35 players, including quarterback Chandler Rogers. They also retained the rights to 15 players who earned spots on NFL rosters entering the 2026 season in the event they return to the UFL in the spring. As of mid-November, only two of those players remained with teams at the sport's highest level: defensive back Bryce Hall (Houston Texans) and wide receiver Lucky Jackson (Detroit Lions).

Karl-Anthony Towns’ ‘bittersweet’ Minnesota return comes as a Knicks champion

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

TORONTO — Karl-Anthony Towns is returning to Minnesota as an NBA champion, which solidifies the big trade as a Knicks victory but still feels "bittersweet" for the player.

"I wanted to win one there. I didn't accomplish that," Towns said ahead of Thursday's game against the Timberwolves. "But it was great to have those moments, to bring Minnesota back to the playoffs and be able to do a lot of things."

Towns spent nine seasons with the Timberwolves and served as the franchise star for most of them. There was a lot of losing but also an appearance in the conference finals, which was immediately followed by Towns' departure to New York in a 2024 blockbuster.

Although it often takes years to declare the winning side of a trade, this one can already be called in the Knicks' favor:

New York Knicks center Karl-Anthony Towns smiles after receiving his championship ring before a game on Tuesday, Oct. 20, 2026, at Madison Square Garden. Charles Wenzelberg / New York Post

●  Julius Randle, who was shipped to Minnesota for Towns, was traded in the summer to the Nets after a couple of clunky seasons with the Timberwolves. He was basically a salary dump.

●  Donte DiVincenzo, who was also sent to Minnesota for Towns, has played well when healthy but has been out since April with a torn Achilles.

●  With the 2025 first-round pick they acquired in the trade, the Timberwolves selected French center Joan Beringer at No. 17. Beringer is still only 20 years old and has appeared in just 55 games, including 15 this season.

●  Towns won a title with the Knicks, played in two All-Star Games and is playing spectacularly to start this campaign. Thursday, he comes back as a champ.

"It's going to be bittersweet," he said. "I'm just happy that I have such amazing fans and had such an amazing time in Minnesota. I love it to death. I love that place so it's going to be cool to go back."

Minnesota Timberwolves center Karl-Anthony Towns reacts after a foul call against New York Knicks forward Julius Randle on Monday, Jan. 1, 2024, in New York. Charles Wenzelberg/New York Post

The Knicks entered Toronto with a 1-6 record in close games.

They left with a 3-6 mark, and there were multiple examples of New York coming through in the clutch.

The Knicks shot 8-for-9 on free throws (89 percent) during clutch time in the two victories over the Raptors; in their seven previous close games, the Knicks shot 6-for-10 (60 percent) from the stripe during clutch time.

Sometimes a free throw — or two — can make all the difference.

Jose Alvarado logged seven productive minutes in Tuesday's victory, which followed a string of four straight games completely out of the rotation before he played 2:22 in the first half Sunday.

Alvarado's insertion in Toronto sped up the offense. As proof, the point guard picked up four assists during the cameo.

Jose Alvarado of the New York Knicks plays against the Toronto Raptors during an NBA game in Toronto, Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026. Anadolu via Getty Images

Knicks coach Mike Brown credited assistant coach Rick Brunson with the adjustment.

"Just a great call. Rick Brunson made the call of throwing Jose in to see if he can give us a spark," Brown said. "So, just a heckuva call by Rick Brunson and just a fantastic job by Jose producing on both sides of the ball when his name was called."

Towns, as the facilitator at the hub, is still producing for the Knicks. His turnovers are down over the past seven games, while the assists are up.

Brown said the center has achieved a better understanding of when to throw the home-run pass to cutters and when to accept the safer play.

"It's a fine balance because he's capable of making some beautiful passes in tight windows so you want to let him be him," Brown said. "But I thought earlier in the year — and we talked to him about it, and said it a couple times — he was trying to make the home-run pass a little too much.

"And he's gotten to a point where he's mixing it up now. … We're going to trust him because he's obviously done it for many years now with good results. We'll lean on him quite a bit in that regard but if we turn the ball over too much, we'll let him know."

bjarke ingels group proposes four timber buildings in thun, switzerland with stepped hotel terraces overlooking the alps.

Bjarke Ingels Group / BIG unveils plans for four timber buildings in Thun, Switzerland, where the architecture firm will help shape the entrance to the city’s emerging Siegenthalergut district. The 13,000-square-meter development brings together two hotels and residential buildings, with stacked wooden volumes that project outward above the street. Set against the Bernese Alps, the architecture is defined by deep window grids and large terraces carved into the buildings’ stepped facades.

The proposal, developed with landscape architects August + Margrith Künzel, was selected through a competition organized by Frutiger AG and the city of Thun. Titled Nachwachsende Stadt, or Growing City, the scheme occupies one of the first two plots planned for the new neighborhood. The site sits opposite a sports center and the future headquarters of the Swiss Football Association to give the BIG-designed buildings a prominent position along the city’s western entrance.

At the southwest corner of the site, the team at BIG brings a three-star and a four-star hotel together under one roof. Shared amenity spaces cut diagonally through the structure, creating a series of setbacks that interrupt its otherwise regular timber grid. These shifts make room for outdoor terraces overlooking the neighboring sports facilities, with the Bernese Alps rising in the distance. A restaurant at the top of the building will give guests an even broader view of the surrounding landscape.

At street level, a cantilevered corner marks the main hotel entrance, opening onto a drop-off plaza beneath the projecting upper floors. Commercial spaces line the adjacent boulevard, bringing activity to the building’s ground floor. Across the facade, large square windows sit within deeply framed timber modules, while recessed portions reveal warmer, sheltered spaces behind the exterior grid. The alternating volumes give the hotel a sculptural profile that changes depending on where it is viewed from.

To the east, the remaining timber buildings accommodate residences with flexible layouts and projecting bays. Here, the same geometric language takes on a more domestic scale. Balconies extend outward from the facades, while recessed loggias create sheltered outdoor rooms. The shifting arrangement of these openings gives each elevation a different rhythm, with the projecting volumes bringing daylight deeper into the apartments.

The residential buildings face an internal courtyard, where winding paths move through gardens and planted gathering spaces. Renderings picture grassy areas beneath the timber facades, with balconies overlooking the shared landscape. Small plazas and seating areas are distributed throughout the development, connecting the homes to the surrounding neighborhood.

Of the project’s total 13,000 square meters, approximately 6,500 are dedicated to residential uses, alongside 6,000 square meters of hotel space and another 400 square meters for commercial activities.

The use of timber extends across all four buildings, establishing a consistent material language while helping reduce the development’s embodied carbon footprint. BIG has designed the timber elements for future disassembly and reuse, allowing components to be recovered as the buildings change over time. The deep balconies and roof overhangs also serve a practical purpose, providing natural shade in summer and shelter during the colder months.

Green roofs and rainwater retention systems will help manage water across the site, while approximately 1,800 square meters of photovoltaic panels are planned to generate renewable energy. The development is targeting Switzerland’s SNBS Gold sustainability certification.

The winning proposal will now progress through further design and approval stages, with construction of the district’s first buildings possible from 2028. For BIG, which opened its Zurich office in 2023, the project adds another timber-focused development to its growing Swiss portfolio, alongside the planned Dock A terminal at Zurich Airport.

49ers might have just outsmarted the Vikings with unique travel plan for Week 11 NFC showdown in Mexico City

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

For the second time this season, the San Francisco 49ers are set to play an international game, and once again, their travel schedule could end up being a key factor in the game's outcome.

Back in Week 1, the 49ers were sent to Australia to play the Rams, and the two teams took opposite approaches to travel. The 49ers headed to Melbourne a full week before the game while the Rams showed up just 24 hours before kickoff. By the time the game kicked off, the 49ers were fully acclimated to the 17-hour time difference, and it showed: San Francisco won 27-7.

The 49ers outsmarted the Rams with their travel plan in Week 1, and they might try to do the same this week against the Vikings in Mexico City.  Mexico's capital creates a unique challenge for NFL teams because it sits at an elevation of about 7,350 feet. To put that in perspective, Denver has the highest altitude of any NFL city at 5,280 feet, over a quarter-mile of elevation below what the 49ers and Vikings will be dealing with in Mexico.

When you're playing at an altitude that high, it's not easy to get acclimated, so the 49ers have decided to spend their entire practice week in Colorado Springs at Air Force Academy's Falcon Stadium, which sits at 6,621 feet.

Niners coach Kyle Shanahan was raised in Denver, so he knows how difficult it can be to adjust to high altitude, which is why he decided to move the team's base to Colorado this week.

"I think it helps," Shanahan said this week, when discussing practice at a higher altitude. "I grew up mainly in Denver, and I always know how bad it would be for me. It didn't matter how good a shape you're in. Like, when I came home from college, you'd run up the stairs and be out of breath. It takes some time to get used to it. It just is what it is with altitude. I know when you go to play in Denver, it's always a challenge, and I know Mexico City's higher than Denver. It's not make-or-break by any means, but I do think it makes you a couple percent better."

The 49ers have shown that they're willing to adjust their travel plan for the chance to be a "couple percent" better. They did it for Australia, and now, they're doing it for this trip. This will mark the NFL's first game in Mexico City since 2022, and the 49ers also played in that game. Four years ago, they also spent a full week in Colorado before heading to Mexico, and it apparently helped, because they ended up beating the Cardinals 38-10.

On the Vikings' end, they won't be making any changes to their practice location to prepare for the high altitude in Mexico. Instead, the Vikings will spend the week at their practice facility in Eagan, Minnesota, which sits at an altitude of 945 feet.

Minnesota might not be adjusting its travel schedule, but the team did install an altitude chamber to help players acclimate.

The chamber allows the players to get a workout in at a "high" altitude, but riding a stationary bike or running on a treadmill isn't exactly the same thing as practicing actual football.

Defensive coordinator Brian Flores previously coached a game in Mexico City as a member of the Patriots' coaching staff in 2017 — a game New England won 33-8 over the Raiders — so he has some first-hand experience with what the trip will be like. During an interview on Tuesday, Flores admitted he'll probably have to change his substitution patterns since the high altitude will likely cause his players to get winded faster.

"There's less oxygen, it's a tough place to play," Flores said of Mexico City. "It's certainly taxing. As a staff, we're already talking about, 'Hey, we're going have to rotate these guys and everybody's going to have to be ready to play.' And so, yeah, it's certainly something that we're going to have to deal with."

Flores has faced Shanahan three times as a head coach or defensive coordinator, and he's won all three games, but this presents a different challenge. The Vikings have had the best defense in the NFL this year, but it will be interesting to see if the altitude changes that. If the defense is winded, it will be tough for them to keep up with San Francisco's offense.

Although the 49ers should be acclimated to the altitude by the time they arrive in Mexico City, the same can't be said for Minnesota. The Vikings will be stealing a page from the Rams' playbook when it comes to traveling this week. The Rams showed up in Australia one day before the game, and that's exactly what the Vikings plan on doing, with the team arriving on Saturday.

This a huge game that could ultimately decide the No. 1 seed in the NFC and the 49ers have decided not to leave anything to chance. The 49ers and Vikings kick off at 8:20 p.m. ET on Sunday night in what will be the final international game of the NFL season.

This year, the new NFL meta will be defined Sunday night in Mexico City, where the San Francisco 49ers and Shanahan will face the Minnesota Vikings and defensive coordinator Brian Flores in one of the most anticipated matchups of the year. Both the Niners' offense and Vikings' defense are resetting what their respective sides can and should do, and seeing which side blinks first could be a blueprint for how offenses and defenses attack each other in the near future.

If we recall a story I wrote earlier this season, NFL teams are blitzing at an astronomical rate. Over ten weeks, defenses league-wide have a 32.3% blitz rate, the second-highest since 2010 and one of only four seasons with a blitz rate over 30% (2011, 2013, and 2010). The number jumps even higher when teams go under center to an astounding 50.5%, the highest since 2010 and the only season TruMedia has recorded in that time period with a blitz rate over 50% against under-center offenses. When these defenses blitz, they have a 54.9% defensive success rate overall, and that number drops to 50.1% when offenses are under center.

At the forefront of this are Flores and the Minnesota Vikings, who are redefining what it means to be aggressive. The Vikings currently sit with an absurd 72.6% blitz rate, by far the highest for any team through 10 weeks since 2010. In fact, you have to go all the way back to the 2006 Miami Dolphins to find a team that went higher than that (what was Nick Saban cooking in 2006?). The way that Minnesota can dictate the flow of not only a play, but the entire game through their pressure looks that completely stymie opposing offenses.

The Vikings are essentially playing rock-paper-scissors on every play, daring you to throw anything other than rock. One way they can dictate the flow is by forcing teams to leave extra pass protectors in to help against pressure looks.

The alarm bells are ringing. This is the most obvious Cover 0 all-out blitz look, eight guys up at the line of scrimmage. The common answer for the Packers and Love has been to keep the RB and TE in the protection, calling it max protection. This is the Packers throwing rock, hoping to catch the Vikings in a Cover 0 look and hit them for an explosive.

The Vikings actually spin everyone out of this except the four defensive linemen and run Cover 3, frazzling Jordan Love before he ends up throwing it 10 yards short of the first-down marker on third down. The Vikings got the Packers to throw rock, then they throw paper.

This ability to force offenses to keep seven people in to protect the QB is unprecedented at the NFL level. According to Sumer Sports, the 2026 Vikings are facing dropbacks where all five eligible receivers are out to catch a pass on 35.7% of their dropbacks, the lowest in the NFL by a significant margin. They're the only team in the NFL below 50% in that metric, with the New York Giants in second place…at 58.5%! Flores and the defense force offenses to play a completely different sport when they play Minnesota, using pressure to dictate the terms of the passing game. They force you to waste backs and tight ends in pass protection, then force you to play with a hand behind your back.

You're probably wondering, "Yes, the Vikings defense is amazing, but what does that have to do with the 49ers and how this will decide the state of NFL schematics?" The answer lies in the protection plans. The Vikings see the lowest amount of five-out in the passing concept in the NFL. Well, guess which team leads the NFL in percentage of dropbacks with all five eligible receivers out in the passing concept? That's right, Kyle Shanahan and the San Francisco 49ers.

I'd describe the Niners' offense as "malleable." They can use multiple players in multiple ways, keeping their best players on the field while putting linebackers in a blender. They do that by getting all five guys out in the passing concept. If we include chip blocks, the Niners get all five eligibles out at an 80.1% clip, the highest in the NFL (not including chips, that number drops to 60.8%, second in the NFL behind the Pittsburgh Steelers). Against pressure looks where linebackers are mugged, or showing that they're blitzing, the 49ers like to get everyone into the concept and spread teams out. The Niners (and Bengals as well) are zagging when everyone else is zigging, spreading out and getting five eligibles into the concept against the blitz.

With simulated pressures, there's always a law of equivalent exchange: for every guy you add, a guy has to drop. For most defenses to stay structurally intact while showing these pressures in two-high shells, someone has to drop from the defensive line. Shanahan knows this, and knows he has the personnel to live in five-out looks (we'll get to that later). If we're going to the rock, paper, scissors comparison again, the Niners are choosing to throw paper instead of rock, because if they can find the weakest point in the pressure, they'll hammer it.

Instead of checking into max protection, they stay with what they have, giving RB Christian McCaffrey a choice route on the backside. Cowboys spin out and drop into Cover 2, but CMC has already found the hole, and Brock Purdy finds him to move the chains.

The reason the 49ers can live this way is not only because of Kyle Shanahan, but because of the personnel on the field. LT Trent Williams is a mute button at left tackle. Having him on the field lets the rest of the line slide the other way and leave Williams on an island. Having McCaffrey also helps in a big way: a guy who can run the offense as well as anyone and run choice routes against zone coverage. But this all comes together because of Brock Purdy and his ability to create out of structure. If the Niners go to five-man protection, and there's someone hot off the edge, Purdy is trusted to make them miss and extend the play. The 2024 tilt is more accurate, but even then it feels like watching two completely different teams. The Vikings weren't leaning nearly as much into their blitzing (the 2024 Vikings only had a 42% blitz rate, still a league high then but a far cry from where we are right now). Conversely, the Niners' offense feels more mature now than it was in 2024, with Purdy playing at a much higher level (plus, McCaffrey didn't play). It's a testament to how much both coordinators have grown and how their respective teams have grown with them, to become the Charizard version they both are today.

This battle between the two most prominent minds in the sport feels like it's going to set the trends for what teams will look like in the future. Already this season, we've seen teams lean more into seven- and eight-man fronts at the line of scrimmage, trying to force offenses to condense their formations and limit receiving options. The best offenses, however, have decided to spread out and get all five eligibles into the passing skeleton, forcing defenses to show their hand and find eligible playmakers in space.

Two of the most prominent minds at the forefront of this push-and-pull will go to battle in Mexico City. What happens during the game could shape how teams approach offensive and defensive design.

It was obvious that the Belgium international’s talent belonged at one of Europe’s elite clubs.

Sure enough, at the end of the season, he abruptly ended years of protracted tabloid speculation and announced on Twitter that he was joining the champions of Europe, Chelsea.

But while Roberto Di Matteo’s Blues played out that final in Munich, Hazard was still in France, out on the lash one last time with his Lille team-mates.

“We were certain to finish third and we were facing Nancy the following day,” former Lille captain Rio Mavuba later revealed. “It was Eden Hazard’s last match with Lille and he wanted to organise a little something.

“We decided to go have a little drink. A little drink that dragged on somewhat. The following morning, Eden was still drunk. That evening against Nancy, not even with 30 minutes played, Eden had already bagged a hat-trick.”

There was certainly no sign of a hangover come kick-off, with a boyish grin as he looked out across a packed-out Stadium Lille Metropole – also Lille’s last game there, after an eight-year exile in the out-of-town athletics venue – full of banners reading ‘Merci Eden’ among Belgian flags, showing their love for a 21-year-old who’d already become one of the greatest players in the club’s history.

Brazilian striker Tulio de Melo led the line, while Hazard supported from the right, with Dimitri Payet on the opposite flank, Joe Cole operating in the No.10 role, future PSG man Idrissa Gueye at the base of midfield and 18-year-old academy graduate Lucas Digne making an early appearance at left-back.

But Lille weren’t going to allow Hazard’s farewell party to be ruined, and soon enough Payet produced a trademark silky touch to turn his man before nutmegging another Nancy defender to tee up De Melo, who tapped home to regain their lead in the 21st minute.

Nancy had enough to worry about with a young Hazard at the peak of his powers. Facing an in-the-mood Payet as well? That’s just cruel.

Although Craig has his partisans, Connery tends to be the consensus choice for the best Bond, though I’ve argued here for the merits of 1970s and ’80s Roger Moore. All the Bonds have their virtues, but Connery benefited from being there first — for anyone who discovered the series in its initial heyday, Connery simply is James Bond. Yet Connery actually wasn’t the first actor to play Bond. That honor goes to journeyman MGM contract player Barry Nelson, who played Bond in a “Casino Royale” adaptation eight years before Connery made the character famous in “Dr. No.” Connery may have been the first 007 to grace the big screen, but Nelson established Bond on the small screen in an episode of the CBS anthology series “Climax!” opposite Peter Lorre in the Le Chiffre role that would later be inhabited by Orson Welles (in the goofy 1967 “Casino Royale”) and Mads Mikkelsen (in the 2006 adaptation that introduced Craig to the series).

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is a fascinating artifact that includes elements that would go on to characterize future James Bond movies, while also going in a few other very different directions. The key difference is in the scale, stripped down by necessity for television production. The hour-long “Climax!” episode is all interiors, taking place entirely in the hotel and casino of the title. It’s more a James Bond play than a movie, with Bond and Le Chiffre facing off in a series of verbal — and ultimately physical — jousts in confined spaces, and it’s surprisingly effective.

It’s also a very strange beginning for a franchise that would come to be known for its globetrotting locales and elaborate action set pieces. Not that this “Casino Royale” was officially part of the James Bond franchise as we know it. In fact, although there have been three adaptations of Ian Fleming’s first James Bond novel, only the third incarnation with Daniel Craig was from EON Productions, the company that controlled the character from “Dr. No” through the Craig era before selling the rights to Amazon.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” came about when producer Gregory Ratoff optioned the rights to Fleming’s novel in May of that year. The novel wasn’t yet a hit, and Fleming was sorely in need of cash, which led him to make one of the worst deals in movie-rights history when he sold the book outright to Ratoff for $6,000 after the TV episode aired. This left “Casino Royale” out of the deal when EON secured the screen rights to James Bond as a character, so Bond producers Albert Broccoli and Harry Saltzman could only sit back and grit their teeth when Ratoff turned “Casino Royale” into a comedy in 1967 and cast Peter Sellers, David Niven, Woody Allen and others as rotating Bonds.

EON ultimately made a deal to bring “Casino Royale” under their umbrella and used the novel to reboot the franchise with Craig, but in 1954 it served as the source for a much more modest affair. The biggest similarities between the 1954 “Climax!” episode and the Daniel Craig movie come in the time spent on extended gambling sequences, and the centering of a brutal torture scene. Given censorship restrictions of the time, Nelson’s torture in a bathtub is considerably less graphic than the mallet to the testicles inflicted on Craig, but it’s rough enough stuff for a 1950s TV production.

Certain aspects of Nelson’s characterization, like Bond’s mix of sexual swagger and icy indifference, would later be adopted by most future 007s, to varying degrees. The biggest difference between Nelson and future Bonds is a simple one: He’s American. Although Broccoli and Saltzman flirted with the idea of an American Bond throughout the series’ history (going so far as to cast James Brolin in “Octopussy” before Moore decided to return at the last minute), to date they’ve never actually pulled the trigger on casting outside the UK.

Nelson is probably best known to modern audiences as the affable hotel manager Stuart Ullman in Stanley Kubrick’s “The Shining,” but in the 1950s he was a sturdy character actor and occasional leading man who bounced around television after an initial sojourn as a studio contract player. As a young man, he appeared in several important MGM films, including “Shadow of the Thin Man,” “Bataan,” and “A Guy Named Joe” (the source material for Steven Spielberg’s “Always”). Later on, he popped up in “Airport” and had a successful Broadway career. He was nominated for a Tony acting opposite Liza Minnelli in Martin Scorsese’s 1977 production “The Act.”

In 1954, though, he was in the middle of a prolific and productive era as a TV stalwart, both as a series regular (on “The Hunter” and “My Favorite Husband”) and a frequent guest star. He doesn’t exhibit a ton of range as American “Combined Intelligence” agent “Jimmy” Bond, but that too is in keeping with the overall conception of the character, at least until Craig tried to bring a little more emotional depth to the role.

In Americanizing Bond, the “Climax!” edition of “Casino Royale” ends up making a number of related changes that are small but make the film feel just a bit off for fans of the EON productions. American Felix Leiter (later played by Jeffrey Wright) becomes Brit Clarence Leiter, for example. Although there’s none of the Bond gadgetry that would become famous later, there are hints of what’s to come in the form of props like a villain’s cane that hides a gun, and the opening credits for the “Climax!” series itself feature a gun barrel pointed at the viewer that predicts the iconic graphics of later Bond title sequences.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is noteworthy for another reason: its composer, Jerry Goldsmith, who would ultimately score hundreds of major films, including “Planet of the Apes,” “Chinatown,” “Basic Instinct,” and most of Joe Dante’s work. Yet in some ways what’s fascinating about “Casino Royale” is how unremarkable it is — it’s a decent, solid piece of entertainment from an era when proliferating anthology shows provided similar programs every week.

If Fleming had his way, it would have only been the start for 007 on television; he wrote several outlines for episodes of a James Bond series before CBS canceled the project. Luckily, he repurposed most of what he wrote as short stories for his 1960 book “For Your Eyes Only,” and television’s loss was cinema’s gain. By 1962, James Bond belonged to the movies — and to Sean Connery.

'I didn't want to look back on my career with regret' - the journey of Aaron Connolly

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Aaron Connolly speaks to Sky Sports about his breakthrough at Brighton and the pitfalls and setbacks that followed on his journey in the game. He is now rebuilding in League One at Leyton Orient and determined to forge a career he can be proud of.

It is easy to forget that Aaron Connolly is still only 26, considering everything he has already experienced.

Just over seven years have passed since that magical full Premier League debut, scoring twice for Brighton against Spurs at the age of 19 in 2019.

In the time since, there have been injuries that have damaged his progress in football and obstacles in his life beyond the pitch.

"It's been tough," Connolly - now at Leyton Orient - tells Sky Sports. "It's probably been well documented that I haven't dealt with setbacks very well before.

"I love playing football. It's what I've done my whole life. So when I can't, it's obviously difficult.

"I've had a spell of injuries and interruptions. It seems to have been the way for the last three or four years of my career.

"At Hull, I was doing well, scoring goals, then got an injury and didn't really get back in.

"Then I left Hull, went to Sunderland, started one of my first games against Millwall, scored and then picked up a little injury and missed the next game. It's just that momentum I never seemed to get.

"Then obviously last year [at Orient] I had that momentum for about 15 to 20 games. I started scoring and assisting, and then tore my quad. I was in a bit of a rush to come back and then obviously did my shoulder and needed surgery again.

"Hopefully, I'm doing everything I can to stay injury-free right now, but with football you never know. There could just be that one late tackle or slip or something. Hopefully, fingers crossed, I'm past that stage now."

Connolly knows, injuries aside, he hasn't always helped himself. Temptations and distractions away from the pitch halted the progress in his late teens and early 20s.

A reputation was built that he is now working hard to shake off.

"There's always going to be football fans who have a certain opinion of you without knowing you," he admits. "I burst onto the scene a few years ago, and it feels like a long time ago now.

"There are people at the highest level because they live and breathe football and do everything properly. I didn't do that.

"I probably worked as hard as I could to get to that position to score those goals against Tottenham. After that, I kind of came away from everything I'd done to get myself in that position.

"Now, obviously, I've realised, and I'm hoping it's not too late to have realised, that kind of lifestyle and that attitude towards the game isn't going to get me very far.

"I probably believed the hype straight away and thought I'd scored two on my full debut, so I'm going to score 30 in the Premier League without dedicating myself to it, being in the gym, being first in and last to leave.

"I let myself down a lot, and that's probably why I ended up getting injured.

"I'd be late into training, be the first one to leave, never really stay and work on my craft or anything. I kind of thought I'd signed a long-term deal here now, so I've got five years to play with. That five years just went, and every footballer will tell you it goes quickly. It does.

"I've realised now that I need to dedicate myself to football. Sometimes when I'm at the training ground now, I come in first and leave last, and I'm sitting there thinking, 'why has it only clicked for me now that that's what you need to do in football?'

"Probably from 23 to 25 years old, I was just looking back on when I was 19, thinking, 'what did I do? Why did I do it for so long?'

"Even when I was injured, I still wanted to do the same thing because when I was at Brighton, if I got a little injury, that injury would take six weeks when it should have been two because of the way I was doing things. I wasn't doing everything properly.

"But listen, that's just the journey. If every player had the same journey, it would be a boring sport.

"I can never look back and say I wish I hadn't scored those two goals straight away, because I might not have ended up at Sunderland, for example, without that kind of CV behind me because of the impact I had early on at Brighton.

"It is difficult to think that I wish it hadn't happened. I do agree it probably was the worst thing that could have happened, looking back now, but at the time it felt like the best thing ever. And some of the issues probably would have come about eventually anyway."

Part of Connolly's journey has been overcoming an alcohol addiction, something he opened up about a couple of years ago on World Mental Health Day, while he was with Sunderland.

"I don't want to be that kind of player that's known for these types of issues, but also the reason I need to remember why I did the interview a few years ago was just to help as many people as possible," he says. "I know in my family my mum's dad struggled a lot with it, so I'd always happily speak about it.

"I'm in a good place with all of it. Obviously, it is a difficult thing to wake up and realise I can't do what a lot of my team-mates can do, which is go out on a Saturday, have maybe a couple of sociable drinks and go home. I know that's not me.

"Trying to find different things at the weekend to entertain myself when all I knew before was Saturday game, Saturday out, Sunday out. That was kind of what I'd do.

"I don't miss it, that's for sure. I have a better life and better relationships with people now without it.

"And my football, if I was drinking and going out, I wouldn't be able to do any of it, at any level.

"I wouldn't have the relationships I have with people. I always have time for everybody now, whereas before I was quite selfish and didn't have time for many people.

"I was probably miserable a lot in different clubs and different scenarios. It would be the only thing on my mind.

"I can't think about whether people like me or like what I've done or opened up about that situation. But for me, it helped, and all the messages and responses I received helped a lot of other people as well. That's what I've done it for."

Connolly is keen to now try and help younger players learn from his own pathway, even if those at Orient aren't quite exposed to what he was in the Premier League.

"Everyone needs to make mistakes," he says. "I'm not going to know all the answers straight away when I was 19 and getting paid all this money and playing in front of 50,000 every week.

"People are texting you saying, 'I can sort you this, I can do this for you', the minute you start playing in the Premier League.

"It took a lot of adjusting and getting used to that. I'm probably still not used to that kind of stuff because when you're growing up in Ireland, you don't get that kind of special treatment with things like that.

"But I always sit with the academy players in the physio room, and if they ever want to come and ask me something, they know they can. I'd like to think I'd give them as much advice as I can.

"They all want to play in the first team, but they're currently on loan in the Isthmian Premier, and they're thinking it's the end of the world. Why am I here at 18 or 19?

"I'll remind them that some journeys are completely different. I was in the Premier League at 19 and now I'm in League One. You might be in the Isthmian at 19 and could be in the Premier League by 26. There are different journeys.

"I always used to have a little bit of back and forth with Adam Webster at Brighton when I'd say, 'where were you at 19?' He was playing for Portsmouth in League One and Two. He'd say, 'well, I'm here at 26. Let's see where you are at 26'.

"He was right. They probably saw it coming before I did.

"I always try to give them that kind of advice. I know they do come in and sit with me and ask me questions, so I just want to be that kind of open space for them as well."

For Connolly, there is a lot of time to get back to where he believes he can be. Right now, he just wants to get a run of fitness and form with Leyton Orient.

Most importantly, he is determined to forge a career with no more regrets.

"A lot of people when I was 19 probably wouldn't have thought I'd be in League One, but that was probably one of the reasons why I'd end up dropping down the leagues to where I am now." he says.

"Like I said before, if this is going to be my level and this is where I end up at 35 or 36, then that's fine. At least I know I've given it everything, and then it's down to whether there are better players than me and better players in my position going ahead of me. I can accept that.

"But if I'd kept living the way I was and not appreciating the game, I'd probably look back when I'm 34 or 35 thinking, I wish I'd realised this 10 years earlier.

"I don't want to look back on my career with regret. You always hear that when I was 18, there used to be people coming into the training ground for educational talks and saying that.

"I'm just looking forward to seeing what I can do at 100 per cent fitness."
