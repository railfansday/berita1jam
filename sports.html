In His Own Words, a Czech Alpinist Describes How He Survived a Nightmare on Peru’s Highest Mountain

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Honza Špička on Huascarán (Photo: Courtesy of Matěj Žák)

My name is Jan “Honza” Špička. My climbing partner, Matěj Žák, and I are two alpinists, climbers, mountaineers, skiers, and endurance athletes from Nove Straseci, a small town in the Czech Republic. Although we have known each other since childhood through our families (and the local ice hockey team), our climbing paths only crossed a few years ago. Since then, we have spent three seasons together in Peru’s Cordillera Blanca and two seasons in Argentine Patagonia. We have roped up together a few times on Czech sandstone as well. We are no professionals, though; rather than extreme lines, what we have behind us are moderately difficult routes climbed in our own style: clean and fast.

Our speciality? Long days in the mountains and a fast, lightweight approach. We take nothing on an expedition that we cannot carry on our own backs.

Huascarán is the highest mountain in Peru and the highest point in the entire tropics. Its south summit reaches 6,768m and the north summit 6,655m; the two are separated by a broad col called La Garganta (“the Throat” in Spanish) at roughly 6,000m. Huascarán lies at the heart of the Cordillera Blanca, the most heavily glaciated tropical mountain range in the world, and it gave its name to the surrounding national park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1985. Because the Earth bulges slightly at the equator, the gravitational acceleration on the summit of Huascarán is among the lowest anywhere on the planet’s surface; a person there measurably weighs a little less than at sea level, by a matter of grams. The summit of Huascarán is also the second most distant point on Earth from the planet’s center, right after Chimborazo.

The mountain is named after Huáscar, the Inca ruler who fought his half-brother Atahualpa for several years over control of the Inca Empire. Atahualpa eventually defeated his brother and had him executed. The American mountaineer Annie Smith Peck, then over fifty years old, climbed the north summit in 1908. The higher south summit held out until 1932, when a German–Austrian expedition made the first ascent.

Huascarán, however, also has a darker side to its history. In 1962, a huge mass of ice broke away from the mountain, and the resulting debris avalanche swept down onto the towns of Ranrahirca. Then, on May 31, 1970, an earthquake off the coast triggered a far larger and more extensive avalanche from the north summit, which within minutes buried, among other places, the entire town of Yungay. The earthquake and avalanche combined took the lives of approximately 67,000 people. It was the deadliest single avalanche disaster by death toll in recorded history.

This tragic event unfortunately also carries a strong Czechoslovak mark. It was right here, in the valley between Huascarán’s north summit and Huandoy Sur, that a Czechoslovak expedition was camped—and the whole expedition became a victim of this mountain.

If we take into account the victims of 1970 and all the climbers who have died on its slopes, it is probably the mountain responsible for more human lives than any other in modern history.

The ascent itself, via the Normal Route, is not particularly difficult, but Huascarán’s glaciers are retreating fast, and so today some routes that were relatively straightforward climbs just a generation ago involve significant objective hazards. In the six years that I have lived in Peru, I cannot remember a year when Huascarán did not claim another victim. There have even been years when it allowed no one at all to reach its summit.

The start of our trip began on Friday, July 11, in the evening in our favourite “pollo a la brasa” restaurant. Our whole group, which we called the “Cayesh crew,” met for a shared dinner to load up on a solid dose of protein before what was to be the main climbing objective of the season for all of us: Huascarán Sur. It has to be said, though, that for none of us was Huascarán the center of the universe. We all had it in “one day, maybe, I’d like to, if it comes to it…” mode, but there was certainly no obsession with this “fat one.” I already had three unsuccessful attempts behind me, but even so I wasn’t insisting on “bagging it.” Yet somehow the appetite, the motivation, the hunger for it arrived. And essentially by coincidence it arrived not only for Matěj and me but also for our friends from Peru. In the end we formed three teams of two.

I have known Saul—formally, Freddy Saul Mendoza Lizana—since 2021; he was essentially my first climbing partner here in Peru. Since then we have been through a lot together—in the mountains, on the crags, at home, and in the pub. He was like my Peruvian brother. We were so close that my wife Alča and I were already counting on a little room for Saul for when we one day return to the Czech Republic and settle down somewhere.

Agnieszka is a Polish “machine” with several 8a/5.13b sport routes to her name. She had seen plenty in the mountains, too. Definitely no lightweight.

Alejandro “Chicho” Ugarte Jordan was a professional photographer from Lima. His main interest was unique mountain images captured with his DSLR or his drone. We had known each other since 2022 through the Skiing Peru project, which we were both part of. We often spent time together with our girlfriends in Lima outside the season, too.

Artidoro “Arti” Salas Gaitan was a pure Huaracino—someone who comes from Huaraz. He was born in the town at the foot of the Cordillera Blanca and lived there his whole life. He ran the Big Mountain hostel, well known among climbers. I personally have known him since 2021, when I spent my first season in Huaraz at his hostel.

Our expedition began on the morning of Saturday, July 11, when we loaded the van with heavy packs. Under the watchful eye of—as Matěj called him—our adoptive father Paul, our friend and the owner of the cozy Cayesh Guest House bed and breakfast, we set off for our starting point, the village of Armapampa in the Yungay area. From Huaraz, it was about a two-hour drive. Besides the six of us, Alenka came along, too, having arrived from Lima that same morning, as did Míša, a Czech woman living in Nepal who had just popped over to Peru to see friends. The girls, however, were only going on a day trip to the hut and back down the next day.

On the edge of the beautiful village of Armapampa, we unloaded our gear from the van, ate the last gluten-free cinnamon rolls that Alenka baked for the trip, and shouldered our heavy packs. Matěj took a group photo and we set off towards Refugio Huascarán, our base camp. We reached the refugio at 4,650m spread out over quite a wide time range (Matěj first, at 2 hours 45 minutes, and Saul and Aga last at a little over 6 hours). Who knows where and why they lingered; apparently they were finishing off their avocados. Either way, the trail was easy and there was no rush, so everyone went at their own pace.

There was no one at the hut apart from us, so we enjoyed it immensely. The evening was a very friendly affair: dinner, beer, guitar, and singing in four languages mixed together. And, of course, planning the climb.

This is where the first, relatively pivotal moment came. Aga wasn’t feeling well—to be precise, she felt pretty crap—so she decided to walk down with the girls. That left the five of us Mohicans. Saul immediately arranged with the lads to form a three-person team. At the time, it seemed to me a good idea. Saul is the most experienced of the three, and the team would only be stronger for it. In hindsight, I blame myself that we didn’t tell him to stay with Agnieszka; Aga was his big “project.”

After breakfast at the hut, we packed our rucksacks, took a group photo, and set off. The girls headed down; the lads headed up. None of us had any idea that this would be our last photo together, and that the girls would never see Saul, Chicho, and Arti again. Just writing this sentence brings tears to my eyes.

The second day of the climb up Huascarán was easy going. A few hundred vertical meters led up to the glacier, then a few hundred more traversed over a gently angled and relatively safe glacier to the spot where Camp 1 is set, at about 5,300m. Matěj and I arrived first, and shortly after us Arti and Chicho, with Saul behind them. We pitched the tents, melted water, cooked food, took photos, rested, and fine-tuned the details for tomorrow’s summit push.

Huascarán is normally climbed via the Garganta col, where Camp 2 is placed at 5,950m, and from there climbers make for the summit. All of us, however, were heading for the El Escudo (“The Shield”) route, which is technically harder but objectively safer,and of course more fun. It is a roughly 600-meter long ice face at 60 to 70 degrees. Thanks to its shape, there are no seracs hanging above you, no cornices, and far fewer things that can come down on your head. Unfortunately, there is little room for a Camp 2 there, so the Escudo is usually climbed from Camp 1 straight to the top, with the descent via the Normal Route.

We set our departure time for 10 pm. While the sun was still up, we crawled into our sleeping bags to get at least a little sleep and recharge the batteries a bit. We all knew that brutally hard work lay ahead.

This was it: summit day. Matěj and I got up at 9:30 p.m. so that we could leave at 10. We could hear the lads sometime before 9. In the end, we left more or less together: the lads at about 10:15 pm, we a few minutes after them.

Given the profile of the route over the Escudo, the plan from the start was to climb in pairs. Later, we became a pair and a three. You cannot climb this with five people on one rope, and none of us ever suggested it. The same applies to the descent.

The first part of the route was probably the most dangerous section of the whole ascent. It was a zone of crevasses and seracs of a size you will not encounter in Europe. There were a few sections of steep ice, but it wasn’t outright hard; it was just that something could come down at any moment. This section was shared with the Normal Route, which then branched off to the left towards Camp 2, while we headed right towards El Escudo.

We reach the bergschrund in less than two hours, with the lads following an hour or so behind us. Here we re-tied onto the full length of the 60m rope and racked up all our gear. In the process, I dropped my radio somewhere into the bowels of the glacier, and Matěj set off upward. The terrain wasn’t hard, so we simul-climbed on a single rope. Matěj climbed three to four pitches this way until he ran out of ice screws. On his last two snow pickets, he built a belay and let me through. My section was more hard snow than ice:easier climbing, less fun, but with correspondingly worse protection. I had three snow pickets on my pack, but I wanted to save those for later, for the belay anchors. After climbing some six pitches on just a handful of screws, I topped out over the lip of the Escudo, where I brought up Matýsek. (Editor’s note: “Matýsek” is a Czech nickname for “Matěj.”) By then, we were at 6,300m. It was cold as hell, with the sun still nowhere in sight. I dropped a glove and started feeling properly ill from the altitude. From this moment on, I suffered like an animal. The section from the top of the Escudo to the summit was endless; it wasn’t as steep any more, but it was long, and the soft snow did nothing to help our progress.

Sometime before 9 a.m. on Monday, July 13, though, we stood on the summit of Huascarán Sur. We didn’t really feel much joy, because we knew the worst was still ahead of us: the way down. On top of that, the summit was a completely flat plateau. You could play soccer on it, only you’d have a long way to go for a stray ball. Even so, weakness came over me and I cried. It was my fourth attempt, with my partner and childhood friend, and exactly a month to the day since our dog, Pisco, was poisoned. I cried again just remembering him. But the joy was there, too, although the main part of it was supposed to come down below. And it did come. But only for a moment.

We didn’t linger on the summit. There really was nothing there. After a snack, a little tea, and the summit photo, we headed down. No one had been on the summit for about two to three weeks, so there were no tracks and no trail. Our only guide was the offline mapy.cz app, which traced the Normal Route. But it was the route that was walked an unknown number of years ago, so we treated it as a rough indication only. We re-tied into classic glacier-travel mode, roughly 15–20 meters apart, with the rest of the rope coiled around each of our bodies.

A few minutes from the summit, on the empty, white, flat plateau, I saw something that looked like the windblown hint of a trail. Before I even got there, something happened and I was falling through the air into an icy hell.

A crevasse. With no sign whatsoever of its existence, it had been waiting there silently for its victim. I remember precisely the feeling of flying into the void. It was as if I were falling for an eternity. Suddenly, I felt the rope come tight, and I knew that was Matýsek. But I keep falling. In that instant, I was certain that in a moment, Matýsek would come flying in after me and we’d both be dead.

But a miracle happened—a miracle called Matěj. The rope bit again and I stopped. He bloody well caught me. I was hanging about 12–15 meters down in a crevasse that runs 100m in each direction and could easily be 50m, 100m, or 300m deep. Who knows? Below me, I saw nothing but icy hell. The crevasse was completely covered with a cap of fine snow. Zero chance of seeing it.

To my own surprise, I didn’t panic; I knew exactly what to do. My only connection to our world was the rope I was hanging on and whatever Matěj had rigged up there to keep me in this world. I clipped a Micro Traxion onto my harness as a self-belay and climbed up the vertical ice wall towards the surface as far as I could. I felt so OK that I even took three photos inside the crevasse. When the line of the rope stopped me from climbing any farther, I swung out into space. I ascended the rest on prusiks.

In a little under half an hour, I was out, back on the surface, back in the world. Only now did I breathe out.

Matěj and I fell into each other’s arms and began to grasp what we just survived. It would have taken very little for him to come in after me, and no one would ever have found us. In hindsight, I see it as an enormous miracle and a measure of enormous skill on Matěj’s part. He saved our lives. And maybe it was at this very moment that we realized this really was no joke and that things were getting serious. We continued the descent very cautiously, and we were genuinely scared.

We reached the edge of an ice wall where the Normal Route was supposed to be, but there was nothing there—nothing at all. There was no question of searching for a way through this labyrinth. The wall looked to be about 20m high, with a ramp below it along which we hoped to traverse off left toward the Normal Route.

We rappelled off two snow pickets into a relatively safe spot surrounded by seracs. I remember looking at the leaning towers next to us and thinking that one deep breath would be enough to bring the whole lot down. Still, we felt a certain safety in our little hollow. Finally, we had a bit of time to catch our breath, eat and drink a little, and get in touch with the lads.

Before I could do that, they called me. They said they were about 200 vertical meters below the summit. But it was already late, around 12 to 1 p.m., so their plan was to traverse off towards the Normal Route and descend by it.

I told them to try to find our tracks and our rappel, and to really watch out for crevasses, because I had just fallen into one. We confirmed that everything was OK and that we would meet in the evening at Camp 1, or if need be, at base camp, depending on the time and our energy. If I had known that this was the last time I would speak to them, I would have told them so much more.

A little later, while searching for the route on the map, my phone slipped out of my hand and vanished somewhere below. But I didn’t dwell on it. I knew we had other worries. It was just a shame to lose the photos from inside the crevasse.

Another unpleasant rappel off a 25-meter serac, and we were on the slope leading towards the Garganta. By now the weather had turned and visibility had dropped to a few tens of meters. But we were already in the Garganta, at 6,000m. All we needed now was to find Camp 2, which was just a flat patch where tents stood two days ago. From there, there should be some tracks and a route down.

We wandered about a bit, but eventually we found Camp 2 and there really was a hint of tracks. Shortly before that, the summit shook off its cloud, and we saw the lads above us at about 6,300 or 6,400 m. It even looked to us as though they were closer to the Normal Route than we were. We were really relieved.

The route from Camp 2 to Camp 1 is the most dangerous crux of the climb. It is a zone of avalanches, collapsing seracs, and crevasses. Later, Matěj and I agreed that we both felt as if we were living in some kind of virtual reality, outside our bodies, just waiting for the video game to end. Maybe that was why I didn’t really register the risk.

We arrived back at Camp 1 at sunset. With our last reserves of strength, we packed up and continued down to the refugio. A hot dinner and a bed was, after all, a big draw. And it was only a little under two hours more.

We reached the hut sometime before nine in the evening, after 23 hours on our feet. We asked for something to eat and a beer, then went to sleep. We thought about the lads and somehow had no doubt that they were fine and that they were at Camp 1. After all, we saw them a short way above Camp 2.

In the morning, we woke up at the hut completely wrecked, but suddenly also happy with what we’d achieved. We expected the lads to turn up any moment, and then we’d race down into the valley. The news from the lads, however, was different.

Saul wrote that they couldn’t find the way down and had to bivouac somewhere above Camp 2. But they were OK, he said; they survived the night up there without any camping gear. They were tired, Chicho was apparently feeling ill, but all were OK. They would try to find the descent route. From then on, we were to communicate with Chicho, as Saul’s battery was dead.

We still had no idea that things were bad. But neither did the lads. Because things weren’t bad yet. We managed to download our GPS track onto a phone and send it to the lads as a mapy.cz link. Up above Camp 2, there was occasionally a signal, so we communicated with the lads via WhatsApp.

At 9:10 a.m., Chicho wrote asking us to send our route. At 9:15 a.m., we sent the mapy.cz link and it was delivered.

At 10:16 am, Chicho sent a photo and a video from where they were. They sounded fine; Camp 2 was visible below them. At 10:18 a.m., we sent them a crib sheet of roughly which way to go down, including a sketch drawn onto their photo. This was delivered. At 10:21 a.m., we asked Chicho whether he had our route on mapy.cz. This was the last message delivered to Chicho. After 10:21 am, no further messages were delivered. From then on, there was no communication from them.

Since the weather was beautiful, the lads were fine, and there was no signal lower down towards Camp 2, it never occurred to us that the lads might be in trouble. We were certain they had descended lower, lost signal, had flat phones, and so on—but were descending. There was essentially no reason to think anything else. They werebujacos”—tough guys. They had survived a night without sleeping bags at 6,400m, and now they would just run down to us, to the girls, to lunch, to a beer.

Given these assumptions, we knew we had several hours of waiting before they would appear above Camp 1, which could be seen from the hut. Meanwhile, we tried to contact them by radio and by phone, in case they happened to come into signal. But still, we were completely calm.

When about 3 p.m. came and there was no sign of them, though, it seemed odd to us. It had gone on too long. We decided we would go up and look for them. They were probably tired and frozen, and another night up there would be truly critical.

We ate, brewed tea, and took all the clothing we had. In the meantime, I called a friend, a guide, down in town so that he knew something was going on and could start rounding up help if needed. But there was still a chance alive in us that they were at Camp 1, asleep in the tent, and simply had no way of letting us know. Down in town, a group of volunteers was already getting ready to set out to help. There is no organized professional mountain rescue in Peru.

But there was no one at Camp 1. By then we sensed that things were bad; we just didn’t know how bad. On top of that, a snowstorm was moving in.

The message we sent down was clear: It was official, we needed help. The lads were somewhere up there. We were going up for them. We knew that down in the valley things were already under way and would keep going without us. But we had a different task: to find them and bring them down.

We pack all their sleeping bags as well and set off up into the gale. Matěj created a Garmin messaging group and made our position trackable online. He realized more than I did that we were heading into real trouble.

I have friends up there – my “churaks,” my “hermanos.” I registered nothing.

For the first time in my life, I was faster than Matěj. In my head, I knew they were up there, frozen and exhausted but alive, and that we were their only chance.

In these conditions I would turn around on any climb of my own; I would never go on. But now it wasn’t about me. It was about the lads. The moment we gave up, they would have no chance.

We got as far as above Camp 2, to 6,000m, but there was nothing anywhere. That was shortly before midnight. Just gale, snowfall, a temperature I’d estimate at around minus 30 degrees, and not a trace of the lads.

By this point I knew things were very bad. In this weather, no one survives up here for even an hour. Matýsek was very afraid of going down because of the avalanche danger and pushed for us to stay at Camp 2 and somehow sit it out with all those sleeping bags. I knew that if we stayed, we would be dead within the hour. In the end, I convinced him that we had to go down. That was about 50/50. But staying up there felt to me like 90/10.

The descent was horrific. Avalanche terrain was everywhere we looked, with giant seracs over our heads almost the entire way. Visibility was near zero, and our tracks drifted over with snow. Nevertheless, our guardian angel was on duty again and guided us down. We get back to Camp 1 sometime around 3 a.m. on Wednesday, July 15. There I well and truly collapsed, mentally and physically. We crawled into the tent for a while and waited for the first group of volunteer rescuers, who, as we already knew, were on their way from the refugio.

Even in the tent and in a sleeping bag, 700 vertical meters lower, the weather was terrible, and it was here that Matýsek told me we were one–all. He had saved us above that crevasse, and I had by insisting that we go down.

The first rescue team reached Camp 1 just after first light, sometime after 6 a.m. It was five people in all, three guides and two aspirant guides, and among them my good friend Szymon, who was the only one closer to me than Saul. That was all. We handed over all the information we had and let them go up. We would head for the refugio, where further teams were supposedly bound: one to set up a support base at Camp 2, another at Camp 1. Supposedly lots of people were coming.

Unfortunately, we were in Peru, where everything does not quite work. We met the Camp 1 team below the glacier—five lads, all friends, climbers, and volunteers from Peru, Colombia, Spain, and the Czech Republic. No Camp 2 team ever came, and in fact, there never was one. The five lads up above had packed late in the evening and set out from Huaraz to help friends in need. In a single push they climbed to over 6,000m. But because of the lack of support and the conditions up there, they had to turn back in the afternoon and descend to Camp 1. That essentially ended any realistic chance of finding the lads alive. I personally had known already at Camp 2, which we had climbed to in our attempt to find them, that there was almost no chance left.

From then on, great chaos set in across the whole organization of the rescue and search. As I already mentioned, Peru has no mountain rescue service; there are no rescue helicopters, no rescuers, no system, no protocol, no designated person to take charge of any coordination. Everything happened through the WhatsApp group that Matěj created when we set out for the lads. Everything was done on the basis of volunteers, friends, guides and family members.

Eventually, the police, the high-mountain police (Policía de Alta Montaña), and apparently even the army joined in to help. But their help consisted solely of transporting volunteers by helicopter from the town of Caraz to the refugio at 4,650m, and the first helicopter took off 24 hours after the official call for help. What’s more, it flew up empty, to assess the situation, only to find that another police helicopter had been sitting on the “helipad” by the refugio for several days, badly parked, and that its pilots were afraid to take off in it.

In the end, some transport up did take place, but effectively only on the following days. If someone down below had been able to coordinate it, they could have sent the line of volunteers towards base camp on foot, with a herd of donkeys, on the very first day of the search (July 15), and they would have been there far sooner than waiting until the next day to see whether anyone would get a place on the helicopter.

The entire rescue, and subsequently the search, was run by volunteers and the families. It was they who hired private companies with advanced drones, they who coordinated everything, mainly with the police, they who did everything they could. I myself went back with two volunteers a few days later to a point just above Camp 1 to attempt a further search with drones—without success. The zone where all the clues ended—GPS signals, the location of the last photos, the hint of the last tracks, and so on—is at around 6,500m. Camp 1 is at 5,300m, and most drones simply cannot fly that high and that far. Only the advanced police drones can, and they in turn lacked experienced pilots willing to risk it. Going higher wasn’t possible because of the conditions on the mountain. So, a stalemate. In the end footage from the suspect zone was obtained, but there was not a trace of the lads.

After almost detective-like work by many friends—using the GPS signal from their watch as requested from Garmin headquarters, footage from private and police drones, plus footage from the guides who went all the way up during the search, information from us, and the lads’ last photos—it appears that the lads missed our belay station by literally a few meters, and that while crossing a snow bridge over gigantic crevasse systems it collapsed beneath them.

When I first wrote this report, it had already been more than three weeks since I last saw the lads, since I last spoke to them. Still nothing has been found today, and in my view it will be hard for anything to be found. The scale of everything on Huascarán is utterly absurd: crevasses several hundred meters wide and deep, seracs the size of apartment blocks… Sure, this isn’t the Himalaya, and it doesn’t look like it, either. Huascarán looks like two lazy fat blokes lying side by side. But beware of it. That mountain is mean and hostile, and although on paper it isn’t hard—the Normal Route was reportedly graded only AD/AD+, the Escudo D—it is no game. You are playing for your life at every single step.

I don’t think that either we or the lads made any fundamental mistake; it was simply that Matýsek and I had a large dose of luck, while the lads had a little less of it.

I am not writing this story for sensation. It is just one real story with a tragic ending. If at least a few of you take something from it—ideally some kind of lesson—it will have served its purpose. As we all know, the mountains give, but sometimes they also take away. We unfortunately now know that this is no empty phrase but the hard truth.

Marathoner Dies of Rhabdomyolysis: What to Know About Rare Condition

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Rhabdomyolysis, which caused the death of NYC marathoner Rachel Apa on Nov. 1, is a relatively rare condition, but here's what to know about what it is, who's at risk and how it becomes fatal.

When you think about athletes, you tend to think about stamina, strength and being in peak physical condition.

But anyone pushing his or her body to its limits also has to beware of overdoing it. The tragic death of Rachel Apa, a former college athlete who collapsed two miles from the finish line of the New York City Marathon, is an extreme case of a body fatally breaking down within a few hours, but a reminder that it can happen.

According to her father Steve Apa, Rachel died of rhabdomyolysis, a condition in which damaged muscle tissue breaks down and sends harmful proteins into the bloodstream.

“It’s unfathomable,” he told the New York Times. “Perfect health, perfect shape, 24 years old.”

Rachel, running her first marathon, had texted her dad after passing the 9-mile mark of the 26.2-mile route, which starts on Staten Island and finishes in Central Park, "I feel amazing. I've never felt this good."

Steve texted back from the family's home in Lewisberry, Penn., per NBC News, "Great, don’t overdo it. First marathon. This is a learning run. Don’t overdo it.”

Rachel had played field hockey for Bucknell University, was a regular exerciser and had trained for months ahead of the event.

“She was always on the go, always go," Steve said, "and it doesn’t matter if it’s school or sports or whatever it is. She was go."

So, how the heck does something like this happen?

Here is what to know about rhabdomyolysis, who is most at risk and more:

If you had never heard of rhabdomyolysis (or "rhabdo," as it's often abbreviated) you're hardly alone, as it's considered relatively rare, with about 26,000 cases reported in the United States each year, meaning it affects .0076 percent of the country's population.

According to the National Institutes of Health, rhabdomyolysis is a condition in which skeletal muscles break down and release intracellular components such as myoglobin, creatine phosphokinase (CPK), electrolytes and sarcoplasmic proteins into the bloodstream.

When toxic components of your muscle fibers enter your circulation system and kidneys, it can lead to acute kidney injury (AKI), electrolyte disturbances, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (a blood clotting disorder), per the NIH.

A "crushing injury" is the most common cause of rhabdomyolysis, according to the Cleveland Clinic, while overexertion (too much exercise without rest) and severe dehydration and overheating can also cause rapid muscle breakdown.

Cited causes of rhabdomyolysis also include certain medications, such as antipsychotic, antidepressant and antiviral medications, as well as statins; long periods of inactivity and physical conditions such as muscular dystrophy.

Endurance athletes and members of the military—i.e. those who engage in high-intensity physical activity—are considered more at risk, per the Cleveland Clinic, as are firefighters and others who work in hot environments and people over 65, as they're considered more prone to falls that could lead to injuries and prolonged immobility.

“Exertional rhabdomyolysis can occur after strenuous exercise and from high-intensity workouts during which the muscles are overused,” Niloofar Nobakht, MD, associate clinical professor of nephrology, told UCLA Health in 2023. “You can also get rhabdo from direct trauma, such as a crushing injury from a motor vehicle accident or a fall.”

But almost any intense activity can cause rhabdo, most commonly among people who are trying something new, according to Eric Rawson, chair of the department of health, nutrition and exercise science at Messiah College in Pennsylvania.

"You can be fit, and I can come up with a workout that you are unaccustomed to, and that could be what causes rhabdo," he told the NY Times in 2017, explaining why it's so important to start out at a beginner's level of a new activity before notching up the intensity.

Experiencing sore or weak muscles a few days after exercising, muscle swelling and abnormally dark or otherwise off-color urine can be signs of the condition.

The Cleveland Clinic notes that symptoms usually develop one to three days after a muscle injury, and persistent soreness doesn't always register as a potential life-threatening complication.

Rhabdomyolysis is survivable but requires prompt treatment, according to medical experts. Usually the condition is treated with fluids and electrolytes administered intravenously, while extreme kidney damage could require dialysis.

“We might start with an IV fluid and closely monitor electrolytes such as potassium, phosphorus and calcium," UCLA's Nobakht said. "Potassium and calcium are the two main electrolytes that play a significant role in recovering muscle function."

Three people, two of whom said they were doctors, took turns administering CPR, spectator Michaela Nicholas told the NY Times. New York Road Runners, which organizes the marathon, said event medical staff used a defibrillator on the fallen athlete before paramedics arrived and transported her to Mount Sinai Medical Center, where she was pronounced dead after six hours of attempted life-saving measures.

University of Michigan Health emergency medicine physician Dr. Bradley Uren told the publication that, for Rachel, "that worst case scenario happened just very, very quickly."

Four US Army Sikorsky UH-60M Black Hawk helicopters from the 2-158th Assault Helicopter Battalion, part of the 16th Combat Aviation Brigade, performed the ceremonial flyover at Lumen Field. While some footage appears to show fireworks passing directly alongside the aircraft, it remains unclear whether any projectile actually made contact. Neither an official explanation for the timing nor confirmation of an aircraft inspection was available in the initial reporting.

Fireworks are generally not compatible with aircraft operating in close proximity, especially during low-altitude helicopter demonstrations. Although a military helicopter such as the UH-60M is designed for demanding operating environments, its rotors, engines, flight controls, and other components remain vulnerable to foreign objects and external damage. A firework exploding near a helicopter could potentially expose its crew to burning debris, heat, and fragments, while also creating a distraction during a maneuver that requires precise aircraft control.

Sikorsky, part of Lockheed Martin, manufactures the Black Hawk, a twin-engine military utility helicopter. Its design emphasizes operational versatility, survivability, and the ability to continue flying under challenging conditions. However, these characteristics should not be mistaken for immunity to airborne hazards. Even a relatively small incident involving a rotor blade or an engine intake could have serious consequences, particularly when an aircraft is operating close to a crowded venue.

A historical example also shows why coordination between aircraft operators and pyrotechnics teams is essential. The accident killed actor Vic Morrow and two child actors, and injured six other people. According to the National Transportation Safety Board's investigation, debris and heat from the explosions damaged the helicopter's rotor blades, causing the tail rotor assembly to separate and the helicopter to descend uncontrollably. "Covert Contracts" Might Be Wrecking Your Relationship

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If you’ve ever thought “after everything I do,” therapists say you might have one.

You did bedtime solo every single night this week. You did the baths, with all the splishing and the splashing and the bubble anarchy. You fielded the lightning round of existential questions at 8:47 p.m. (“Mommy, what happens to doggies when they die?”). You tucked everyone in and then did your regular closing-time routine to get the rest of the house buttoned up for bed.

So when Saturday morning rolls around, you’re planning to sleep in. You fully expect your partner to wake up and handle the kids, the breakfast, the early morning dog walking duties. The whole shebang. Tit for tat.

Then your kids are in your face before you can even pry your eyes open, wondering where their soccer cleats are or if you can get more juice. Cue the rage, right? OK, so here comes the uncomfortable gut-check… did you communicate your expectations to your partner? Or did you just sort of assume it was a done deal?

This scenario has been dubbed a "covert contract," and therapists say it can lead to a whole lot of resentment building up in a relationship over time. Here’s what experts want you to know about where this behavior comes from, how to spot it in your own actions, and how it differs from the imbalance of the invisible load.

“A covert contract is a deal only one person signed,” says Kristyn Carmichael, Esq., a family mediator, certified divorce financial analyst, and founder of Couples Solutions Center. “It sounds like ‘If I do X, you’ll do Y,’ but the other partner never saw the terms, never agreed to them, and usually doesn’t know the contract exists.”

And she often has a front-row seat to what happens when those deals go unaddressed for a prolonged period of time. “In mediation, I see the end stage of these: one spouse arrives with years of unpaid ‘invoices,’ and the other is honestly confused about what they owe.”

Cheryl Groskopf, an anxiety, trauma, and attachment therapist who authored The Somatic Healing Workbook, likens it to writing a big ol' agreement and assigning your partner an equally big role in it. Somehow, your partner is the only person unaware of said agreement.

But your partner being unaware doesn’t change the way you feel. The hurt is just as real as if they’d actually broken a promise. “In your brain, they did,” Groskopf explains.

Um, yes. One hundred percent. This was something every expert agreed on! However, the need itself isn’t the issue here; it’s the delivery method.

“The need underneath is reasonable, but if it’s not articulated overtly, then it’s unreasonable to think your partner would be a mind reader,” says Dr. Jenni Skyler, Adam & Eve’s resident sex and relationship therapist.

Already, as a busy working mom who happens to be the default parent, I instinctively start to feel myself bristle at this idea. No one tells me these things or what needs to be done — I just notice and do it because someone has to. The experts say that sort of thinking is a fast path to frustration.

“‘I want my partner to make an effort on my birthday’ is a totally reasonable desire,” Groskopf says. “‘I shouldn’t have to tell them what I want because if they really loved me, they’d already know’ is when it becomes problematic.”

Now, the experts were also abundantly clear that none of this means moms should just silently accept an unfair split.

“One person also shouldn’t have to become a household manager simply because they are more organized, quicker to notice what needs to be done, or less comfortable leaving things unfinished,” says Ilona Phillips, a psychotherapist and founder of Lotus Counseling in Ann Arbor, Michigan. “Expecting shared responsibility is reasonable. Expecting a partner to recognize an unspoken sacrifice and reciprocate exactly one-for-one can get partners in trouble.”

“If I ask for a break and you say no, that’s a direct rejection,” Carmichael says. “If I never ask, I’m protected from that, and I keep the moral high ground.” And, yes, there’s also that belief so many of us carry that “if I have to ask, it doesn’t count,” she adds, “as if a gesture only proves love when it’s unprompted.”

That belief tends to run deep, too (ask me how I know). Says Groskopf, “If you grew up learning that your needs were inconvenient, asking can feel incredibly vulnerable.” Instead of just asking, we end up creating these little litmus tests our partners don’t even know they’re taking.

She uses flowers as an example. If you ask for them, you know they showed up because you asked. But if your partner brings them on their own, "There’s this feeling that you were appreciated, that they noticed you, that you MATTER. And for someone who grew up feeling overlooked or emotionally neglected, that distinction can feel huge.”

Oof. The catch here is that this strategy often just backfires.

“There is a fear that if we directly communicate what we need, and that need is not met, we will be hurt,” explains Dr. Paulette Didia, a licensed clinical psychologist in New York City. “However, if we do not communicate what we need, our partner will not know, our partner will not do it, and we will be hurt anyway. We think hurting in our own mind ‘hurts less,’ and that is not true.”

Admittedly, the lines are a bit blurry. When you’re doing the lion’s share of invisible work in your home and family, it’s hard not to “keep score” or have a running tally in your head of what your partner did versus what you did.

Unfortunately, that scoreboard warps how both partners see each other.

“Once you develop a narrative that your partner isn’t doing enough, it becomes very easy to fill that bucket with examples of how they’re falling short. Meanwhile, your spouse feels like they’re always in the doghouse and starts to wonder if it’s even worth trying. One person is thinking, ‘Don’t you see how hard I’m working?’ while the other is thinking, ‘What did I do wrong now?’” says Mary McLaughlin, LCSW, a couples therapist and discernment counselor.

So while it’s fair to feel frustrated by an invisible (or visible) load imbalance, a little communication can go a long way. That’s true on both sides of the line. “Even if the score is unequal, if all parties know the score and acknowledge it and offer gratitude, this can offset resentment,” says Skyler.

Well, obviously this is tricky since you probably don’t know you’re making them. However, there are a few tells you can look for, according to the experts:

First and foremost, resist the urge to try settling years of “back pay.” You can’t Uno Reverse this one. Instead, focus on rewriting the terms and making sure you and your partner are, in fact, on the same page. Here are a few practical pointers from our pros on how to pull that off.

Didia asks couples to fill out a simple weekly worksheet together that includes tasks along with who does them and when. She has couples do this at the same time each week so that it doesn’t become one more thing Mom has to track, and they post the list where both can see it so no one becomes the default reminder.

If you want to follow suit, start by listing every task (big and small). Next, let each partner choose what they’ll take, and split the ones nobody wants. Very important info: The person who owns a task decides how and when it gets done. “Maybe you think this should be done by Monday, but your partner indicates it will be done by Wednesday,” Didia says. “Since it is their task, they get to decide and, by Tuesday, you will not already be annoyed.”

Phillips encourages a weekly check-in where partners take turns asking: What’s been working well between us? Is anything feeling off? Extra difficult? Frustrating as hell? Unacknowledged? And, finally, how can I be a better partner to you this week?

You may be tempted to stop doing certain things, like washing the dishes, hoping that someone will notice. That’s just a covert contract in a different outfit, says Phillips. And even if your partner does pick up the task, resentment can still linger. You might then think, Why did I have to stop before they noticed?

“Relationships change. What worked when you were first dating, before kids, 10 years ago may not work anymore,” reminds McLaughlin. “Couples need to renegotiate instead of assuming the old arrangement still works.”

When you realize you’re locked in a pattern that won’t budge, it’s probably time to see a couples therapist. Carmichael’s advice is to get help sooner rather than later. By the time couples land in her office, she says the covert contracts show up even in how they divorce, i.e., “I gave up my career, so I deserve more.”

At the end of the day, adds McLaughlin, “Resentment paired with avoidance is a particularly risky combination for couples.” And that means the fix, both annoyingly simple and incredibly hard, is saying what you need out loud… and holding each other accountable to those needs in a way that feels fair and equitable to you both.

59 Years Later, Peanuts' Best Quote On Happiness Remains Its Greatest Line Not From Charles Schulz

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Across nearly 50 years and 17,897 daily comic strips between 1950 and 2000, Charles M. Schulz gave Peanuts fans no shortage of memorable lines. Some captured the awkwardness and anxiety of growing up, while others delivered the kind of simple wisdom that sticks with readers long after the punchline. Schulz was famously hands-on with the strip, writing, drawing, and lettering every panel himself. So it's easy to assume that every famous Peanuts quote came directly from him.

When it comes to happiness, Schulz certainly left his mark. His April 1960 comic strip gave readers the now-famous line, "Happiness is a warm puppy," which later became the title of his bestselling 1962 picture book. It's one of the most recognisable quotes in the franchise, but it isn't the only line worth remembering. In fact, Peanuts' best take on happiness came from someone other than Schulz.

That line appears at the very end of You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown, the stage musical that premiered Off-Broadway on March 7, 1967, at Theatre 80 St. Marks in New York City. With Charlie Brown, Snoopy, and the rest of the gang brought to life on stage, the production captured much of the charm and dry humor that made the newspaper strips so popular. It went on to run for 1,597 performances, but its greatest contribution might just be its final line. Nearly 59 years later, that simple thought about happiness still feels right at home alongside Schulz's best writing.

Charles M. Schulz may have created Peanuts, but its best line about happiness came from songwriter Clark Gesner. Gesner wrote the book, music, and lyrics for You're a Good Man, Charlie Brown, though the musical wasn't exactly what he had in mind when he started. Back in the early 1960s, he was simply writing songs about his favourite newspaper comic strip.

He eventually sent a demo tape to Schulz, who liked the songs enough to give Gesner his personal blessing to release them as a 1966 concept album. That record opened the door to the 1967 Off-Broadway musical, with Gary Burghoff (yes, the future Radar from MASH*) playing Charlie Brown, Bob Balaban as Linus, and Bill Hinnant as Snoopy.

The musical's closing number is called "Happiness", and it's pretty much everything that makes Peanuts work packed into one song. Charlie Brown and his friends take turns singing about the little things that make them happy: learning to whistle, finally tying a shoe, having five different crayons, sharing a sandwich, or catching a firefly just to let it go. There's no grand adventure or big life lesson here. It's just the gang enjoying the sort of small victories that Charlie Brown's world is full of. And then Gesner saves the best line for last:

"For happiness is anyone and anything at all, that's loved by you."

That's the line that gets to the heart of Peanuts. Schulz's "Happiness is a warm puppy" is wonderful, and honestly, it's hard to argue with Snoopy-sized doses of puppy love. But Gesner's lyric takes that same idea and gives it a little more room. Happiness can be a pet, a friend, a favourite crayon, or someone who makes even a miserable day feel a bit better.

Anyone who has spent time with Charlie Brown knows that his life isn't exactly short on disappointments. His baseball team keeps losing, his kites keep meeting trees, and Lucy is always ready to pull the football away at the worst possible moment. Yet Peanuts never feels completely miserable.

There's Snoopy on his doghouse, Linus with his blanket, and a group of kids who always find reasons to spend time together. That's the magic of the strip: happiness isn't about winning every game. Sometimes, it's just about having someone to share the day with.

A truly unforgettable sendoff to a legendary comic strip still hits home 26 years later with the final Peanuts Sunday strip.

Snoopy turns dinner into a Broadway showstopper in "Suppertime", Linus celebrates his blanket in "My Blanket and Me", and Sally steals the show with "My New Philosophy", added for the 1999 Broadway revival. The musical understands what made Peanuts special without changing what fans loved about it. And perhaps that's why Gesner's final line still feels so right nearly six decades later.

Herniated Discs Are a Common Cycling Injury—Here’s Everything You Need to Know

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Keep your back health on track with these tips from top docs and bike-fit experts.

Sometimes it happens with a bang, like when former Tour de France pro Marty Jemison was racing Tour of Flanders in the late 1990s. His saddle slipped to be nose up. He thought “no big deal” until one of the steep cobbled climbs, where he was in the saddle cranking out maximum wattage.“And my back just popped,” he says.

Jemison finished the race and the others that followed until he retired about 18 months later, though he had pain off the bike and for the first 30 or 60 minutes of riding, and knew something was wrong. That something was a herniated disc that became nearly debilitating after retirement, eventually required two procedures, and still flares up to this day if he’s not meticulous about his bike position and on top of his core work.

Other times, it’s the proverbial straw that breaks the camel’s (or the cyclist’s) back, as was the case with avid long-distance cyclist Mark Elsasser of Philadelphia who developed some sciatica in 2018. He was able to ride through it just fine, but couldn’t sit for any length of time before the pain was unbearable. The diagnosis: a herniated disc in his low back, which he resolved with physical therapy and has kept at bay by changing his position on the bike, going from a 0-rise to 5-degree stem. “My spine is less compromised being a bit more upright rather than nose down on top of the stem,” he says.

Herniated discs are fairly common in the general population, occurring most frequently among 30- to 50-year-old adults, and men more often than women. Interestingly, you may have one right now and not even know it. “We know from radiographical studies that 50 percent of those aged 40 may have some form of disc herniation. We might consider it normal, age-related changes, and many of those won’t be symptomatic,” says bike fitter and sports physiotherapist Bianca Broadbent, co-owner of The Cycling Physios based in the United Kingdom.

In fact, one research review published in the American Journal of Neuroradiology found that degenerative changes in the discs, which include herniated discs, are present in nearly 90 percent of adults 60 and older and often require no intervention. A herniated disc only causes pain if it’s pressing or otherwise irritating a nerve root in the spine

Your spine is comprised of 24 vertebrae. Between those vertebrae are fluid-filled cushions known as discs, which act as shock absorbers for your spinal bones.

“Imagine your spinal discs as jelly-filled doughnuts,” says Andrew Pruitt, Ed.D., the founder of the Boulder Center for Sports Medicine and CU Sports Medicine and Performance Center. “A herniation happens when the jelly inside pops out.”

You may hear people using other terms like “bulging disc,” “ruptured disc,” or “slipped disc.” Technically, a bulging disc is just that: It’s when the jelly inside the donut is pushing against the side enough to bulge out from the vertebrae but isn’t leaking out. A ruptured or slipped disc refers to an actual herniation, where the doughnut cracks, allowing the gel-filled center to squeeze out.

A bulging and/or herniated disc can cause pain by creating pressure on the nearby nerves. There’s some evidence that the leaked fluid from herniated discs also can cause chemical irritation and inflammation of the spinal nerve roots.

Again, it’s possible to have an asymptomatic herniated disc where you feel no pain. When you do have pain from a herniated disc, however, you’ll know it.

Pain from the herniated discs is sharp and radiating. Most cyclists experience herniated discs in the lower, lumbar back (we’ll get to why in a bit). So you may feel burning pain often known as sciatica that radiates from your lower back into your glutes and down one or both legs, sometimes even into your feet. You may feel pain similar to an electric shock when you stand or sit or walk. You may also feel radiating numbness, tingling, and/or weakness in your lower extremities.

So how does the jelly get out of the doughnut and start wreaking havoc on your spinal nerves? In a word: pressure. It can be extreme and sudden, like Jemison cranking on the cobbles. Or, in many instances, it can happen gradually over time, as was the case with Elsasser.

When your spine is in its generally straight, neutral position, the pressure from the top down is uniform on your discs, so the jelly inside those cushions is evenly distributed, Pruitt explains.

“When you bend forward, the jelly is pushed back, stressing the skin of that doughnut,” he says. “If you’re sitting all day, that jelly is pressing back there for eight hours. Now jump on a bike, and you add another two hours in an even more extreme position, pressing that jelly further and further, until—pop—it comes out.”

Though herniation can happen anywhere in the spine, in cyclists it happens most frequently in the lumbar (low back) region close to the base of the spine, or the L5-S1, the bottom-most disc that sits right on top of your tailbone, because that’s where we bend the most, says Menachem Brodie, C.S.C.S., head coach at Human Vortex Training and USA Cycling expert coach.

“For many cyclists, the L5-S1 is where they’re getting their flexion, and the lumbar spine has about six degrees of movement,” Brodie says. So if you’re not hinging from the hips and keeping your spine within its safe, natural range, you’re pushing those limits. Keeping your spine under that kind of stress for prolonged periods of time pushes you closer to the failure point.

Along those lines, research shows that riders with a lower handlebar position and greater flexion in that key lower back region were more likely to have low back pain. Though, importantly, the research also suggests that poor core strength and endurance could contribute to the spinal stress and low back pain in cyclists.

Of course, the bike isn’t the only place you can stress your spine. The gym can be another place riders put their spine under unhealthy stress, says Dane DeLozier, D.P.T., the biomechanics lab director at and co-founder of Revo Physiotherapy and Sports Performance. Maintaining a stable, neutral spine is very important when resistance training, especially when doing big lifts like deadlifts and squats as well as under velocity like with kettlebell swings, DeLozier says.

“When you have your spine under load, you want it to be neutral and very stabilized. You want to be hinging through your hips, not bending and extending your spine under load,” he says.

If you’re unsure of if you’re maintaining a neutral spine, DeLozier recommends setting up your phone and recording yourself (or having a friend video record you) so you can see if you are breaking or flexing under load.

The good news is a 2020 scientific paper, “Slipped disc: Overview,” reports that about 90 percent of people will get relief from their pain and restricted movement within six weeks even if they receive no medical treatment. That’s because the body gets rid of part of the prolapsed tissue or it shifts position so that the nerves aren’t irritated anymore, according to the overview.

You can help that process along with physical therapy, DeLozier says. “The vast majority of folks feel better and have a great outcome by doing mobility exercises for the hip and low back and decompression exercises for the spine.”

The exercises you do depends on your individual situation, but your physical therapist may recommend mobility, stability, and core strengthening exercises such as:

Press Up: Lie facedown with your feet together, toes pointed, and your forearms on the floor palms. Pull your shoulder blades down and in towards your spine and press into forearms to lift your chest off of the floor, keeping your neck long and hips on the floor. If mobility allows, you can press up further by placing hands below your shoulders to come into an up-dog position. Hold for 3 to 5 seconds. Repeat about 15 times.

Hip Flexor Stretch: Kneel with the affected knee on the floor, tighten your glutes, and brace your abdominal muscles while keeping your back straight. Pressing hips forward. Hold this stretch for 30 seconds. Do 3 times.

Bird Dog: Get on your hands and knees with your palms flat on the floor, shoulder-width apart. Slowly raise and straighten your right leg and left arm at the same time. Keep your hips aligned with the torso and not tilted to one side. Hold for 10 seconds. Return to start. Repeat on the other side.

Cycling itself, when done with a healthy riding posture, can also be a great rehab tool to strengthen the discs and surrounding structures, Broadbent says. In fact, a study done on high-volume road cyclists who rode nearly 100 miles a week for five or more years had beneficial adaptations to their discs and psoas (the biggest hip flexor muscle), which were beneficial for spinal health.

If you’re riding through your rehab, Brodie recommends “crocodile breathing” when you get off the bike. To do it, lie facedown with your forehead resting on the back of your hands and let your chest and hips sink into the floor. Inhale deeply into your belly, expanding your diaphragm for four slow counts. Pause for two seconds, then exhale for six slow counts.

Finally, while many herniated discs can be treated conservatively with physical therapy, some require injection therapy like nerve blocks, if the symptoms are severe, Broadbent says. In rare cases, spinal disc herniation can cause extreme pressure and swelling of the nerves at the end of the spinal cord causing a medical emergency known as cauda equina syndrome.

“If your bladder and bowels aren’t working properly, you have saddle numbness (off the bike as well), or you have sexual dysfunction such as unable to get an erection then you should seek urgent medical investigation,” Broadbent says.

Again, cycling can actually improve spinal strength and the health and hydration of your discs, says Broadbent.

Having a good bike position and the right saddle is key. “You want your spine in a healthy position that doesn’t strain those discs in your lower back,” Pruitt says. If you ride a gravel or a mountain bike, you’re likely already in a slightly more upright, more neutral spine position, he says.

An anatomical saddle will also allow you to put your weight on your perineum without putting harmful pressure on those sensitive tissues, so you’re able to reach the bars without bending too far and putting your spine in an unhealthy position. “Saddle technology improvements have really helped reduce degenerative disc disease,” he says.

You also can protect your spine by doing core-strengthening exercises several times a week. Brodie recommends what are known as the “big three” exercises for spinal stability, developed by renowned back pain expert, Stuart McGill, Ph.D., Professor Emeritus of Spine Biomechanics at the University of Waterloo. These exercises stiffen the musculature throughout the core in a way that enhances stability in the spine.

How to do these exercises: The American Council of Exercise recommends performing three sets for each using a reverse pyramid rep/set scheme. Start with a higher number of repetitions for the first set, then decrease by 2 to 6 reps, then decrease by another 2 to 4 reps on the last set. So you might start with 8-6-4 reps and work your way up to 12-10-8 reps as you get stronger and build endurance. Hold each exercise for no more than 8 to 10 seconds.

McGill Curl-up: Lie on your back with one leg extended and the other leg bent, foot flat on the floor. Put your hands under your low back to maintain your natural arch. In one move, lift your head, shoulders, and chest off the floor, keeping your neck and back in the neutral position. Hold. Then slowly return to start. Do half a set. Then switch legs and complete the set.

Side Bridge: Lie on your right side, with your forearm on the floor and elbow underneath your shoulder. Bend your knees at a 90-degree angle. Place your left hand on the opposite shoulder for stability. Lift your hips off the floor so your body forms a straight line from your head to your knees. Hold. Then lower to the starting position and repeat to the other side. Work up to doing the move with your legs straight.
