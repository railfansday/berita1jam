Photographer’s Dog Show Pictures Pose the Question: What Is a Proper Dog?

Photographer Jasper Cable Alexander has spent the past three years turning up to British dog shows in search of humorous images.

Alexander, who is the son of Royal photographer Lord Snowdon, lost sight in his left eye after a freak accident. He spent ten days in hospital, six months with vision in only one eye, a cornea transplant, and at least eight operations.

It was a tough time. But he found solace in humor, using it to bring a little lightness and fun to an otherwise difficult situation.

His dog project grew from that instinct. After three years of traipsing around Crufts and specialist Chihuahua competitions to village halls, sports centers, and local shows across the U.K., it eventually became a book: A Proper Dog.

What he found was not really about winning. The prizes are often little more than certificates, ribbons, and recognition. The real reward is the ritual, the friendships and the community.

Photographed almost entirely on analog film, the book feels like stepping into a world stuck somewhere between the 1990s and now.

“I wanted it to feel like this kind of timeless warp that you go into that doesn’t feel like part of this world. A lot of the venues are in community halls or sports halls and places that haven’t really changed much since the 90s, so it feels stuck in that era,” Alexander says.

All proceeds from the book will go to Bristol Eye Hospital, which helped the photographer regain his vision.

The title of the book, A Proper Dog, asks the question: what makes a proper dog? Many people laugh at Chihuahuas, dismissing them as yappy rodents, but Alexander asks: is all that matters the love between a dog and a person?

“A Proper Dog is about humour, tenderness and observation. It is a book made to raise money, but mostly to make people smile,” a press release adds.

Crucially, the tickets available on events365.com were for all Premier League clubs with the exception of games involving Chelsea.

The website have reportedly now removed all Premier League offerings, with Eghbali's scheme seemingly set to hit the buffers.

Everton’s former chief Wyness – who served as CEO at Goodison Park between 2004 and 2009 and now runs a football consultancy advising elite clubs – believes that Eghbali has likely dodged a breach of Premier League regulations by not selling inflated tickets for his own club.

He and the club's former chairman Todd Boehly have both been shrouded in controversy for their revenue-generating methods in recent years.

Roberto Mancini gave a brief interview to Rai ahead of Italy’s Nations League clash against Belgium on Sunday, paying his respects to the victims of the Heysel disaster and explaining why he decided to leave Sebastiano Esposito out of his matchday squad.

Italy are in Brussels for their final fixture of the Nations League group stage on Sunday evening. The Azzurri top the group on 10 points ahead of kick-off, ahead of France on 10, Belgium on nine and Turkey on zero.

Italy require a draw to qualify for the Nations League quarter-finals and in all likelihood a victory to secure top spot. If Italy lose, they will require Turkey to beat Belgium in order to still qualify for the quarter-finals.

Head coach Mancini spoke briefly to Rai a few hours before kick-off. He says that his players ‘deserve’ qualification for the next round of the Nations League given the work they have put in, but does not need reminding that the job is far from over.

“The boys deserve to get through to the last eight of the Nations League because they’re doing an extraordinary job, but there’s still a lot to do,” said Mancini.

“France are among the top three sides in the world and Belgium are not far behind them,” he added.

When asked how to stop Belgium’s creator-in-chief Kevin De Bruyne, Mancini simply replied: “By not allowing him to play.”

As is customary for Nations League group stage fixtures, Mancini has whittled his list of players down to a matchday squad of 23. Davide Frattesi and Nicolo Cambiaghi have not recovered from the France game on Thursday night and have been left out of the squad.

The surprising omission is Sebastiano Esposito, who missed a penalty during Italy’s 3-1 victory over France on Thursday night. Samuele Ricci, Federico Gatti and Matteo Palma have also been left out of the matchday squad to face Belgium.

Asked if Seba Esposito had been left out as a ‘punishment’ for missing the penalty against France, Mancini replied: “Don’t make me laugh. It’s just a technical decision, he’s played in practically every game.

“It’s a shame that we won’t have Frattesi or Cambiaghi, who were decisive in the game against France. Dimarco can also play as an attacking winger.”

Lastly, Mancini was asked about his memories of the Heysel disaster in Brussels back in 1985.

“We will never forget a tragedy as immense as this,” said Mancini. “I remember everything from that day of May 29, 1985. I played for Sampdoria at the time, but I am a Juventus supporter.”

Why do birds flap before landing? It may be about more than slowing down

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

New calculations suggest birds flap during landing not just to control speed, but to stop within limited space.

Most birds flap their wings as they come in to land, but those final wingbeats may do more than slow them down. New calculations suggest they also help birds stop within a tight space.

A bird the size of a pigeon could glide to a complete stop with its wings held still.

To manage it, though, the bird would have to begin its landing maneuver at about 80 mph (36 meters per second). It would also have to climb roughly 65 feet (20 meters), the height of a six-story building.

Flapping, the researchers propose, lets a bird stop inside the space it has.

Tom Rottier carried out the study at the University of Manchester and the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies. He worked with Philippe Lavoie of Toronto and Ben Parslew of Manchester.

No birds were flown for the work. Instead, equations were written for a bird gliding on fixed wings.

Only gravity, lift, and air resistance acted on this model bird. The team then solved the equations for the slowest possible touchdown.

The forces on a wing depend on how fast the wing moves through the air. Those forces shrink as a bird slows down. Its control over where it goes shrinks with them.

Arriving fast is no better. Whatever speed is left at touchdown is usually absorbed by the legs. A bad landing can damage muscle and bone, or it can kill the bird.

The difficulty, said Parslew, an aerospace engineering lecturer at Manchester, is that “landing isn’t as simple as just slowing down.”

A wing also has a minimum working speed. In level flight, the bird’s whole weight has to be carried by lift. A wing that moves too slowly cannot make enough of it. Engineers call that minimum the stall speed.

The stall speed is not fixed, however, as the team’s equations show. A bird that climbs more steeply puts less of its weight on its wings. The stall speed drops as a result. In a climb straight up, it is zero.

A climb that ends straight up is how a gliding bird could stop without flapping. The bird comes in fast and pulls up. It then climbs more and more steeply while gravity and air resistance slow it down.

At the top, the bird is pointed straight up and has no speed left.

More lift also means more drag, and drag takes away the speed the climb depends on. For that reason, the tilt of the wings has to keep changing on the way up. By the end, the wings are turned broadside to the air.

The team calculated what such a landing would take for a rock pigeon weighing 14 ounces (400 grams).

Starting from level flight, the bird would need that 80 mph speed. This is more than double a pigeon’s typical cruising speed.

The bird would travel about 200 feet (60 meters) horizontally, roughly two-thirds of a football field, while climbing 65 feet.

City pigeons fly between buildings. A landing of that size is not feasible in many of those places, the team wrote. Bigger birds would need even more room.

The researchers therefore propose that flapping is not simply a way to slow down. A glide can do that. Flapping does it in a small space.

A perch can be approached from below, but the ground cannot. That changes the answer.

The researchers then tested landings in which the model bird could not climb at any point. No path brought it to a stop.

The slowest landing allowed by the equations came in at an angle of about 34 degrees below level.

A bird that glides down to the ground therefore arrives with some speed left. That speed has to be removed by flapping, by braking with the legs, or by both.

The legs need enough muscle and bone to absorb the impact. But that adds weight the bird must carry in flight.

The researchers suggest this trade-off may have shaped how birds evolved. The need to withstand landing impacts may have set a minimum strength for their legs.

Leftover speed may also help explain why ducks, geese, and swans land differently on water than on solid ground.

More of a bird’s energy is absorbed by water than by the ground. The bird can therefore arrive faster.

Some birds of prey do finish a landing without flapping.

A 2022 study observed Harris’s hawks flying between two perches. They learned to dive low and then glide up to the perch.

A wing that is tilted very steeply is stalled, meaning air no longer flows smoothly over it.

The authors of that study proposed an explanation for the swoop. In their view, it keeps the distance a hawk flies with stalled wings as short as possible.

Rottier’s team tested the idea by giving their model bird one goal at a time. For this test, the model bird was allowed to flap during the dive, as the hawks did.

First, it was told only to spend as little time stalled as possible. The model bird climbed and then came down onto the perch. It arrived at speeds that would harm a real hawk, the team wrote.

Next, the only goal was to land as slowly as possible. This time the model bird swooped deep.

A mix of the two goals generally matched the real hawks’ paths best. Touchdown speed did more than stall time to produce the swoop.

Diving builds speed, and speed allows a steeper climb. A steeper climb ends in a slower arrival.

The pigeon figures come from equations, not from a bird in the air. In its main form, the model has no wind, no body or tail, and no flapping.

A wing gets a brief boost in lift and drag when it tilts up quickly. The wings of landing birds do this, yet the boost is also left out of the model. To see how much it could matter, the team doubled the model’s maximum lift.

For a slow touchdown, the starting speed the bird needed fell by 44%. The forward distance fell by 65% and the climb by 55%. When the maximum drag was doubled instead, the speed needed went up.

A living bird may therefore be able to glide to a stop in less room than the pigeon numbers suggest. How much less cannot be said from the model.

The equations were written so that wind can be added later. The team also described the model as a base for studies of single species.

The same physics may apply to animals that never flap, such as flying squirrels and flying snakes. It may also apply to drones built to land on perches.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society A.

Jerry Jones: "I don't think we're far away" from 18 regular-season games

As the NFL embarks on the second half of its current 18-week regular-season schedule, it’s just a matter of time before the current structure changes.

It’s no secret that the NFL wants to expand the regular season from 17 to 18 games. The league is sufficiently confident about it happening that folks like Cowboys owner Jerry Jones speak of it as a given.

“I don’t think we’re far away from it,” Jones said Friday on 105.3 The Fan in Dallas, regarding the addition of an extra game. “I don’t know how — what is far away. Next month? No. Next year? Maybe. [The] next year? Maybe. That has to be negotiated with the players.

“But it’s in everybody’s best interests.  You’d cut back on the preseason games. You’d have teams more develop in the first few games as the type of thing you were trying to get done in preseason. But it’s in everybody’s interest, and it certainly is in the players’ best interest in my mind, for sure.  And having said that, I would anticipate that coming.”

His comments presume the NFL Players Association will happily agree to 18 regular-season games. They likely won’t. At a minimum, they’ll seek major concessions in exchange for the willingness to add yet another game that counts.

The unspoken goal had been 2027. The NFL delayed picking a date for Super Bowl LXII in Atlanta in order to accommodate the possibility that the game will slide a week or two deeper into February. That would make 2028 the earliest year for expansion, as a practical matter. (Adding a game for 2027 would be possible only if the dead week between the conference championships is wiped out or if the season starts a week earlier. If an 18th game will result in a second bye, both would be required.)

Expansion by 2027 would require expedited negotiations. Expansion by 2028 would hinge on the NFL and NFLPA striking a deal within the next year or so. The union has shown no inclination, however, to engage in talks aimed at extending the current Collective Bargaining Agreement, which runs through the 2030 season.

Still, if push comes to shove, there will be 18 games. The blueprint was created in 2011. The NFL will lock the players out until the players agree to the things the owners want. Most notably, 18 regular-season games and 16 annual international games.

Beyond that, Jones’s comment about the early games of the season is odd. “You’d have teams more develop in the first few games as the type of thing you were trying to get done in preseason.”

He says that as if it’s a good thing. It definitely is not. Already, teams struggle in September to get up to speed, given that most teams (with the exception of those assigned to the Hall of Fame Game) play only three preseason games. When the preseason reduces to two games, that’s even fewer chances to prepare.

It’s also fewer chances for the non-starters to get the attention of coaches and General Managers in games that don’t count in the standings. It will become harder for undrafted free agents to make the 53-man rosters.

Regardless, the motivation isn’t football. It’s money. More inventory equals more weekends equals more broadcast windows equals more revenue.

And that likely will continue until the preseason shrinks to zero games — and the regular season expands to 20 games.

The Oklahoma Sooners will be back at home in Week 11, with a record of 5-4 overall and 3-3 in the SEC. Head coach Brent Venables and his team will be looking to win consecutive games for the first time all season and get their first home win in conference play in 2026.

The up-and-down season began with a win over UTEP, but then a loss to Michigan. The Sooners beat New Mexico, but lost to Georgia. After the bye week, OU beat Texas, then lost to Kentucky. Oklahoma then beat Mississippi State, but lost to South Carolina. After beating Florida last week, the Sooners are looking to build on their first win of the season.

This week, the Ole Miss Rebels will be in Norman. They went 13-2 last year, made the College Football Playoff with an 11-1 record, and beat Tulane at home and Georgia in the Sugar Bowl in the first two rounds. A Fiesta Bowl semifinal loss against Miami ended their season just short of the title game.

It's been a whirlwind 12 months for the Rebs, beginning with the infamous end-of-regular-season departure of former head coach Lane Kiffin, who left Oxford to coach LSU. Kiffin's defensive coordinator, Pete Golding, was hired as the full-time head coach and led the team through the CFP run. Now, Golding is in his first full season as the program's head coach. He arrived at Ole Miss after stints as the defensive coordinator at UTSA and Alabama. He was the defensive play-caller for 'Bama's 2020 national title team that went undefeated. He was Ole Miss' defensive coordinator for three seasons before landing the head-coaching job.

In 2026, the Rebels are 6-3 and 4-3 in the SEC. A strong start to the season saw the Rebels beat Louisville (41-38), Charlotte (41-9), and get revenge against Kiffin and LSU with a 32-24 win at home. A loss to Florida (52-28) followed, but Golding's team bounced back after its bye week, beating Vanderbilt (52-17) and Missouri (31-3). However, the last three weeks have seen the Rebels lose to Texas (34-27), beat Auburn (45-15), and get dominated last week by Georgia in a 42-7 home loss.

This game pits two teams coming off of very different emotional results in great primetime atmospheres, and if the SEC has taught us anything this year, it's that it is definitely a week-to-week league in 2026.

On offense, the Rebels are led by co-offensive coordinators John David Baker and Cody Woodiel. Baker is the play-caller, with Golding having hired him away from East Carolina ahead of this season. He spent two years there, after he'd been on Kiffin's staff in Oxford from 2021 to 2023. He'll be matched up with Venables, who will call the defense for the Sooners. Woodiel comes over from Miami, where he'd coached for the last four years on Mario Cristobal's staff.

Baker's offense is very similar to what Ole Miss has run for the past few years with Kiffin and former offensive coordinator Charlie Weis Jr. at the controls. There are elements of the Art Briles-style veer-and-shoot that OU has already seen this year against Mississippi State and South Carolina, but there are also plenty of pro-style or more traditional spread elements in the mix. Baker and the Rebels put a lot of pressure on opposing defenses, and they have the talent to play well against anyone. Wide receivers coach L'Damian Washington has worked under Venables before, as he was OU's interim WRs coach in 2022.

It all starts with star quarterback Trinidad Chambliss, who came out of nowhere to take the starting job and run with it for Ole Miss last year. He won a court case this offseason to get to come back and play one more collegiate season, and he is one of the most dynamic dual-threat QBs in the nation. Chambliss' playmaking ability and accuracy gave the Sooners fits last year, and it's yet another week against an athletic signal-caller for Venables' defense.

Star running back Kewan Lacy is out for the season, which means the ground game will lean on Makhi Frazier (if he's able to play) and Shekai Mills-Knight. The Rebels certainly won't be shy about running Chambliss as well. Through the air, wide receivers Deuce Alexander and Horatio Fields are the top targets, along with tight end Caleb Odom. The Rebels have a dynamite passing attack, and OU's secondary will have to be up to the challenge this week. Offensive lineman Troy Everett will return to Owen Field this week after a long career as a Sooner, including serving as a captain last year.

Schoonover's unit is one of the nation's best, led by star kicker Lucas Carneiro, who is one of the nation's best. Oklahoma's special teams had a poor day in bad weather against the Rebels last year, and with rain in the forecast again this weekend, kicker Tate Sandell and company will have to be on point against a very strong group.

OU and Ole Miss have just three meetings all-time between, but they're now annual rivals for the next four years, at least according to the SEC. The first meeting came in the 1999 Independence Bowl in Bob Stoops' first season at the helm of the Sooners. OU lost a tight 27-25 game that night in Shreveport, but went undefeated and won the national championship the next year.

Building on success is not something Oklahoma has done well this season, and it's certainly been a talking point inside the walls of the Switzer Center this week.

Ole Miss is talented and explosive enough to pull away in this game, especially if OU's offense reverts back to its old self for long stretches of this game. However, if the Sooners get help on offense, contain Chambliss, and get a few breaks on defense, they are good enough to win this game against a Rebel team that has a few more issues than it did last year.

One more win would ensure Oklahoma's 28th-straight bowl berth, but the focus right now for Venables' Sooners is on finishing out this year in the best possible fashion. Last week's cathartic win over Florida was impressive and an encouraging sign after last week, but can Oklahoma build on that success and string together wins? It's something this team has been unable to do all season long, but the next opportunity to change that comes this week. Seven or eight wins looks a whole lot better than five or six wins, and a strong finish to the year could at least salvage a little pride from a 2026 season gone wrong.

The 10 Most Ambitious Documentaries of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Yes, documentaries can be a bit formulaic, and there are conventional ones that have interviews, voiceover, and fairly mundane visuals that all serve to depict something objectively, or give the impression that things are being explored objectively, but that’s not every documentary, by any means. You can put that criticism on any genre, really, because there are generic action movies, horror films, comedies, etc.

Documentaries seem to get that criticism the most often, though. It’s not really fair, and maybe one way to combat that sentiment is by highlighting some particularly cinematic and groundbreaking ones. So, the following might go some way toward that, because these feel like some of the most ambitious documentary movies of all time, with the lot of them being interesting for different reasons and certainly unexpected compared to what you might picture when you think of “ordinary” or “normal” documentaries.

Since Apocalypse Now is a tremendously ambitious – and unusual – war movie, it’s inevitable that any behind-the-scenes documentary about its production is going to be, at the very least, also quite interesting. Yet everything that happened behind the scenes with this movie was more chaotic than you'd expect, even if you were expecting chaos, and so Hearts of Darkness: A Filmmaker’s Apocalypse is ultimately a dizzying watch.

It doesn’t mess around in showcasing the hectic production of Apocalypse Now, and it’s about as psychologically intense and even as surreal as a good chunk of the actual movie is. Also, Apocalypse Now is already an easy movie to admire, but watching Hearts of Darkness: A Filmmaker’s Apocalypse is a pretty much guaranteed way to respect the fact that filming was completed – and the final product made some kind of sense – even more.

Tokyo Olympiad is a documentary about the 1964 Tokyo Summer Olympics, or might be better described as a look at said event, kind of neutral and without commentary or much of a narrative. It might sound daunting, just because of its approach and the fact that it’s almost three hours long, but it’s honestly as artistic and mesmerizing as sports footage can get, even if you're not hugely into watching footage of sports.

You notice the three-hour runtime to some extent, sure, but Tokyo Olympiad does remain interesting throughout, with everything captured in unbelievable detail. It's far more than just a clip show, as all the camera angles are a good deal more interesting than what you'd see on a TV broadcast, with the editing playing a huge role in enhancing everything and making it all feel like a largely hypnotic viewing experience.

Historical value/context is important when it comes to Man with a Movie Camera, since the existence of this film is such that the whole documentary format might look entirely different today had this movie never been made. No hyperbole, but it's likely one of the most important and influential documentaries of all time, using inventive visuals and creative editing techniques to show how life was in the Soviet Union during the 1920s.

That really is the premise, though. Man with a Movie Camera is simple, and outside the style, there isn't really a whole to this silent film. Yet it’s the style that makes it dazzling and engaging to this day, doing some interesting enough things with camera tricks and editing to make it feel exciting and very alive, all to the point where it’s worth checking out, even if you might assume that a listless documentary that’s nearly a century old would be dull. Maybe other listless 100-year-old documentaries would be. Not Man with a Movie Camera, though.

Two filmmakers were credited with making The Five Obstructions: Lars von Trier and Jørgen Leth. The former’s the one you're more likely to have heard of, and he does feel somewhat in control for much of this documentary, but that’s largely got to do with Leth being more of the subject here, since The Five Obstructions involves Lars von Trier challenging him to remake one of his old short films five different times, and with absurd rules on each occasion.

Well, technically, the rules get more unusual with each new remake, and then those shorts Leth did make (or seemed to make) are shown in quite a bit of detail, so that’s where his directing definitely comes in. There might be more involvement on his part, as it’s hard to know how much of The Five Obstructions is genuine and how much is two Danish filmmakers playing a feature-length prank on the audience, but in any event, it’s all very engaging to watch, and certainly interesting to think about, too.

Flee is an animated documentary, which is interesting enough on its own (see also another documentary from 2008 that’ll be mentioned a little further down). It’s about a man’s life experiences, basically, including his time as a refugee and how he grappled with his sexuality while surrounded by people who weren't always accepting of it… and the animation itself is a way to protect his identity, essentially.

There's quite a bit of time covered throughout Flee, and also numerous weighty subjects discussed and explored.

It’s simple animation, sure, but it does still lead to interesting presentation, and there’s quite a bit of time covered throughout Flee, and also numerous weighty subjects discussed and explored. The whole thing ends up being rather moving and quite memorable. Though it’s currently in an awkward middle-ground between being a new release and something old enough to be considered a classic, there’s a good chance time will ultimately be kind to Flee.

Rightly sometimes regarded as one of the greatest films of all time, including non-documentaries, Shoah is a mammoth documentary and one that people owe to themselves to watch at least once in their lives, though you do need to devote a ton of time to finishing it. The runtime stands out and has to be addressed, since Shoah goes for more than nine hours, using that length of time to hammer home the enormity of the event it explores.

It’s made up entirely of interviews with people recalling the Holocaust, including survivors, witnesses/bystanders, and even a few perpetrators. There’s no archival footage or much else beyond those interviews, with the approach nevertheless being used to form a sprawling and daunting narrative by stringing together all those different testimonies. Shoah is a challenging watch, but it has to be, also feeling about as essential and historically significant as war documentaries can get.

O.J.: Made in America could well be the greatest crime documentary of all time. However, it's also more than a documentary about one particularly publicized event and subsequent criminal trial, using the story of O.J. Simpson and his much-discussed trial to unpack a whole range of things, including the American dream, the country's history of racial conflict, and, more broadly, investigating American culture throughout the second half of the 20th century.

Sure, it’s seven and a half hours long, which means it has room to do a lot, but it’s still impressive how much is packed into that runtime. Also, it has to be acknowledged that it exists in a strange middle-ground between movie and miniseries, as it was screened theatrically and therefore won an Oscar for Best Documentary, but most would've watched it as a miniseries in five parts, and the Academy Awards changed the rules after O.J.: Made in America won. Oh well… point is, it deserved to win, and it’s easily one of the most monumental documentaries of the century so far.

Depending on where you spend your time online in 2026, you may feel like You Can See Everything is one of the biggest movies of the year. At least in some circles, it’s one of the most discussed, but then also if you want to look at things more objectively, or outside certain circles, it has already earned a lot for a documentary (and, at the time of writing, it’s only just starting to get released outside the U.S.), and the level of critical acclaim has been similarly remarkable.

It’s co-directed by Nathan Fielder, who uses the documentary to build on some of the stuff he was doing in The Rehearsal, which was a show where he built upon the stuff he was doing in Nathan for You, but what Lance Oppenheim contributes here as the other co-director shouldn’t be overlooked (he’s had a good year, since he was also behind the recent Primetime). As for the content of You Can See Everything… it’s wild. It’s best not to say anything about why it’s wild. But it is. It’s a big deal. It’s hard to stop thinking about, once seen, and moves so well for such a long and static documentary, if it can even be called a documentary in the first place.

A film that covers a particular war within an extensive conflict that’s still relevant to this day, Waltz with Bashir is uniquely presented and shattering as a film. It has director Ari Folman taking a personal approach to covering the 1982 Lebanon War, wartime atrocities committed, and attached post-traumatic stress disorder, with the film being about his attempts to recover his memories from his involvement in the conflict as a young man.

Waltz with Bashir explores the conflict – in particular an infamous event known as the Sabra and Shatila massacre – not from the victims’ point of view, which has made it controversial. However, it doesn’t feel like it’s in favor of the attack’s perpetrators, showing the horror of what happened and condemning both it and other atrocities around the same time. It feels distinctly anti-war, with Folman addressing what he feels he can personally speak to, doing so in a way that feels self-reflective and critical. Not everyone agrees with that, but in any event, what this does as a wholly distinctive and unusually presented documentary is impressive, and it does feel like Waltz with Bashir inevitably achieves what it set out to do.

Of all the documentaries here that feel like so much more than “just” documentaries, Koyaanisqatsi is perhaps the most groundbreaking, unusual, and compelling. There isn't anything here by way of voiceover or interviews, and you don’t even get all that much text explaining the things you're seeing and hearing, but Koyaanisqatsi does still get its message across through all its sights and sounds.

Different people will get slightly different things from it, yet the environmental message and the exploration of a conflict between humanity and the natural world are still very much effectively conveyed, not to mention ultimately hard to miss if you pay even the slightest amount of attention. And Koyaanisqatsi is worth paying much more than a slight amount of attention. It’s easily among the most memorable and radically presented documentary movies of all time, and it’s lost none of its power when watched today, more than four decades on from its initial release.

Alessandro Bastoni will be available for selection when Italy take on Belgium in their final Nations League group stage fixture on Sunday evening as he did not pick up a yellow card during Thursday’s 3-1 victory over France, despite reports from UEFA suggesting that he had.

In UEFA Nations League matches, players earn one-match suspensions for every even number of yellow cards they receive, i.e. after every second, fourth and sixth booking.

Bastoni was one of five players in Italy’s matchday squad against France who had been carrying a yellow card, along with Gianluigi Donnarumma, Nicolo Fagioli, Antonio Vergara and Gianluca Scamacca. Moise Kean, Daniel Maldini, Gianluca Mancini and Matteo Ruggeri had also been booked before the France game, but were not in the squad on Thursday night.

There were momentary fears on Thursday night when UEFA reported that Bastoni had been shown a yellow card during the second half of the France victory, which would have earned him a suspension for Sunday’s test away against Belgium.

However, it has since been confirmed by the FIGC that this was an error in reporting and that the yellow card had actually been shown to Davide Frattesi. Neither Bastoni nor Frattesi will be suspended against Belgium.

Pio Esposito was also booked against France, but this was his first yellow card of the Nations League campaign and he also remains eligible to face Belgium on Sunday.

Pochettino was the first USMNT head coach with genuine international appeal, U.S. Soccer more often than not picking from a domestic talent pool in the team's modern history. His tenure has reflected just that – he became the federation's highest-paid coach ever when he initially joined in 2024, the salary and the scope of his role only increasing in his recent contract extension. U.S. Soccer surprisingly plucked him out of the European club game, where he had spent the entirety of his coaching career beforehand, making a name for himself at Tottenham Hotspur before later stops at Paris Saint-Germain and Chelsea. Naturally, clubs of that ilk never lost interest and over the course of his time with the USMNT, he was linked to a handful of jobs. The most notable approach came from AC Milan, who were considering the move in the build-up to the World Cup but ultimately hired ex-Manchester United coach Ruben Amorim instead.

They've been great partners and are, of course, excited about the summer … There's been a longer list of outreach than what has even been reported and so they've been great about that."

Pochettino has also been open about his desire to return to the Premier League during his stint with the USMNT, something the move to United will accomplish.

United are currently in the relegation zone with one win in 10 games, the least of any team in the Premier League this season.

Pochettino's track record with the USMNT is not exactly notable – he has 21 wins in 45 games, nearly all of them against teams below them in the FIFA rankings. They stumbled in a variety of ways when they attempted to punch above their weight, most notably in the World Cup round of 16 loss to Belgium that was tinged by the Folarin Balogun affair. His attempts to lead the USMNT to a statement-making run at a World Cup on home soil were mixed at best, since the team could not pass the last 16 hurdle they have been stuck at since 2010, but Pochettino still found a way to recalibrate the U.S. team in his two years in charge.

The specifics of the round of 16 elimination were a surprise, even if the exit was not. The U.S. team's player pool, talented as it was at the World Cup, was not considered amongst the world's elites so a genuinely deep run over the summer felt improbable. Pochettino, though, managed to get the best out of the group and had the USMNT playing in a way it rarely had previously – in an entertaining, high-intensity, attack-minded system. The style is a hallmark of Pochettino's coaching career but has been historically difficult to integrate at the international level, with teams usually gathering for 10 days at a time, weeks or months apart, and has historically not been the USMNT's strategy. Pochettino's predecessor Gregg Berhalter, for example, was more reserved in his tactical approach and before him, the U.S. team were known more for grit than style.

Slowly but surely, Pochettino's approach worked with the generation of players Berhalter brought in. He correctly identified that the player pool's strengths were in attack, forwards Balogun and Christian Pulisic continuing to serve as central figures for the team. More notably, though, Pochettino found use for players Berhalter had previously cast aside – midfielder Malik Tillman became a key player, his versatility something Pochettino counted as an asset. Forward Ricardo Pepi also rose up the ranks in the build-up to the World Cup, his first after being one of the last omissions from Berhalter's 2022 team.

Pochettino's legacy might actually be the player pool shakeup he oversaw on the road to the World Cup, which felt rushed because of the short timeline but was ultimately useful. Defender Alex Freeman was the breakout of the Pochettino years, going from relative obscurity at Orlando City to a World Cup starter and goalscorer in less than 500 days. Midfielder Sebastian Berhalter, the son of Pochettino's predecessor, was not on the national team's radar until his first call-up in 2025 and looks like a national team fixture for years to come. The player pool expansion project will only get more exciting from here with the USMNT's up-and-comers, with some like Cavan Sullivan and Adri Mehmeti poised to be some of the best players in the history of the men's program.

The coach's departure will sting in some ways, but his tenure ensured that the USMNT would be in a position of strength, which was hardly the case when he arrived. He inherited a team that had lost its way after a group stage exit in the Copa America and will leave behind a team with a clear enough roadmap to the 2030 World Cup. The Pochettino years prove that the next head coach should primarily be a strong tactical fit, no matter the candidate's global appeal, because he was right to identify that an attack-minded approach would suit the player pool. That's especially true with the up-and-comers since many of the brightest prospects are attackers.

There is no reason U.S. Soccer should not hire the best fit for the job but for perhaps the first time in the program's history, the USMNT's prospects might truly rest with the players at their disposal. Pochettino's successor will be chiefly tasked with getting the best out of them, much as he did with the U.S. team's core. As an exciting generation of talent finds their footing with the senior team, that's exactly how it should be.
