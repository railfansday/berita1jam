20 Fantastic Animated Series That Outshine Most Live-Action Shows

📅 Feb 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It's widely agreed by most that animated film and television are incredibly and unfairly disregarded by many as true art. So much more can be accomplished visually and stylistically in the realm of animation. It's especially impressive when there's a great animated television show, requiring not just a longer runtime to animate, but typically released every week.

Animated shows are so wonderful. Being serialized projects doesn't make them only for kids, though, like typical Saturday morning cartoons are (however, some of these are). If animated television has shown the world anything, it's that animation can be for everyone, kids and adults — sometimes even adults only. While some may see them as just "cartoons" and nothing to take too seriously, in fact, many animated products often find themselves being better than what one would find in live-action projects.

With the X-Men coming to the Marvel Cinematic Universe, people are all theorizing and wondering what the plot could be like for this new iteration. With the cast that's been announced being so young, many people have been hoping — praying — that they take from the likes of the animated series, X-Men: Evolution.

This series brings the iconic characters into a much younger age, making them high schoolers, rather than teachers at Xavier's School for Gifted Youngsters. Making the main team so much younger than they are typically seen brings the opportunity for all-new kinds of interpersonal drama that can't be found outside of it. It makes one wonder why they aren't depicted as teenagers more often (it also kills the controversial love triangle that is commonly between Wolverine, Jean Grey, and Cyclops—thank goodness).

While 2011 may seem like a disastrous year for the Green Lantern character, many don't realize that was only in cinema. The hero had an animated series that began the same year that is genuinely one of the best adaptations of the Hal Jordan (Josh Keaton) character. Overshadowed by the terrible Green Lantern movie, though, Green Lantern: The Animated Series was never given the shot it deserved.

The way this series adapts the entire Green Lantern Corps is almost perfect. It manages to have some mature themes and stories while still being wholly accessible for younger audiences. It feels like a space opera in its scale, but still juggles personal relationships and character arcs as if it were a grounded street-level show somehow.

If someone hasn't already heard the comic book fans of the world raving about 1992's X-Men: The Animated Series, they've probably been living under a rock for a few decades. It's so popular that it even got revived with Disney+'s X-Men '97—people love it that much. For good reason, too, as X-Men: The Animated Series has some of the best superhero storytelling on television.

This five-year-long show has some of the deepest, most meaningful storytelling in animated superhero television. The X-Men have always had this over most Marvel Comics properties. The show accurately and truthfully depicts themes and messages about discrimination and bigotry while still being an action-packed, kid-friendly romp.

One of the most popular modern anime is none other than the 2016 series My Hero Academia. Kohei Horikoshi—the mangaka of the franchise—does an exceptional job blending typical Western superhero tropes and character archetypes with the shonen anime style. My Hero Academia is different than what most people expect from a superhero show, with a unique premise, world, and characters that can only be found in this franchise.

The premise of a young boy in a mostly superpowered society who has no abilities getting the greatest power on the planet from the literal Symbol of Peace is already engrossing. But when mixed with the one-of-a-kind characters surrounding him — with equally special power sets — and emotionally compelling stories and character arcs, My Hero Academia becomes a truly distinctive and special series.

From the exceptional minds of Greg Weisman and Brandon Vietti, Young Justice is one of those shows that truly captures the teenage experience (much like another popular show of Weisman's). Taking a page out of Teen Titans, Young Justice depicts a team of sidekicks and young heroes coming together to form a team of their own.

Unlike Teen Titans, this show goes even deeper with its mature storytelling — less anime-inspired. When it comes to good animated DC Comics content, Young Justice is up there with the best of the best. The storytelling at play, as well as the character arcs, make Young Justice one of the best DC Comics animated properties out there.

It was always going to be next to impossible to perfectly follow up Avatar: The Last Airbender, but with this in mind, the studios behind both that series and The Legend of Korra did pretty dang well when it came to developing the latter. The sequel series, following an all-new Avatar, Korra (Janet Varney), is not perfect, but still an exceptional show, at the end of the day.

People give The Legend of Korra a lot of flak for simply not being the same series Avatar: The Last Airbender was. Expecting the follow-up of one of the best television series of all time to be even better is the definition of overly wishful thinking. At the end of the day, though, The Legend of Korra still finds itself being better than a plethora of series out there, thanks to how well-written it is, especially when it comes to the development of the show's world and characters.

Kids who grew up on Regular Show consistently call back to it even today, thanks to how hilarious it is. Many attribute Regular Show to their appreciation for absurdist humor, and absolutely adore the wonderful Mordecai (J. G. Quintel) and Rigby (William Salyers), along with the numerous characters that surround them.

Regular Show gets used as a meme—people use the absurd situations that Mordecai and Rigby consistently find themselves in on a typical Tuesday as a joke, all the time, which has kept the memory of the show alive. People simply love this show, and some even beg for it to make a comeback somehow, despite the ending of the original series being incredibly good and satisfying as it is.

One of the most beloved superhero animated series of the last few years is, without a doubt, Invincible. Based on the Skybound comic book of the same name, written by Robert Kirkman, this show came to Prime Video and captured the hearts of the world almost immediately. Despite some failings in the animation department, at times, Invincible still remains one of the most popular shows on the air at the moment.

Thanks to the work of some amazing vocal performances from the likes of Steven Yeun, J.K. Simmons, Gillian Jacobs, Seth Rogen, Walton Goggins, and many more, the characters pop off the screen and come to life. This, combined with some great storytelling thanks to Kirkman and the new writers on the writing team, makes Invincible such a great watch.

One of the greatest accomplishments to come from Disney Channel/Disney XD is, without a doubt, the ever-iconic Gravity Falls. Brought to the screen by Alex Hirsch, this animated series does an abundance of things right. It crafts a compelling mystery across the entire plot, while crafting just as interesting ones each week in a serialized format.

There's genuinely so much to love about this show. Despite being an animated show on Disney Channel/Disney XD, anyone can watch and enjoy Gravity Falls. The humor is stellar, the voice acting is lively, the story is engaging, and all of the character arcs are compelling. Not to mention, it went the extra mile to get audiences invested by hiding little hints, secrets, clues, and more into the show that could be used to discover more — the intro theme has a backwards message at the end, which changed each season to act as another cipher.

One of the greatest anime ever made comes from the studio that made the extremely popular My Hero Academia, Studio Bones. After a first series that was pretty mixed, they brought the Fullmetal Alchemist franchise back in 2009 with a show that was more accurate and faithful to the original manga, which was called Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood.

Thanks to how amazing the manga by Hiromu Arakawa is, Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood has found itself being one of the most beloved anime of the modern age, with a whopping and clean 100% on Rotten Tomatoes. It's such an excellently told story that audiences can't help but get engaged and care about it. Not to mention, Studio Bones animated it phenomenally well.

Pecan Cream Pie With Brown Sugar Custard

📅 Oct 21, 2025  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Inspired by the towering Praline Pecan Pie served at Das Dutchman Essenhaus in Middlebury, Indiana, this pecan cream pie brings together the best parts of pecan pie and cream pie. A gooey, nut-packed base sits beneath silky brown sugar custard and a pillow of whipped cream, with crunchy candied pecans scattered over the top. It’s a lot of pie—in the best possible way.

The pecan layer is jammed full of butter-toasted nuts, but the secret ingredient is a dollop of sour cream, which helps prevent crystallization and keeps the filling from becoming overly sweet. More sour cream goes into the whipped topping, adding tang and helping it hold its shape. Dark brown sugar and plenty of vanilla give the custard its toffeelike flavor.

This is admittedly more involved than a classic pecan pie, but the work is straightforward and easy to spread over a couple of days, making this a particularly good project to tackle before Thanksgiving Day. Toast the pecans once for both the gooey filling and crunchy topping, and make the crust and pecan layer ahead so all that’s left is the custard, whipped cream, and a little patience while everything chills.

Use a standard 9" metal pie pan. Metal promotes even browning and can accommodate more filling than a standard glass or ceramic dish of the same diameter. This is a generously filled pie, so pan capacity matters.

Can I make pecan cream pie ahead of time?

Yes. You can bake the crust and pecan layer up to one day ahead and store them, covered, at room temperature. Once you’ve added the custard, the pie needs at least four hours in the refrigerator to set. The fully assembled pie can also be refrigerated for up to one day. For the crunchiest topping, scatter the candied pecans over the whipped cream just before serving.

How do I know when the brown sugar custard is thick enough?

The custard should start to bubble, then become glossy and thick like pudding as you stir it over medium heat. This generally takes 3–4 minutes. Don’t rely on refrigeration alone to thicken it: The cornstarch needs to cook sufficiently on the stovetop for the custard to set properly. Keep stirring to prevent lumps or curdling.

Whisk 1⅔ cups (208 g) all-purpose flour, ¼ tsp. baking powder, and 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a large bowl. Add 2 oz. chilled cream cheese, cut into 1" pieces; rub into dry ingredients with your fingers until combined (mixture will be very shaggy). Add 10 Tbsp. chilled unsalted butter, sliced ½" thick, and toss to coat, then smash into smaller, flatter pieces (about chickpea size). Make a well in the center; pour in ¼ cup ice water. Toss with a fork or your hands to distribute, then knead dough in bowl a couple of times to bring together (some dry bits may remain).

Turn dough out onto a lightly dusted surface; dust top of dough with rice flour or all-purpose flour. Roll out to a ½"-thick rectangle. Fold in half, rotate 90°, and fold in half again. Roll out again to a ½"-thick rectangle and repeat folding process, dusting with more flour as needed. Pat into a ½"-thick disk; wrap in plastic. Chill at least 2 hours and, preferably, up to 12 hours.

Place racks in upper and lower thirds of oven; preheat to 425°. Let dough sit at room temperature 5 minutes. Unwrap and roll out on a lightly floured surface to a ¼"–⅛"-thick round. Wrap around rolling pin; transfer to a standard 9"-diameter pie dish (preferably metal). Unfurl into dish, then lift edges and allow dough to slump down into dish. Trim overhang to 1". Fold overhang under and crimp edges as desired. Prick bottom and sides of dough in several places with a fork. Freeze 15 minutes.

Crumple up 2 sheets of parchment paper; smooth out and place over dough. Fill dish with pie weights or dried beans. Set pan on a foil-lined baking sheet and bake crust on lower rack, tenting edges with foil if browning too quickly, until edges are golden brown and bottom is opaque, 35–40 minutes. Remove from oven; reduce oven temperature to 350°. Lift parchment and weights out of pan.

While the crust is baking, whisk 2 large eggs, ⅔ cup (packed; 133 g) dark brown sugar, and remaining 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a medium bowl. Set egg mixture aside to use for the candied pecan topping and the pie filling.

Melt remaining 6 Tbsp. unsalted butter in a medium saucepan set over medium. Add 2½ cups (300 g) raw pecans and cook, stirring gently, until nuts are slightly darkened in color and fragrant, 5–7 minutes. Pour through a fine-mesh sieve set over a small bowl, reserving both the pecans and the browned butter. (You’ll use the same pan to make the custard; no need to wash it.)

Transfer ½ cup toasted pecans to another small bowl to make the topping: Add 1 Tbsp. reserved egg mixture and toss to coat. Spread on a parchment-lined baking sheet and bake on upper rack until dry to the touch and a honeycomb-like texture appears on surface, about 5 minutes. Let candied nuts cool.

For the filling: Finely chop remaining toasted pecans and add to the remaining egg mixture along with the reserved browned butter, 2 Tbsp. sour cream, and 1 tsp. vanilla extract. Stir to combine. Scrape into crust and bake on lower rack until pecan layer is mostly set but still jiggles slightly in the center when gently shaken, 15–18 minutes. Transfer pan to a wire rack; let cool until barely warm, at least 30 minutes.

Do Ahead: Candied pecan topping can be made 3 days ahead; store airtight at room temperature. Crust with pecan layer can be baked 1 day ahead; store, covered with foil, at room temperature.

Heat 1½ cups heavy cream and 1 cup whole milk in the same saucepan you used for the nuts over medium until steaming, about 2 minutes.

Meanwhile, whisk 6 large egg yolks, ¾ cup (packed; 150 g) dark brown sugar, ⅓ cup (40 g) cornstarch, ½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt, and remaining ½ cup whole milk in a medium bowl.

Gradually pour warm cream mixture into egg mixture, whisking constantly until combined. Pour mixture back into pan and cook over medium heat, stirring constantly, until it starts to bubble, then becomes glossy and thick (like pudding), 3–4 minutes. Scrape into a medium bowl, add 6 Tbsp. chilled unsalted butter, cut into pieces, and 4 tsp. vanilla extract, and whisk until butter is melted and custard is emulsified. Cover with plastic wrap, pressing directly onto surface. Let custard stand at room temperature while the pecan layer cools. The pecan layer can still be slightly warm when you add the custard; neither needs to cool completely.

Uncover custard; whisk until smooth. Spoon over gooey pecan layer, spreading evenly to edges. Cover and chill at least 4 hours and up to 1 day.

Whisk ¼ cup sour cream, 3 Tbsp. powdered sugar, remaining 1 tsp. vanilla extract, a pinch of kosher salt, and remaining 1 cup heavy cream in a medium bowl to medium peaks. Spoon over custard and spread into an even layer. Break candied pecans into large pieces and scatter on top.

Do Ahead: Pie can be assembled 1 day ahead. Chill.

Power outages are a common problem during thunderstorms, so it’s a good idea to have a backup plan ready to go when the lights go out. Gas and propane generators are common choices for home backup power, RV use, and camping trips.

Propane generators, for example, burn cleaner than gasoline generators, but tend to be more costly to purchase and operate. Gasoline generators are more efficient, but the fuel has a shorter shelf life and may not always be available in emergencies due to panic buying at local gas stations. This guide outlines other differences between the two that will help you decide which type of generator is right for your home.

Having a basic understanding of how the different types of generators work can help decide on the right choice for your home. A propane generator operates by burning the propane in an internal combustion engine. This process creates mechanical energy, which is used to turn a rotor within a magnetic field to generate electricity that can then be used to power appliances and other electronic devices.

Choosing a propane emergency generator for the home makes it easier to stockpile fuel for emergencies. Ken Goodrich, Founder and CEO of Kenerator, says “Propane is a great option for home backup power because it stores well and burns clean. Even when natural gas is temporarily shut off due to a natural disaster or infrastructure damage, a propane tank will continue to supply power.” Additionally, propane burns cleaner than gasoline, so you can expect the best propane generators to require less maintenance and produce fewer emissions.

Understanding how much propane a generator uses is key to deciding on the right model for your home, since propane generators tend to have lower fuel efficiency than gasoline generators. “Additionally, propane suppliers usually deliver to customers on a routine basis, so the homeowner needs to ensure they have enough propane to make it through an average outage,” notes Goodrich. It should also be mentioned that propane generators typically cost more up front and have higher operating costs, so they may not be the best choice for those looking for a cheap solution.

Whether you are looking to purchase a gas or propane generator, it’s good to know how the appliance operates. Generally, the operation of a gasoline generator can be described as a process that converts chemical energy into mechanical energy, which in turn produces electrical energy. More specifically, the engine burns gasoline to spin a shaft, which rotates magnets or electromagnetic fields near the stator, a stationary coil of wires. This induces a change in the magnetic field, creating an electrical current in the coils, that can then be used to power connected appliances and electrical devices.

The main benefit of a gas generator is that these appliances are more affordable to purchase and operate than propane generators, though there can be some fluctuation in the energy efficiency between specific models, so don’t hesitate to ask how much gas does a generator use when researching potential products.

Goodrich mentions that “portable gasoline generators are good options for camping and construction sites,” so you can make use of your generator even when you aren’t at home. Goodrich goes on to say that “gasoline generators are affordable, portable, easy to find, and simple to use,” making them a great choice for just about any home.

While gasoline generators may be cheaper to purchase and operate, gas does not burn as cleanly as propane, so there is a higher risk that these appliances will break down or require frequent maintenance to keep them in good condition. “Gasoline engines use carburetors, as opposed to mixers, which clog easily if not run on a weekly or monthly schedule,” Goodrich points out.

If you like the potential of both propane and gasoline generators, but can’t decide between one or the other, then you may find the best dual-fuel generators to be a good alternative. These generators work by switching between gasoline and propane using a manual or automatic fuel selector.

While the engine has the same combustion cycle for both fuels, it utilizes a built-in carburetor to atomize gasoline for combustion, but when using propane, the fuel passes through a separate external line and regulator before it is pulled into the engine by the vacuum created by the piston. Goodrich says that dual-fuel generators are “definitely a viable option. A lot of homeowners like having propane for its clean burn and long storage life, with gasoline as a backup if they run through their propane supply.”

The Conference League, be it through their county’s coefficient or their own league rankings, gives teams a chance in Europe that they otherwise may not have access to. While a side could qualify for the Europa or Champions League, they may not be well-equipped for the calibre of the tournament but have a squad that perfectly suits the Conference League.

In its early life thus far, the Conference League has made a claim for being the most unpredictable competition in Europe, with Roma, West Ham United, Olympiacos, Chelsea and Crystal Palace having won the competition.

Along with its other European competitions, the Conference League was revamped ahead of the 2024/25 campaign, with a league stage replacing the traditional group stage formatting. As clubs battle it out to clinch the crown once more, it is worth asking, just how is the prize money in the Conference League is divided.

For the clubs that reached the league stage of the Conference League in the 2026/27 season, they were all awarded £2.8 million for their participation. Not only that, but any victory saw teams awarded a further £349,000, with a draw seeing the sides earn just under £116,000.

Much akin to the new-look format of the Champions League and Europa League, the top eight sides in the Conference League table progress automatically to the round of 16, with teams playing just six games in that stage. Any side placed ninth, 24th or anywhere in-between, progresses to the play-offs, where eight games are contested to determine the final half of the last 16.

Teams that advance from the league stage do so with a reward of £175,000, a number that jumps quite substantially for the sides that were successful in winning their play-off clashes. Qualification for the Conference League round of 16 also means an extra £698,000 comes into the team’s accounts.

Beyond the play-off matches, the remainder of the Conference League’s format is entirely the same as European competitions have been in the past. After the round of 16 sits the quarter-finals, with the teams that qualify for it each year all having a tentative eye on the trophy as one of the competition’s final participants.

In 2026/27, clubs that qualify for the quarter-finals do so with a bonus of just under £1.1 million, with the quarter-finals being the first stage of the Conference League–other than the base league stage–to offer participants a six-figure sum for reaching it.

The semi-finals of the Conference League, at least for the 2026/27 season, take place in the final week of April and the first week of May, with the four teams remaining by that point set to battle it out for a spot in the final. In reaching the final quartet of sides in the competition, the clubs will be awarded about £2.1 million.

Then, to the final itself. Set to be played at Besiktas Park in Turkey on June 2, 2027, it will determine the new Conference League champions. The teams that reach the final will receive almost £3.5 million while the winner will be given an extra £2.6 million on top of that.

The 15 Best War Miniseries of All Time, Ranked

TV miniseries can cover a wide array of topics, from horror to drama, to romance, and even to war. War miniseries are definitely not super common, as war is generally more of a movie thing due to the demanding production value that the genre entails, but that's not to say that many war miniseries haven't come to exist in the age of television. From various countries, networks, and streaming platforms, these miniseries tell a whole lot of stories, ranging from Medieval conflicts to modern warfare.

Many of these series have generated a lot of buzz and have drawn a lot of critical attention due to their outstanding quality, sweeping dramatic flair, or entertainment value, delivering their messages in gradual, bite-sized formats that don't overstay their welcome nor exit too soon. These are the best war miniseries ever made, which any fans of the war movie genre should definitely check out.

Generation War, known as Our Mothers, Our Fathers in its native Germany, is a three-part miniseries set during the Second World War. The series has been the topic of a lot of hot debate, as it follows the perspective of the Nazis and has a lot of major historical inaccuracies. However, it is also undoubtedly well-made and technically impressive, though its hiccups are pretty major.

Some criticized it for its inability or unwillingness to show the Nazis' true motive of purging the world of anyone they deemed unworthy, while others praised it for its harrowing drama and fantastic action. One thing is for sure: this is not a show that is for everyone. But for the right audience, who don't mind delving into some hot-button issues and a fresh stain on the history of Germany, it's pretty enjoyable.

Women at War is a Franco-Belgian miniseries created for Netflix. It is set during World War I and focuses on the lives of four very different women on the home front rather than in active combat. One is a nurse, one is a prostitute, one is a nun, and the last is a factory worker. Though many of them have troubled pasts and some are even actively on the run from law enforcement, their destinies intertwine, and they all wind up supporting the war effort as much as they can.

Generally, World War I shows or movies tend to hone in on the combat, as that's where the most action is. However, Women at War allowed the untold stories of conflict to come to light and show how massive an undertaking the effort was, with ordinary people having to sew uniforms, work in factories, or serve as nurses in military hospitals. Not only does the show emphasize the massive scale of the war and how everybody was forced to help out, but it also shows that women did indeed have an important role in the conflict, even if they weren't on the front lines.

All the Light We Cannot See was positively annihilated by critics, earning scathing reviews and harsh criticisms. But most audiences were a lot nicer to it, earning a divisive score of 27% for critics, but 81% for audiences on Rotten Tomatoes, which might be one of the biggest differences in scores in television. To be fair, this miniseries, based on the novel by Anthony Doerr, was not without its fair share of questionable choices or rough moments.

The four-part story is set in occupied France during World War II, where a blind French woman living in the shadow of the Third Reich whose life intersects with a Wehrmacht soldier who was forcibly conscripted into the military. It may not be the most technically amazing miniseries ever, with some major changes from its source material, and its inability to capture the real emotional depth of the book, but many audiences still found it entertaining, although far from the best thing out there.

Set during the First World War, Birdsong may be short at a mere two episodes, but this miniseries was viewed by millions in its home country of the United Kingdom, and presents a very emotional story in spite of its meager runtime. The show stars Eddie Redmayne and Clémence Poésy as Stephen and Isabelle, respectively. Stephen is a British soldier overseas in France fighting in the Great War, while Isabelle is a French woman living in the countryside who becomes the subject of a love affair with Stephen.

Portraying forbidden love and nightmarish trench conditions in equal measure, Birdsong may not be as famous as some of the other miniseries, but it is emotionally moving, all the same. This one is definitely more of a drama than an action flick, though, so be prepared for something different. Fans of the show commended it for how beautifully it captured the essence of the war and of love, and for the heartfelt performances of its principal cast.

Shōgun is more commonly known for the 2024 TV show based on James Clavell's novel; however, while it began as a miniseries, it is receiving a second season, thus it no longer fits the criteria for this list. In any event, the five-part 1980 miniseries is still amazing on its own. It serves as a perfectly fine (and much more faithful) adaptation of the source material.

The miniseries is set during the dawn of the Edo Period of Japan, which was marked by civil war, political upheaval, and constant societal unrest. It is more commonly known as "Feudal Japan." Though all of the characters and events in this show are fictional, they are inspired by real historical figures. Shōgun is a great show if you like political dramas, Japanese history, or this turbulent period of time that became so internationally famous.

Catch-22 is a satirical dark comedy set during World War II and is once again based on a novel, this time by Joseph Heller. There was a 1970 film adaptation of the book, which is a very good film, so hopes weren't very high for the miniseries. Yet the show is just as good as the movie, sporting a whole new cast and updated filming techniques, making it feel a lot more polished and poignant. Story-wise, it's about a United States Air Force bombardier who is frustrated with the amount of dangerous missions he is being sent on, so tries to weasel his way out of the military through a series of legal loopholes.

There are some heavy hitters in this show, including George Clooney and Hugh Laurie, among others, with much of the action centering on the Mediterranean Theatre of the war. Catch-22 was nominated for two Golden Globes and two Emmys, yet amazingly, didn't win any of them. Regardless, a nomination is more than enough to speak to its quality, which is matched by its high critical scores and rave reviews.

The White Queen is based on Philippa Gregory's novel of the same name, which is set during the Wars of the Roses. This historical conflict took place at the end of the Middle Ages and involved a Civil War in England involving the Royal Houses of Tudor and Lancaster versus the House of York in a series of attempts to seize the throne. The result was a victory for the House of Tudor, from which the current British royal family is descended.

While the conflict itself is pretty famous worldwide, often focusing on Medieval warfare and sword-and-shield combat, what is less famous is the role of women behind the scenes, which is what this story is about. The White Queen examines how many of the royal women pulled strings and executed a series of daring political maneuvers to ensure victory for their house. It might not be the most action-packed series, but it's a fantastic Medieval political drama that deserved every ounce of praise it received.

A Small Light is an eight-part miniseries set during World War II, and the accompanying genocide known around the world as the Holocaust. The main character is Miep Gies (Bel Powley), a Dutch secretary who worked for Otto Frank (Liev Schreiber). Otto Frank is, of course, the father of Anne Frank (Billie Boullet), whose diary became an international sensation after her death. As many already know, the Frank family was Jewish, making them a primary target of the Third Reich's genocide. Gies, being a Christian, risked her life to hide the family from the Nazis.

Even though she was eventually discovered and the Frank family was abducted, with Anne dying in a labour camp shortly thereafter, Gies is still regarded as a hero for putting everything on the line to do what was right. It provides an interesting new perspective on one of the most famous historical accounts of the Third Reich's evil, and shows how anyone can be a hero in their own way. Of course, one should take warning, though, as this is obviously a real tear-jerker.

Masters of the Air serves as a companion piece to the classic miniseries Band of Brothers, taking audiences high above occupied Europe during World War II, where a British bombing squadron conducts a series of raids against key locations to the Allied war effort. With a new cast of younger, rising actors like Austin Butler and Barry Keoghan, Masters of the Air is a welcome return to the small screen for the Band of Brothers universe, and is proof that it hasn't lost its charm, even all these years later.

Winning the Emmy for Outstanding Sound Design, Masters of the Air offered a fresh spin and a new perspective on some of the most classic war stories, allowing audiences to see them with a bird's-eye view, experiencing the tragedy and hardships that Royal Air Force members experienced above the clouds of the tumult on the ground. There are some huge inaccuracies regarding its historical aspects, but in terms of sheer entertainment value, Masters of the Air is definitely up there.

Chief of War is something of a passion project for creator and star Jason Momoa. Owing to Momoa's Hawaiian heritage, the story is set in the 19th Century, during a time when the Hawaiian Islands had yet to become a part of the US. The islands were fragmented into four kingdoms, with one of the most famous being the kingdom of Maui, which was ruled by Kamehameha I (Kaina Makua).

The series follows Ka'iana (Momoa), a historic Hawaiian warrior who served under Kamehameha I, waging a bloody conquest against the other island kingdoms after returning home from a long journey. Chief of War is such an authentic miniseries, with versions in both English and Hawaiian, for that extra level of immersion. Better yet, it talks about a historical conflict that is often ignored or left out of history books, yet is rich and fascinating.
