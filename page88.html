Matt Damon's return as Jason Bourne has received a promising new update, although the long-awaited sequel still faces one major condition.

Damon first played the former CIA assassin in 2002's The Bourne Identity, returning for The Bourne Supremacy (2004), The Bourne Ultimatum (2007), and Jason Bourne (2016). Despite the encouraging update, the sequel has not officially been greenlit.

The comments follow Damon's previous update about the Bourne franchise, in which he expressed interest in another installment. Speaking with Rich Eisen during his press tour for Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey, Damon said that if another Bourne movie moves forward, he wants it to feel like the original trilogy. He also explained that, unlike the James Bond franchise, the Bourne movies follow one continuing story rather than sending their central character on a separate mission with each installment.

Quand la vis d'Archimède devient un moteur moléculaire : mon plongée dans NexoPeek

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

J’ai lu l’article de ScienceDaily le 30 octobre 2026 en sirotant un café froid dans mon bureau de thèse. Un moteur moléculaire bidirectionnel, inspiré de la vis d’Archimède, actionné par l’électricité, capable d’augmenter la conductivité d’un facteur 100. Une machine vieille de deux mille ans, réinventée à l’échelle du nanomètre. La phrase a fait tilt : « Et si je pouvais vraiment voir cette vis tourner, sentir la torsion entre deux atomes, mesurer la distance angström qui sépare le rotor du stator ? »

Depuis des mois, je bataillais avec des fichiers PDB sur un écran 24 pouces, à tourner des modèles en boule, à zoomer, à cliquer, à exporter des images pour mes articles. La structure de ce moteur (code PDB 8XYZ, déposée la veille) était là, quelque part dans RCSB, mais l’explorer restait une gymnastique de souris. J’ai pensé à mon casque Meta Quest qui dormait dans le tiroir, acheté pour tester des prototypes de visualisation VR. J’ai ouvert l’application NexoPeek, développée par Seven Sportz, et j’ai lancé le mode « Professional PDB Viewer ».

La recherche intelligente a résolu le nom du gène en un clin d’œil : grâce à la résolution de synonymes UniProt, le moteur a été récupéré depuis RCSB PDB, UniProtKB, AlphaFold DB, SWISS-MODEL et PDBe en une seule requête. Pas de copier‑coller d’identifiants, pas de téléchargement manuel. Le modèle s’est affiché en mode « cartoon » avec le preset Mol* « Publication », prêt pour une capture d’écran de qualité article.

J’ai basculé en « Room View ». Le moteur moléculaire a flotté au centre de mon bureau, les parois de la pièce restant visibles grâce au passthrough. J’ai pu marcher autour, tendre la main, attraper le rotor virtuel avec mes doigts (le suivi de main natif du Quest fonctionne sans contrôleur). La sensation d’échelle humaine — le rotor fait environ 5 nm de diamètre, mais ici il occupe la place d’un ballon de basket — change radicalement la perception de la géométrie.

Curieux, j’ai ouvert l’outil « AI Pocket Prediction ». Le moteur propose deux moteurs : le cloud‑based PrankWeb/P2Rank et un moteur hors‑ligne intégré. J’ai lancé le premier ; en quelques secondes, une série de poches druggables s’est illuminée en rouge translucide sur la surface du stator. L’interface affiche le score de chaque poche, le volume, les résidus impliqués. J’ai noté la poche 3, proche du site de liaison du ligand expérimental, et j’ai lancé le « Ligand Inspector » pour y charger le composé co‑cristallisé (récupéré via PubChem en un clic). Le réseau de liaisons hydrogène s’est dessiné en traits pointillés, et j’ai pu vérifier la distance exacte entre l’oxygène du ligand et l’azote d’une lysine clé : 2,8 Å, mesuré avec le « Distance Calculator » en pointant simplement les deux atomes.

Ensuite, j’ai voulu comparer ce moteur à une hélicase connue pour voir si le mécanisme de torsion était conservé. J’ai utilisé l’alignement structurel basé sur Foldseek : j’ai chargé la structure de la hélicase (PDB 5ABC) depuis AlphaFold DB, lancé la superposition, et obtenu un RMSD de 1,9 Å sur le cœur du domaine. Le rapport d’alignement, généré automatiquement, m’a donné les résidus équivalents et m’a permis d’émettre l’hypothèse que le même principe de vis pourrait être exploité pour concevoir un inhibiteur allostérique.

Tout cela s’est passé en moins d’une heure. J’ai pu exporter des rendus haute résolution (cartoon, surface, B‑factor heat) directement depuis les six presets Mol* sans quitter le casque. J’ai même enregistré une courte vidéo en mixed reality pour la présenter à mon directeur de thèse lors de notre réunion hebdomadaire : le moteur flottant au-dessus de la table de conférence, les poches druggables pulsant, les distances angström s’affichant en temps réel. Il a souri, a dit « C’est exactement ce qu’il nous faut pour le prochain grant ».

Ce qui m’a le plus marqué, c’est la fluidité entre le mode éducatif et le mode recherche. Les douze visites guidées — « Biotechnology Basics », « Foundations of Life », « Pathways & Interactions », « Medicines in Action », « Genetic Technologies », « Green Biotech » — sont narrées, basées sur des structures réelles du PDB, et m’ont permis de revisiter les bases (hémoglobine, glycolyse, CRISPR‑Cas9) avant de plonger dans mon analyse pointue. Le passage de l’un à l’autre se fait sans rechargement, grâce à une architecture modulaire qui sépare le moteur de rendu (Mol* via WebXR) du backend de recherche (API REST vers RCSB, UniProt, AlphaFold, SWISS-MODEL, PDBe) et des services d’analyse (PrankWeb, Foldseek, PubChem).

En tant que développeur, j’apprécie que l’application supporte l’ingestion de fichiers locaux (.pdb, .cif, .mol2, .gro, .xyz, .dx, .xtc, .py) et permette d’y superposer des champs électrostatiques, des définitions de liaisons personnalisées ou des volumes de densité électronique. Private Signed URLs for AI-Generated Images: A Node.js SaaS Governance Checklist

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The constraint that changes this storage decision is governance: a support customer must be able to retrieve the right generated report without turning an object key into an authorization token. Short answer: keep report images private, make the application prove tenant ownership, and issue a short-lived signed URL only after that check. Evaluate US or EU placement, S3-compatible behavior, recovery controls, and on-call work as evidence in a release checklist, not as a feature-list contest.

For an authenticated customer-support report, there should be a durable chain from request to object: session identity, tenant, report ID, object key, content type, size, and retention state. The database owns the relationship. A prefix such as tenants/t-184/reports/r-9021/summary.png helps operators understand storage, but it is not an authorization mechanism, and a user-controlled path must never be parsed to infer ownership.

The useful review question is simple: can an engineer explain why this caller may read this object without looking at the URL? If the answer is no, the design has put policy in the wrong place. A gallery endpoint that signs before completing the tenant lookup may pass a demo, then disclose another customer's image when someone guesses a report ID. No clever key naming fixes that.

Keep the service credential on the server. The browser can receive temporary read authority; it should not receive the credential that can mint authority for other objects. That boundary matters during support investigations too, because signed query strings and credentials do not belong in logs.

Treat “US or EU” as an input to a written control, not as a region label you vaguely remember from a dashboard. Record where objects live, where report generation runs, where customer requests are served, whether support staff may access the data, and which transfers are permitted. Then test the actual subset of the interface: private upload, metadata lookup, signed download, deletion, and multipart completion if large images require it.

The language choice does not alter the trust boundary. Node.js can authenticate the request and call a presigning library, while the storage adapter exposes only the small operation the application needs. The code below is deliberately a generic contract. It keeps the authorization decision ahead of signing and leaves provider-specific details behind the adapter.

Five minutes is a policy example, not a promise. A longer lifetime reduces repeat signing but extends exposure after a session change; a shorter one increases API traffic and may expire during a slow download. Tie the lifetime to report sensitivity, expected transfer duration, and the revocation promise. A signed URL does not provide instant revocation by itself.

For browser uploads, add CORS to the test plan. Cross-origin requests depend on the allowed origin, methods, and headers, so a presigned upload still needs a browser policy the storage endpoint will honor. The MDN guidance in References is the standards starting point; the production origin still needs an end-to-end test. Use a server-mediated upload when the policy cannot be expressed and verified.

Capacity planning starts with ranges: reports per day, images per report, median and maximum object size, reads per report, link requests per session, retention days, and cleanup lag. Model a burst separately from the average. A pretty monthly estimate that ignores a popular gallery or a generation retry is not a capacity plan.

Define an access SLO for authorized requests receiving a usable link, then separate SLOs for report generation, deletion, cleanup, and any promised revocation behavior. A reachable bucket is not evidence that a customer can retrieve the correct report. Measure link issuance latency, download outcomes, 429 responses, expired-link complaints, orphan age, and failed deletions; keep tenant and report identifiers available for diagnosis without logging signed query strings.

Pricing belongs after those constraints. Storage bytes, requests, and transfer should be calculated from the capacity sheet, and a published pricing page is evidence for one billing model rather than a portable estimate for every service. I’m not sure which cost line will dominate until object count, read frequency, and retention are measured. Your mileage may vary.

Private signed access is not suitable when the product needs permanent public image URLs, static-site hosting, or anonymous CDN-style delivery. Keep the report store private and add a separate delivery layer with its own cache invalidation and abuse controls. Switching a private bucket to public read is not rollback; it changes the access model.

It is also the wrong fit when the team cannot meet its own recovery objective with the selected service controls. Unique report IDs reduce accidental key reuse, but they do not make an overwrite recoverable. If versioning or object-lock semantics are required and unavailable, add an independent recovery copy or select a service whose controls satisfy the objective. Private delivery protects reads, not writers.

The trade-off is deliberate: signed delivery preserves tenant authorization, but it adds link issuance, expiry policy, and cleanup work that anonymous delivery does not. That limitation is acceptable for authenticated reports and a poor fit for a public image catalog.

In a non-production tenant, create a known report, upload its image through the server path, request it as the owning user, and fetch the returned link without exposing a service credential. Verify that another tenant receives no link, a deleted report cannot mint a new link, and an expired link stops working. Test a small image and the largest permitted image; assumptions often fail at the boundary. Repeat the checks after a simulated region or provider change, because the migration path is part of the access-control design: the copied object must still resolve to the authoritative report record, the destination must meet the same residency rule, the counts and hashes must agree, the read-path SLO must hold under the expected burst, and the application must be able to stop minting new links without making the old private data public.

Add a race test with two workers targeting one report. Decide whether the second writer is rejected, returns the existing result, or creates a new version. Make that policy visible in database state and metrics. A silent last-writer-wins result is a product decision disguised as storage behavior.

Rollback happens at the application boundary: stop issuing new signed links, pause or drain writers, preserve report-to-key records, and keep the bucket private while investigating. If a provider or region must change, copy objects from authoritative records, verify counts and hashes, exercise the destination read-path SLO, and switch link issuance only after that evidence exists. Keep the old read path for the documented rollback window.

The decision rule is narrow: choose the tested interface and residency, recovery, and operational controls that meet the written requirements. Familiar SDK ergonomics cannot compensate for missing evidence.

Over Pinchot Pass — John Muir Trail Day 10

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The night before, at Rae Lake, I ate some kind of buffalo stew, which was supposed to provide about 900 calories.  It is funny how flavor (usually) takes a backseat to caloric value on the trail.  I am just so conscious that I am exerting myself — a lot — and need to maximize my fuel intake.

After dinner I had my headlamp on in the tent, probably dictating my notes, and was not aware that Evelyn (or Serena?) was taking pictures of my tent in the darkness under a starry sky.

Night camping at Rae Lakes (photo courtesy of Evelyn or Serena)

My sleep started deep and sound.  Then I did wake up, coughing a bit, and I thought, “Oh, no!”  But it was still much less than before, and I was very tired, so I slept again.  I vaguely remember dreaming about how some people were organizing something-or-other and I was confused how it was going to work.  Maybe it meant I was wondering how to manage this journey?

Around 4:40 in the morning, when I got up to pee, I checked the Garmin and saw the time.  I thought, “Well, I think I’m not going to go back to sleep.”  So I didn’t; instead I fetched my food from the bear box, and ate my breakfast.  But I felt sluggish, like I was swimming through molasses.

How sluggish?  Well, I rose at 4:40 but I did not get on the trail until 7:00 a.m.  Despite everything, it was only thirty minutes later than the previous day, but it was far from efficient.  I said goodbye to Evelyn and Serena; they were heading southbound, and I was heading northbound.  I left the crowded campsite and started on my way.

One consequence of the Rae Lake crowds is that it was hard to find a private place to dig a cathole.  I decided to wait for a better place.  About a half hour down the trail, I pulled off and found a spot that was quite lovely and dug my hole and did my business.  In the middle of it, a deer appeared in the meadow next to me and gazed at me curiously.

Cat hole in the foreground, amazing view in the background!

Less than a half mile further, I discovered a beautiful camping site with a bear box and no crowds at all.  I thought, “Oh my gosh, if I’d just known and been willing to take the risk to go a little further yesterday, it would have been a much nicer situation.”  It was too late for me, but I resolved to tell any southbound hikers I encountered that they would be more comfortable stopping at the last camp site before the main Rae Lake location.

More beautiful and fewer crowds — the place to be!

As I looked at the long day ahead of me, I realized it would be 6-7 miles, all downhill, followed by 8-9 miles all uphill, followed by a mile or two of downhill to my next camp site at Lake Marjorie.  In elevation I started at 10,500 feet, would drop to 8,500 feet, and then would cross Pinchot Pass at 12,200 feet.  I planned for a long day.

I did about four miles on that nice downhill to start the day when I encountered a small stream I had to cross.  In general, these stream crossings had not been too challenging, frankly.  I brought my water shoes explicitly because I thought I would wade through river crossings, perhaps as much a thigh-deep, and there hadn’t been any like that – at least so far.

This one was no different, a two-stone hop across with a couple of different options as to route.  That, it turned out, was the problem.  As I prepared to take my second step I decided to go via a different sequence of stones, and I turned slightly to change direction.  It was a slight turn, nothing notable about it at all.  Except… Suddenly I felt a sharp pain in my left knee.

I let out a yowl and finished crossing.  But I was worried.  Clearly, I had injured something. The only question was, what was it and how badly was it damaged?

I was still able to move it, so that was a good first sign.  Working it gently, it barked at me in certain angles; but just going straight ahead, it seemed not to bother me too much.

I decided to take an Ibuprofen at the bottom of the hill to prevent swelling from whatever it was that had happened.  Five minutes later I changed my mind, thinking, “Why am I waiting  to take the medication?  I just sustained the injury.  I should take the ibuprofen immediately, right NOW.”  And I did.

I continued down gingerly, favoring my knee a bit, worried that this might be something serious, and not knowing whether it would be or not.

My main distraction was lizards.  Yes!  I had observed no reptiles at all above 10,000 feet, but coming lower they started to appear.  I think they were most likely Western Fence Lizards, but they could also have been Sagebrush Lizards.  Because of my focus on my knee, I did not photograph them, but I resolved to do so the next time they appeared.

Finally, I reached the bottom of the downhill.  There was a suspension bridge to cross there, and it was one of the bouncier ones I had ever been on.  With a GoPro I would gladly have filmed myself crossing it, but with my hand-held phone it was too easy to imagine it flying out of my hand into the rushing waters below.

From solid ground I took a couple of pictures of the bridge anyway.

Then I crossed to the other side and began the big uphill.  I was facing around 3,600 feet of elevation gain, while worrying about my stupid knee.  I also worried that the trail might feel kind of endless, and it did feel kind of endless.

Along the way I started leapfrogging another hiker named Amy.  Our hiking styles almost made it inevitable.  I would go fast but then stop and rest; she kept a steady pace.  So, I would go by her moving faster, and she would move by me when I stopped.  Then I would resume and pass her again, and the cycle would continue.  At one point, when I was ahead, I saw a grouse.  I wondered if it would still be there when she came by.

The good news in all of this was that either the Ibuprofen was working, or my knee was not badly injured, or both.

Amy and I had both set Lake Marjorie, on the other side of Pinchot Pass, as our destination for the night.  This was the first camping space about a mile and a half below the summit.

We kept ascending.  The one advantage was that the ascent took place over many miles, so it was rarely going up very steeply.  But over the course of the afternoon, it was feeling quite relentless.  Only the series of waterfalls along the way kept me engaged and appreciative.

…another waterfall falling in perpetuity!

Approaching Pinchot Pass I ran into a guy coming downhill toward me.  We chatted and I learned that, like me, he was 69 years old.  He seemed very demoralized.  He said he felt like he was shuffling, like he could barely even do it.  He said he was planning to get off at Kearsarge Pass and probably would not return.  I said, “Well, what you should do is get off at Kearsarge and go to Independence and then spend a day there doing nothing.  After a day off trail, your mind will be clearer, and then you can decide if that’s the end for you, or if you do actually want to come back.”  That was my recommendation; I don’t know if he took it.

Nearing the top, there were many rocks patterned with white striations.  I wanted to turn to Sam and ask the geologist to explain what had caused them.  But of course, he was back in Boulder by then.

You can see the white stripes in the foreground rocks, but also in the background cliff face. Quartz maybe? Sam could have told me…

The end of the trail up to the pass was a series of switchbacks.  You couldn’t really see where they were leading until, suddenly, ta-da!, you had reached the saddle of the pass.

It was a really, really hard day.  I started at seven a.m. and I got to the pass at exactly five p.m.  If you do the math, that’s ten hours.  And I still had the mile and a half to go down to the lake.

The views over the lakes as the sun was setting were beautiful.  Amy finally caught up to me again, at a stream where I stopped to top off my water supply before making camp.  That late in the afternoon, we were both worried that the camping spots might already be filled up.  But when I saw a truly beautiful location, on nice flat sandy ground, I said, “That’s the one for me!”  Amy kept going to the next spot further down the trail, but I called it a day.

Arriving at Rae Lake the previous day, I was so tired it was difficult to function.  On this occasion, at Lake Marjorie, I set up calmly and reasonably efficiently, and it just felt much more natural than it had up to that point.  I pitched my tent, soaked my food, and set up my sleeping system, of course.  But I also went down to the lake shore where I washed my socks and washed my feet.  While I had my feet in the lake’s icy cold water, I thought, “Oh, I need to ice my knee too.”  So, I went a little deeper into the lake and got my knee in the icy cold water as well.

Unfortunately, my dinner was disgusting.  Some of these freeze-dried meals work great for a cold-soaker, but some really need boiling water to be edible.  I just wish I had kept track of which was which for future hikes!  Dinner was the low point of the day (other than my knee tweak), but I ate it for the calories.

Early Evening by Lake Marjorie. Notice my tent to the right of the boulder and — at that point — nothing to the left of the boulder.

Sitting in my tent pitched next to a large boulder it was getting colder-and-colder at our high elevation.  It was definitely a night for my sleeping bag liner!  In deep twilight I heard a large animal moving around on the other side of the big boulder.  Initially I was quite spooked, but when I checked I saw that a late-arriving hiker was setting up camp there.

For a moment I was annoyed that my delicious privacy had been disturbed.  Then I overcame that feeling and went over to welcome the newcomer and see if they needed anything.  It was a solo hiker named Sally, born in China but now an Australian citizen, also NOBO on the John Muir Trail.  She was friendly and we chatted for a few moments before I returned to my tent to call it a night.

It had been a 17-mile day, my longest yet on the trail.  Sleep came easily.
