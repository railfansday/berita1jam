Tommy’s Take on Red–the Color Fashion Can’t (and Shouldn’t) Quit

📅 Jun 21, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

One of fashion’s earliest street-style photographers, Tommy Ton has always had a unique point of view on what makes an outfit sing. His subjects—individuals with style and substance in equal measure—aren’t following trends. They’re expressing something deeper, and that difference is what makes his shots so compelling. Sure, the clothes are always great, but his takes capture the true essence of the wearer. In monthly installments surrounding a fashion theme, he'll explore the outfits that caught his eye and why. Here’s Tommy’s Take.

There is something about red that fashion simply cannot get enough of. Crimson, poppy, scarlet, cherry, oxblood—the shades are seemingly endless, and so is our fascination with them. What began as an easy way to inject color into an otherwise neutral wardrobe has evolved into something much bigger. At this point, I would say fashion has developed a full-blown case of scarlet fever.

Traditionally, I associated red with evening dressing. There was something inherently glamorous, perhaps even a little theatrical, about wearing it after dark. But that distinction has all but disappeared. Red has found its way into every corner of the wardrobe, from casual daytime looks to evening ensembles, and some of the most compelling ways I've seen it worn lately are decidedly understated.

Not so long ago, a red accessory was the easiest entry point: a handbag, a pair of shoes, or perhaps a flash of red socks. It was the little punctuation mark that made an outfit feel more considered. Today, however, we're embracing red as the starting point of an entire look. Take Polo Ralph Lauren's red Harrington jacket, a beautifully executed Chanel cap-toe pump, or even a luxurious red sweater casually tied around the waist. I particularly love the latter, bringing an unexpected flash of color to jeans or tailored trousers. It's a simple styling gesture that can transform an entire outfit.

And then there is the appeal of wearing red from head to toe. Who could forget the monochromatic look that came down Michael Rider's Celine runway this past season? There was an almost collective gasp. Seeing such a saturated look presented with that kind of conviction was a reminder of just how arresting the color can be. Red wasn't merely an accent; it was the entire statement.

There is a certain pleasure in committing to one color, allowing different textures, proportions, and shades to create interest. A scarlet knit paired with matching trousers, or a cherry-red anorak worn over an equally saturated ensemble, makes quite a statement. After seasons of pared-back palettes and quiet luxury, there is something refreshing about dressing with a little more conviction.

Depending on the shade, red can be romantic, provocative, sporty, polished, or even a little rebellious. Cherry has a playful quality, scarlet carries a certain theatricality, while oxblood offers a moodier, more understated interpretation. Perhaps that's why our infatuation with the color has lasted so long. It has been reinterpreted countless times, yet somehow manages to feel exciting all over again.

Whatever the reason, I'm not quite ready to give up on red. Whether worn as a subtle accent, an everyday staple, or embraced from head to toe, it has a way of making even the most familiar wardrobe feel exciting again. And judging by the way we're wearing it now, I don't see our scarlet fever subsiding anytime soon.

Is more more, or is less more? For years, we’ve been told that good taste is knowing when to stop. Coco Chanel’s famous advice about looking in the mirror before leaving the house and removing one thing has become something of a fashion commandment for many. But I’ve always wondered what happens when we do the opposite. What if, instead of taking something away, you add another necklace, another ring, another earring, another bracelet? What if the most interesting thing about getting dressed is not knowing when to stop, but having the confidence not to?

After seasons of quiet luxury and wardrobes edited down to the essentials, I find myself drawn to a more expressive approach to accessorizing. Somewhere along the way, restraint became synonymous with sophistication, and the absence of adornment was elevated into a marker of taste. But minimalism, for all its elegance, can become its own uniform. When everyone is wearing the same monogram pendant or oversized belt buckle, how much individuality is left?

Jewelry is most interesting when it disrupts that uniformity. I love the woman with rings on nearly every finger, a wrist stacked with cuffs and bracelets, necklaces layered at different lengths and crowded with pendants, and earrings that seem to be having a conversation of their own. There is something wonderfully assured about a look that refuses to be fully resolved. It doesn’t ask whether every piece matches, whether the proportions are balanced, or whether one more accessory might be too much. It simply embraces the pleasure of wearing what you love.

What strikes me most about this more-is-more approach is that it rarely feels calculated. The best examples are always a little imperfect: a delicate chain mixed with a heavy pendant; a diamond ring beside an inexpensive silver band; a charm-laden brooch that clinks with every movement. Nothing needs to match, nor should it. The most compelling combinations look as if they came together over time, collected from different places, people, and phases of one’s life. The result feels lived-in rather than meticulously styled.

Perhaps this is why the current appetite for accessorizing feels so refreshing. Not everything needs to be versatile, timeless, or sensible. Some things are worth wearing simply because they make you happy.

So the next time you reach for just one necklace, consider adding another. Then perhaps a third. Put on the rings you usually save for evenings out, stack your bracelets, and let the earrings be a little outrageous. You can always take something off. But there is a certain joy in discovering that you don’t want to.

In an industry as vast and layered as fashion, it’s worth asking: Who inspires the tastemakers? Who shapes the eye of the people who decide what is photographed and published, what earns a place on the runway, and what ultimately finds its way onto the racks of the world’s most coveted department stores?

Designers long occupied the highest tier of fashion’s hierarchy of influence. Together with editors, journalists, stylists, and buyers, they defined what we desired before we had the language to desire it ourselves. Now that we live under the influence of algorithms and celebrity and influencer culture, genuine style has become increasingly difficult to distinguish from mere visibility. True originality is quieter—and infinitely more compelling.

Several times a year, the industry converges on the world’s fashion capitals. Equal parts work and reunion, Fashion Week carries the unmistakable energy of summer camp, bringing together friends and colleagues who have spent months apart. Between the action of the shows themselves, another spectacle quietly unfolds on the sidewalks and in the queues. Fashion week remains one of the world’s greatest people-watching opportunities.

It is often here, unexpectedly, that the most memorable style reveals itself. Not on the runway, but on an unfamiliar face glimpsed again and again throughout the week. You begin to notice the subtle details: the effortless knot of a belt cinching a jacket, the unexpected layering of heirloom pendants, the confidence of someone dressing entirely for themselves rather than for the cameras. Before long, you’ve developed a style crush on someone whose name you may never learn.

This is an ode to those women: the ones who influence the influential. The insiders whose instinct, rather than their following, has earned the admiration of fashion’s most discerning insiders. They are, quite simply, your favorite fashion insider’s favorite fashion insider.

Fashion experts have always emphasized investing in a wardrobe of foundational essentials: the perfect white shirt, a tailored suit, a little black dress, and so on. But once you’ve found those building blocks, the next question becomes: how do you avoid getting caught up in the constant seasonal cycle of trends just to make your closet feel relevant again?

The answer is quite simple: focus on accessories. Small accents, whether it’s a bag in an interesting color, a brooch that has a deeper meaning, or a belt that invites a closer look, can instantly give your wardrobe more clarity and a fresh perspective. Clothes come and go, but a beautifully curated collection of accessories stays in rotation for longer than a single season when you invest wisely in the right pieces. After all, no one ever said you can have too many accessories.

If there has been one brand that has sparked a renewed interest in accessorizing, it’s without a doubt Celine under Michael Rider. From the very first look of his debut collection, what immediately caught your eye was that wrist full of charm bracelets, the hand stacked with rings, and the oversized gold medallion belt buckle slightly peeking out from beneath a blazer. It was a sign that all those never-ending discussions about quiet luxury were finally going to be put to rest.

It was a clever exercise in merchandising as well, as you couldn’t help but be drawn to all those delicious accessories that perfectly accented each look. It was like a fashion enthusiast’s version of walking into a candy store. Whether it was a silk foulard effortlessly tied around a model’s jacket or half-tucked beneath a lapel, it made you want to replicate the styling and adopt it into your wardrobe immediately. You could sense that this collection was going to influence fashion at the highest level before trickling down to the masses; the true mark of an impactful collection is when its imprint can be seen on fashion insiders the following season.

Fast-forward nine months later to Paris Fashion Week, outside a show at the Petit Palais, and all those chic gestural styling cues from the Celine runway had made their way to the street in an unexpected masterclass in styling. Caught in conversation, it was quite a sight to see how three fashion professionals (Sarah Park, Ramya Giangola, and Lisa Aiken) unintentionally complemented one another and somehow captured the mood of the moment.

Everything that felt timeless yet modern was there: glimpses of bold primary colors, subtle accents of gold and silver hardware, small leather wallets and pouchettes, and classic black frames. It was very much in the French way of dressing, where the individual nuances of each look made you curious and encouraged you to reconsider what you already own. After closely scanning each look from head to toe, it became clear that each of these women had adopted elements of the Celine preppy bourgeois aesthetic. Yet it wasn’t a head-to-toe runway look that made their outfits stand out. Rather, it was the fact that each woman carefully borrowed styling cues from Celine and made them distinctly her own. You could easily sense the playfulness and joy in the way they got dressed.

What’s crucial when reevaluating your wardrobe each season is looking for pieces that offer day-to-night versatility and can be effortlessly thrown together without too much thought. Practicality matters, but it’s also about allowing yourself to step slightly outside your comfort zone. Whether it’s buying the right jewelry or embracing color, you don’t want to feel intimidated by your accessories or uncertain about when it’s appropriate to wear them. You want these timeless investment pieces to feel like lifelong treasured friends that come out to play when the occasion is right. They are ultimately what truly bring an outfit together.

15 'Invincible' Episodes That Are Considered Masterpieces, Ranked

📅 Jun 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Invincible is, without a doubt, one of the most popular superhero animated series of all time. In the vein of The Boys, this Prime Video show is brutal, bloody, and vulgar. However, it is filled to the brim with far more heart, love, and soul that helps define this series as one not of ridiculous gore and shock value, but telling a story about heroism, the price of it, and what it means to truly be one as a young person.

Mark Grayson (Steven Yeun) is very well-written, and the world around him is also very well-established. Not only does Invincible have the writing of the original Invincible (2003) comic series writer, Robert Kirkman, behind it, but a bunch of new writers are helping out now, as well. Having a whole writers' room supporting the show and giving new ideas and reframing old ones has helped Invincible become something even more amazing than the original book, in some people's opinions.

One of the greatest parts of Invincible is the way it handles morality and how gray it can be. Mark Grayson is consistently having this tested in each episode, and one of the best examples of an episode that does this is "All I Can Say Is I'm Sorry." It also brings the iconic Aaron Paul into the fold, playing Powerplex.

What the sixth episode of this third season does so well is it covers what it is like to be a normal, unassuming civilian in the world of Invincible. With just one terrible battle, one's loved ones, livelihood, and more could be taken from them—exactly what happened to Powerplex, and what led him to go crazy. This new villain is the literal physical manifestation of Mark's own questions about morality and the gray area between right and wrong. It's incredibly thought-provoking.

With how things played out at the end of Season 1 and the beginning of Season 2, Nolan Grayson (J.K. Simmons) never really got the chance to spend much time with his other, younger son, Oliver Grayson (Christian Convery). After Mark is mortally wounded in his rematch against the horrifying Conquest (Jeffrey Dean Morgan), the Grayson boys must take refuge on a planet and wait for the eldest son to recover.

With this, Oliver and Nolan get to spend some real father and son time together. This takes some time to really slow down and give ample room for character development, and it works very well. This feels like a natural slow point between what came before and what's to come. The character work for these three—less so Mark compared to the other two—is wonderfully executed, and leaves this episode proving that an Invincible episode doesn't have to have a bunch of action to be successful.

There's no doubt that Invincible Season 1 is amazing and a great start to this iconic series. One of the best episodes in that first entry is Invincible Season 1, Episode 5 — the introduction to the fan favorite Battle Beast (Michael Dorn). This also features Mark showcasing how naive he began his career — wanting to simply do good, without thinking too hard about his actions at the same time.

When taking a chance on a team-up with a crime lord known as Titan (Mahershala Ali) to take down a far more dangerous one — Machine Head (Jeffery Donovan) — Mark gets himself into a plethora of trouble. The pacing in this episode is stellar and naturally builds up to the action-packed conclusion perfectly.

There's no question that Invincible Season 4 is jam-packed with action, stakes, and fast pacing. This is exemplified by how strong a start it had, with one of the show's best episodes coming within the first two episodes of the season. "I'll Give You the Grand Tour" not only tells the origin of the Viltrumites, but has some hard-hitting action to blend with it.

Among all this, though, the voice acting really stands out, with the whole cast bringing their A-game for such an important episode. The story here of Nolan's origin is actually not featured in the comics, making this an all-new story for comic readers. Not to mention, Nolan and Alan (Seth Rogen) finally get some good, well-deserved screen time together—being a pair that fans absolutely adore.

The penultimate episode of Invincible Season 1 is one that is incredibly thrilling, as Nolan's plot that's been brewing in the background finally comes to a head. Featuring an awesome fight between him and The Immortal (Ross Marquand), this episode shows just how truly unstoppable Omni-Man is, making the fight to come between him and Invincible feel all the more scary.

This episode isn't just action, though, as it has some great character work for both Mark and Atom Eve (Gillian Jacobs)—with him approaching her for advice and them getting some great time for character work before the big finale. "We Need to Talk" perfectly blends action and character work, making it exceptionally well-written.

One of the most complex and interesting characters in Invincible isn't just Mark Grayson, but none other than Cecil Stedman (Walton Goggins). No other episode fully depicts this like the likes of "A Deal with the Devil." The divide that inevitably comes between Invincible and the old, but badass man is one of the best conflicts in the series. After the betrayal of Nolan Grayson — Omni-Man — Cecil's trust in Viltrumites and, honestly, powerful people in general, is at an all-time low. Not to mention, the episode also depicts an origin of sorts for Stedman.

All of this culminates in a conflict that pulls the two apart. When it's revealed that Cecil has been working with both Darkwing II (Cleveland Berto) as an agent and D.A. Sinclair (Eric Bauza) to create the ReAnimen — two people he considers to be vile killers — Mark flips out and attacks him, causing him to reveal his hand: he has implanted anti-Viltrumite technology in Mark's head — a total invasion of privacy. This severs their alliance for seemingly good and puts Mark completely on his own, as well as sowing conflict among the members of the new Guardians of the Globe.

Despite the fact that it sometimes suffers from mid-season slumps in animation quality, the Invincible team always goes all-out for their big finales, and the finale for Season 2, "I Thought You Were Stronger," is a great example. This is one of the first times that Mark's family is genuinely put in danger, and it causes him to put to the test all the moral standards that he holds for himself as Angstrom Levy (Sterling K. Brown) severely harms his mother, Debbie Grayson, and holds her and his little brother hostage.

Not only does this result in an awesome fight, but it features Mark being sent to tons of other universes, including a great reference to Spider-Man and the story the two have together in the comics, as seen in Marvel Team-Up #14 (2005). Now Agent Spider (Josh Keaton), the Web-Slinger, is voiced by none other than Josh Keaton, voice of The Spectacular Spider-Man himself. With a great cameo and phenomenal fight, the Season 2 finale is one to remember.

Four teams, four completely different reasons to put yourself in danger for strangers. This is the roster you actually belong on, what they would want from you, and what it would cost you to stay.

You belong where the stakes are largest and the personalities are loudest. This is a team of people who are all used to being the most important person in the room, which is exactly why it works and exactly why it keeps nearly falling apart.

You have never had the luxury of being thanked for this. The X-Men protect a world that has never once been comfortable with them existing, and something in you understands exactly why they keep doing it anyway.

You do not want colleagues, you want your people. The Titans are the team where the friendship came first and the world-saving turned up afterwards as an inconvenient side effect.

You are not asking who to trust. You worked out the answer a while ago and it was nobody in a cape. This is where you go once you have decided the people in charge need watching, and that nobody else is going to do it.

The Season 4 finale — "Don't Leave Me Hanging Here" — emphasizes the fact that not only does Invincible thrive in action-based conflict, but in narrative-based conflict, as well. After the terrible fight with the Viltrumites and being gone from Earth for so long, Mark deals with the emotional fallout of it all and is given a horrible choice.

Mark doesn't just discover that Atom Eve was pregnant and had to make a difficult choice all on her own (causing him to feel extremely guilty), but he finds out that the Viltrumites have secretly invaded Earth, and Grant Regent Thragg (Lee Pace) comes to him with a choice: fight him now and let Earth be destroyed and conquered, or allow his people to live and breed on Earth, doing nothing about it and letting the planet live another day. It's a terrible position to be in and, while he resists at first, Mark accepts. After a whole episode of what seems like PTSD causing him to think he's seeing Thragg, the eventual reveal of the real deal is executed masterfully.

Everyone loves a good battle-oriented episode, and one of the best comes in the form of Invincible Season 4, Episode 7, as Invincible, Oliver Grayson (Christian Convery), Nolan, Tech Jacket (Zoey Deutch), Battle Beast, Space Racer (Winston Duke), and the Coalition of Planets raid Viltrum to face the Viltrumites head-on. While the fight seems like it'll go alright at the beginning, nothing goes the way they hoped it would.

This fight with the Viltrumites is brutal and has gigantic repercussions for the rest of the series and the characters present in the fight. Not only is Thaedus (Peter Cullen), leader of the Coalition, murdered, but Oliver is gravely injured, seemingly dead until revealed to have a chance at recovery. This episode also ends in the destruction of the entire planet of Viltrum, ripping almost everything Thragg loved away from him.

A good pilot can define whether a series will find success or not, and the pilot of Invincible, "It's About Time," is an amazing one. While it may start out seeming like the standard superhero series with a bit of brutal The Boys flair, the big twist at the end of the episode solidifies this as a show that will be subverting expectations and, unlike what people believed, it would be in the majority of the first entry in this series.

After Mark has his big "first fly" as Invincible, in his new suit, set to an awesome soundtrack, audiences watch as his father, the ever-beloved Omni-Man, murders the Guardians of the Globe in cold blood. Why does he do this? No one is sure, and that leads to a mystery and season-long conflict that has viewers invested from start to finish.

Animated shows are so wonderful. Being serialized projects doesn't make them only for kids, though, like typical Saturday morning cartoons are (however, some of these are). If animated television has shown the world anything, it's that animation can be for everyone, kids and adults — sometimes even adults only. This series brings the iconic characters into a much younger age, making them high schoolers, rather than teachers at Xavier's School for Gifted Youngsters. Making the main team so much younger than they are typically seen brings the opportunity for all-new kinds of interpersonal drama that can't be found outside of it. It makes one wonder why they aren't depicted as teenagers more often (it also kills the controversial love triangle that is commonly between Wolverine, Jean Grey, and Cyclops—thank goodness).

The way this series adapts the entire Green Lantern Corps is almost perfect. It manages to have some mature themes and stories while still being wholly accessible for younger audiences. It feels like a space opera in its scale, but still juggles personal relationships and character arcs as if it were a grounded street-level show somehow.

For good reason, too, as X-Men: The Animated Series has some of the best superhero storytelling on television.

This five-year-long show has some of the deepest, most meaningful storytelling in animated superhero television. The X-Men have always had this over most Marvel Comics properties. The show accurately and truthfully depicts themes and messages about discrimination and bigotry while still being an action-packed, kid-friendly romp.

The premise of a young boy in a mostly superpowered society who has no abilities getting the greatest power on the planet from the literal Symbol of Peace is already engrossing. Pecan Cream Pie With Brown Sugar Custard

📅 Oct 21, 2025  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Inspired by the towering Praline Pecan Pie served at Das Dutchman Essenhaus in Middlebury, Indiana, this pecan cream pie brings together the best parts of pecan pie and cream pie. A gooey, nut-packed base sits beneath silky brown sugar custard and a pillow of whipped cream, with crunchy candied pecans scattered over the top. It’s a lot of pie—in the best possible way.

The pecan layer is jammed full of butter-toasted nuts, but the secret ingredient is a dollop of sour cream, which helps prevent crystallization and keeps the filling from becoming overly sweet. More sour cream goes into the whipped topping, adding tang and helping it hold its shape. Dark brown sugar and plenty of vanilla give the custard its toffeelike flavor.

This is admittedly more involved than a classic pecan pie, but the work is straightforward and easy to spread over a couple of days, making this a particularly good project to tackle before Thanksgiving Day. Toast the pecans once for both the gooey filling and crunchy topping, and make the crust and pecan layer ahead so all that’s left is the custard, whipped cream, and a little patience while everything chills.

Use a standard 9" metal pie pan. Metal promotes even browning and can accommodate more filling than a standard glass or ceramic dish of the same diameter. This is a generously filled pie, so pan capacity matters.

Can I make pecan cream pie ahead of time?

Yes. You can bake the crust and pecan layer up to one day ahead and store them, covered, at room temperature. Once you’ve added the custard, the pie needs at least four hours in the refrigerator to set. The fully assembled pie can also be refrigerated for up to one day. For the crunchiest topping, scatter the candied pecans over the whipped cream just before serving.

How do I know when the brown sugar custard is thick enough?

The custard should start to bubble, then become glossy and thick like pudding as you stir it over medium heat. This generally takes 3–4 minutes. Don’t rely on refrigeration alone to thicken it: The cornstarch needs to cook sufficiently on the stovetop for the custard to set properly. Keep stirring to prevent lumps or curdling.

Whisk 1⅔ cups (208 g) all-purpose flour, ¼ tsp. baking powder, and 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a large bowl. Add 2 oz. chilled cream cheese, cut into 1" pieces; rub into dry ingredients with your fingers until combined (mixture will be very shaggy). Add 10 Tbsp. chilled unsalted butter, sliced ½" thick, and toss to coat, then smash into smaller, flatter pieces (about chickpea size). Make a well in the center; pour in ¼ cup ice water. Toss with a fork or your hands to distribute, then knead dough in bowl a couple of times to bring together (some dry bits may remain).

Turn dough out onto a lightly dusted surface; dust top of dough with rice flour or all-purpose flour. Roll out to a ½"-thick rectangle. Fold in half, rotate 90°, and fold in half again. Roll out again to a ½"-thick rectangle and repeat folding process, dusting with more flour as needed. Pat into a ½"-thick disk; wrap in plastic. Chill at least 2 hours and, preferably, up to 12 hours.

Place racks in upper and lower thirds of oven; preheat to 425°. Let dough sit at room temperature 5 minutes. Unwrap and roll out on a lightly floured surface to a ¼"–⅛"-thick round. Wrap around rolling pin; transfer to a standard 9"-diameter pie dish (preferably metal). Unfurl into dish, then lift edges and allow dough to slump down into dish. Trim overhang to 1". Fold overhang under and crimp edges as desired. Prick bottom and sides of dough in several places with a fork. Freeze 15 minutes.

Crumple up 2 sheets of parchment paper; smooth out and place over dough. Fill dish with pie weights or dried beans. Set pan on a foil-lined baking sheet and bake crust on lower rack, tenting edges with foil if browning too quickly, until edges are golden brown and bottom is opaque, 35–40 minutes. Remove from oven; reduce oven temperature to 350°. Lift parchment and weights out of pan.

While the crust is baking, whisk 2 large eggs, ⅔ cup (packed; 133 g) dark brown sugar, and remaining 1 tsp. Diamond Crystal or ½ tsp. Morton kosher salt in a medium bowl. Set egg mixture aside to use for the candied pecan topping and the pie filling.

Melt remaining 6 Tbsp. unsalted butter in a medium saucepan set over medium. Add 2½ cups (300 g) raw pecans and cook, stirring gently, until nuts are slightly darkened in color and fragrant, 5–7 minutes. Pour through a fine-mesh sieve set over a small bowl, reserving both the pecans and the browned butter. (You’ll use the same pan to make the custard; no need to wash it.)

Transfer ½ cup toasted pecans to another small bowl to make the topping: Add 1 Tbsp. reserved egg mixture and toss to coat. Spread on a parchment-lined baking sheet and bake on upper rack until dry to the touch and a honeycomb-like texture appears on surface, about 5 minutes. Let candied nuts cool.

For the filling: Finely chop remaining toasted pecans and add to the remaining egg mixture along with the reserved browned butter, 2 Tbsp. sour cream, and 1 tsp. vanilla extract. Stir to combine. Scrape into crust and bake on lower rack until pecan layer is mostly set but still jiggles slightly in the center when gently shaken, 15–18 minutes. Transfer pan to a wire rack; let cool until barely warm, at least 30 minutes.

Do Ahead: Candied pecan topping can be made 3 days ahead; store airtight at room temperature. Crust with pecan layer can be baked 1 day ahead; store, covered with foil, at room temperature.

Heat 1½ cups heavy cream and 1 cup whole milk in the same saucepan you used for the nuts over medium until steaming, about 2 minutes.

Meanwhile, whisk 6 large egg yolks, ¾ cup (packed; 150 g) dark brown sugar, ⅓ cup (40 g) cornstarch, ½ tsp. Diamond Crystal or ¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt, and remaining ½ cup whole milk in a medium bowl.

Gradually pour warm cream mixture into egg mixture, whisking constantly until combined. Pour mixture back into pan and cook over medium heat, stirring constantly, until it starts to bubble, then becomes glossy and thick (like pudding), 3–4 minutes. Scrape into a medium bowl, add 6 Tbsp. chilled unsalted butter, cut into pieces, and 4 tsp. vanilla extract, and whisk until butter is melted and custard is emulsified. Cover with plastic wrap, pressing directly onto surface. Let custard stand at room temperature while the pecan layer cools. The pecan layer can still be slightly warm when you add the custard; neither needs to cool completely.

Uncover custard; whisk until smooth. Spoon over gooey pecan layer, spreading evenly to edges. Cover and chill at least 4 hours and up to 1 day.

Whisk ¼ cup sour cream, 3 Tbsp. powdered sugar, remaining 1 tsp. vanilla extract, a pinch of kosher salt, and remaining 1 cup heavy cream in a medium bowl to medium peaks. Spoon over custard and spread into an even layer. Break candied pecans into large pieces and scatter on top.

Do Ahead: Pie can be assembled 1 day ahead. Chill.
