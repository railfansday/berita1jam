A particularly lucrative commercial agreement since, according to the Italian press, the naming contract should bring in around 650 million euros over a period of twenty years, or an average of 32.5 million euros per season. La Gazzetta Dello Sport. A considerable financial windfall for the two Lombard giants, who are seeking to develop their revenues in order to compete with the greatest European powers. Generali will also become the official partner of the two clubs in the insurance sector.

Scheduled for the 2031-2032 season, the inauguration of this new enclosure will mark an important stage in the history of Milanese football. The future stadium will present an original architecture, with a hexagonal exterior structure and stands arranged in a bowl, raised on a podium. The Generali name will be widely visible on the facade of the building. However, a particularity is planned for European competitions: in accordance with UEFA rules concerning the commercial rights of speakers, the Generali Stadium will take the name ” Milan Stadium » during Champions League matches and other international competitions. An operation already applied to several large European stadiums, notably those of Juventus and Arsenal. With this partnership, Milan and Inter join the list of Italian clubs having sold the naming rights to their venue, like the Allianz Stadium in Turin or Atalanta’s New Balance Arena.

In ‘Elsinore,’ Billie Piper Found “Beautiful Chaos” With Andrew Scott — And a New Career Chapter

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The decorated stage and screen star is a scene-stealer in the weepy Oscar contender, leading to the most attention she's received for a film role to date. The experience is inspiring her to forge a new path.

At the SCAD Savannah Film Festival, just ahead of Halloween weekend, Billie Piper held a masterclass for college students. “It was basically me just rattling on about being an actor — I hope some of it was useful, not sure,” she says the next day from a quiet library in the Southern city’s downtown. “Their line of questioning was so insightful and kind of ahead of their years. They were all there with the little moleskin books taking notes. I just didn’t expect that.”

She’d have little reason to. Despite her decades in the business, despite the Olivier Award win and five BAFTA nominations, the English native hasn’t really traveled the film-festival circuit before. She came to town as an honoree for the Oscar contender Elsinore, receiving the Marquee Award for her vivacious turn as the imperious real-life casting director Patsy Pollock. Related Stories Movies Golden Globes: 'Club Kid' Submitted as Drama (Exclusive) Movies Santa Barbara Film Fest: Inde Navarrette, Jaafar Jackson Among Eight Tapped for Virtuoso Awards

In spending time with the kids before the festivities kicked off, it dawned on her: “I actually thought: ‘God, I’ve done some really great jobs.’”

She arrives today with dark brown hair, a far cry from the striking blonde of Elsinore and her other most noted screen roles, and a tablet that she occasionally flashes open to reference movies she’s either seen recently or intends to soon watch. “It’s really fired me up about independent movies again,” she says of running around in support of Elsinore. (The film launched at Telluride, where star Andrew Scott emerged as an instant best-actor front-runner, and has since played all over the world.) “I felt like it was all dead, but it wasn’t dead. I just feel so jacked about all the movies I’m seeing.”

An intensely chameleonic actress unafraid to go big, Piper has never had the largest footprint in the feature space. “Often the films that I read weren’t that good for women,” she says. We may be at something of a turning point. Already, she’s received her first career British Independent Film Award nomination for Elsinore, competing in a supporting category alongside returning faces like Claire Foy and Tom Courtenay.

After breaking out as a teen pop star, the now-44-year-old has always followed the best work. She made her name onscreen by leading series like Doctor Who and Secret Diary of a Callgirl, then as a major stage actor in West End productions of The Effect and Yerma. For the latter revival of Federico García Lorca’s classic, she remains the only actor to have won every official Best Actress prize currently awarded for a single UK theater performance. She wowed again in HBO Max’s I Hate Suzie, a brilliant panic attack of a dark comedy about a moderately famous actress thrown into turmoil when nude photos on her phone leak online.

For one reason or another, these projects didn’t land with as much force in the US as back home. Indeed, Piper co-created I Hate Suzie with her Effect playwright Lucy Prebble, and the show aired right after other stellar series from British female multihyphenates, like Phoebe Waller-Bridge’s Fleabag and Michaela Coel’s I May Destroy You. Rest assured, Piper’s BAFTA-nominated performance is every bit as ferociously compelling — and revealing — as what her peers delivered in those Emmy-winning smashes.

For a good chunk of American audiences, at least, Suzie remains a hidden streaming gem waiting to be discovered. Piper seems comfortable in that zone.

Elsinore dramatizes the heartbreaking, inspiring final act of Ian Charleson, a gay actor not out publicly who was known for his starring role in Chariots of Fire. Charleson decided, shortly after being diagnosed with AIDS, to play Hamlet at the National Theatre while fighting for his life — and somehow pulled it off. Scott’s turn as the late great dominates the movie, but Piper becomes its sneaky heart. Patsy, Charleson’s best friend, emerges as his caretaker and as an anchor in their defiant chosen family.

“Their relationship was beautiful chaos,” Piper says. “They partied hard. He was a big figure in her daughter’s life. She was a single mother working in a male-dominated industry.” When it came to Charleson’s controversial choice to play Hamlet — his doctor, portrayed by Olivia Colman, was initially firmly against it — Patsy simply got it. So did Piper. “It makes complete sense to want to play one of, if not the most challenging parts at your most vulnerable point in your life as you approach your death,” Piper says. “Patsy understood that about him. I understand that about what he chose to do as well. I think a lot of actors get it, to be honest.”

Elsinore is a tearjerker, no doubt, but it’s stuffed with joy. Piper brings plenty of levity with her glamorous, haughty embodiment of an old showbusiness powerhouse. She is clearly having a ball. “I really miss people like Patsy in our industry — the larger-than-life characters — because everything’s so serious and there’s no money now,” Piper says. “It was fun to pull from the characters I knew in my early career. There were agents I had who were cut from a different cloth. It wasn’t all terribly kind, but it was unlike anybody I’d grown up with — the flamboyance, the drama, the quick shifts in tone and mood.”

Her chemistry with Scott is undeniable. The two veterans of the London theater scene previously worked together on Lena Dunham’s critically acclaimed Catherine Called Birdy, including a birthing scene for the ages. Like Piper, Scott is plenty decorated back home, but his frequent co-star finds him unusually playful to work with.

“With that skillset, you expect someone who’s a bit of a dick, but he’s not remotely a dick — he is warm, he is generous, he is informal. The work feels casual,” Piper says of Scott. “He takes it seriously, of course — he knows what he wants and knows what he likes — but it’s not the only thing that’s happening in his life, which I think is healthy.”

Piper can relate. She’s a mother of three and values the separation of the personal and professional. “I do my work, then I go home and am a parent — and so there’s no real processing of what I’ve done in the day, which is often really thrilling and rare and something I’d wanted to do since I was a child,” she says. Take, for instance, the job she’s just come back from: Wednesday season three. Piper grew up on Tim Burton and was now surrounded by his and his team’s singular designs. She got to act opposite the likes of Winona Ryder. And she was gone for nine months, a very long time. Too long, maybe.

“I haven’t worked on a nine-month shoot since Doctor Who, and I left that show for a reason,” she says. “The gratitude is there, but also it’s not without a cost.” Now, of course, she’s even further away, in the American south: “I’m doing a lot of being away. I feel terrible and also excited.”

Some viewers read into the whirring, control-obsessed anxiety of Piper’s I Hate Suzie tour-de-force as a thinly veiled commentary on her own struggles as an artist in the public eye. (Suzie’s career beginnings mirrored Piper’s to some degree.) The show was fiction, of course, but Piper’s guttural performance came from somewhere true.

“It’s really fun to play,” Piper says now, “but it’s just no way to live your life.”

The lesson took some learning. “I need things to be beyond good for me to take it seriously, to take myself seriously — it’s really got nothing to do with anyone else, but with insane levels of achievement,” Piper says. “It’s dysfunctional. I’ve been like that since I was a kid.” She believed the work should feel painful, that it should take everything out of her. She credits Yerma, which she performed in London and New York from 2016-2018 under the direction of Simon Stone — who so happens to be the director of Elsinore — with ushering in a new way forward in that regard.

“Simon completely changed my career for me — completely changed the way I looked at acting,” Piper says. “That’s a nice thing to have in a career that has been going on for a long time, to suddenly work with someone that completely rips up the rule book.”

Brick by brick, Piper is building a thrilling new chapter on that experience. Elsinore has proven a cozy fit. Not just as an awards-bound prestige movie offering her a meaty supporting role — also in the way it’s welcoming her into spaces she’s never even thought much of before. Her main goal this awards season? To hang out with Club Kid writer-director-star Jordan Firstman. “I’ve watched Rotting in the Sun about five times. It’s so funny and so free,” she says, referring to Sebastián Silva’s graphic meta comedy that starred Firstman as a vapid version of himself. “Now I just want to be his best mate.”

She’s written a film that she describes as “romantic horror,” which she’s planning to direct. (Her feature debut, Rare Beasts, premiered at Venice in 2019.) She knows it will be difficult to get off the ground. But she’s been feeling inspired by what she’s seeing. “It’s really useful going into a process of trying to get a film made that may never happen, may never see the light of day — it’s energized my passion,” she says.

Same goes for the students with whom she spent the previous afternoon.

“Even though we live in this difficult day and age, even though it might feel more compromised and frightening in terms of a career choice, they still want to pursue this,” Piper says. “There is still this appetite for the arts. I find it quite moving.”

Elsinore opens in limited North American theatrical release on November 20.

Ethiopian Authorities Suspend Independent Newspaper

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A man reads the Ethiopian Reporter in Addis Ababa on December 11, 2019. © 2019 Eduardo Soteras/AFP via Getty Images

In what has become the new normal, the Ethiopian government has silenced yet another independent media outlet.

The Ethiopian Media Authority announced in a November 2 Facebook post that it is indefinitely suspending the print and online license of the Reporter, one of Ethiopia’s oldest media institutions. Publishing in English and Amharic, the Reporter is also the country’s last privately owned Amharic newspaper.

Stating that the suspension would take effect immediately, the Media Authority accused the Reporter of violating the country’s laws and media ethics by repeatedly publishing content that was against “national interests.” The authority added that the outlet published “distorted content that negatively affects foreign investment, tourism, and diplomacy.”

The action is part of the government’s ongoing assault on the media and civic space in the country and points toward increasing use of vague claims about “national interests” to shut down any critical reporting.

Since 2025, Ethiopian authorities have banned numerous media outlets, deported foreign correspondents, and targeted local journalists through arbitrary arrests, enforced disappearances, and office raids. In October, undercover police detained journalists Samuel Getachew and Amanuel Gebremedhin, charging them with reporting news that would cause disruptions in Addis Ababa, the capital. The two were jailed for eight days.

The Reporter, one of the few remaining independent outlets in the country, hasn’t been spared. Authorities have arrested its journalists, while the outlet itself has been under government pressure. Days before the suspension, the Reporter published an editorial warning that Ethiopia’s privately owned press was “navigating one of its most precarious periods in history” and calling for immediate reforms to prevent its total collapse.

The Ethiopian government should heed the warning; the country’s concerned partners should urge the government to immediately reverse course.

JetBlue is raising checked bag fees again — to as much as $70 for a first bag

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The airline cited higher fuel and operating costs; the new fees apply to tickets booked on or after Nov. 3

JetBlue Airways raised its checked bag fees on Tuesday, with a first bag costing as much as $70 on peak travel dates when the fee is paid less than a day before the flight leaves, according to CNBC.

For a non-peak period, a first bag in economy costs $50 if paid for more than a day ahead of the flight and $60 if paid inside that 24-hour window, according to CNBC. During peak periods such as the holidays and summer, the fee is $55 in advance and $70 within 24 hours, up from $49 for the advance fee.

The new rates apply to any ticket purchased starting Nov. 3, according to Thrifty Traveler. Customers who booked earlier pay the old rates. A second bag costs $70 off-peak and $75 at peak when paid in advance, and a third bag costs $200 regardless of travel dates.

The first bag fee climbed from $60 to $80 each way for travelers buying the lowest-priced basic economy ticket on a transatlantic trip to Europe, according to Thrifty Traveler.

Customers who carry JetBlue's co-branded credit card or have elite frequent flyer status are exempt from the fees, according to CNBC. JetBlue Mint and BlueFirst passengers also receive a minimum of one complimentary checked bag, according to Thrifty Traveler.

"We're adjusting checked bag fees to help offset rising fuel and operating costs," JetBlue said in a statement, according to CNBC. "This helps us keep our base fares competitive while giving customers the choice to pay for a checked bag only if they need one."

The cost of jet fuel has approximately doubled this year, ever since the war in Iran began in February. Jet fuel was priced at $4.44 per gallon in the U.S. Gulf of Mexico market on Tuesday.

JetBlue was the first major U.S. airline to increase baggage charges following the outbreak of the war in late March, according to Thrifty Traveler. Every U.S. carrier matched that increase within weeks.

ESPN's Michael Wilbon Announces Indefinite Leave Amid Health Issues

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Pardon the Interruption cohost Michael Wilbon announced that he will be taking a leave of absence from ESPN and ABC amid multiple ongoing health issues: "This isn't retirement."

The longtime ESPN commentator and cohost of Pardon the Interruption with Tony Kornheiser announced he will be taking an indefinite leave of absence from the network and ABC amid multiple health struggles.

“This isn't retirement, but it is a trip to the injured list. Hopefully not an extended one,” Wilbon posted on X Nov. 2. “Struggles with multiple health issues in recent months lead me to take an indefinite leave of absence from my PTI and NBA duties at ESPN and ABC.”

“Probably, I should have listened to medical professionals and people closest to me,” he admitted, “yep Kornheiser included, who saw worrisome signs in recent years that suggested I take time away earlier. But I didn’t.”

While he didn’t go into details about the struggles, the 67-year-old did assure his fans that it was not related to alcohol or gambling.

He also expressed his gratitude to his colleagues for encouraging him to take time away “to concentrate on me” and keep his focus on his wellbeing.

“I have no idea exactly how this part of my journey will wind up,” Wilbon wrote. “The goal is to come out of it feeling much better than I do now and ready to return to sparring with Tony.”

Wilbon—who began hosting Pardon the Interruption in 2001—shed more insight on his health on the Nov. 2 episode of Kornheiser’s podcast The Tony Kornheiser Show, sharing that he was diagnosed with diabetes in 2008 and the condition is “part of what I have to deal with right now,” although it’s not the only issue.

Amazon rewrites its software engineering playbook for an era when AI writes the code

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Todd Bishop on Nov 3, 2026 at 12:26 pmNovember 3, 2026 at 12:36 pm

Last month, a small team inside Amazon Web Services called Velocity Labs went from concept to the public release of a new open-source AI model in less than a week.

The group, whose existence hasn’t been previously reported, was formed in April. Velocity Labs has about 35 people, most of them in Seattle. The idea is to think beyond Amazon’s normal product-oriented approach, building quick prototypes in areas of AI that the company sees as underexplored and increasingly important to customers.

“We have noticed how the open-source community, and some of our competitors, experiment with new ideas, and deliver initial products fast,” the company says in job listings. “With Velocity Labs we are building an organization that can move faster, explore more, and make sure AWS is at the cutting edge of innovation in agentic AI.”

Inside the group, scientists and engineers work together, and they’re encouraged to cross over: engineers engage in research, and scientists do engineering work.

Velocity Labs is an example of the ways Amazon is trying “to create organizations that we think are going to be better suited to working with this new set of tools,” said Marc Brooker, an AWS vice president and distinguished engineer who leads the team.

For the vast majority of Amazon’s developers, he said, AI agents now write almost all the code that would have previously been written by hand.

Redefining the role: The team is part of Amazon’s larger rethinking of software development for the AI era, laid out in revised guidelines that the company’s engineering leaders sent to its developers earlier this year.

A letter that accompanied the guidelines, published last week under the byline of Michael “Terk” Terkowitz, a VP and distinguished engineer in Amazon’s stores business, advises engineers to learn AI now and to stay close to their customers and products.

The letter acknowledges that as Amazon has grown, some engineers and managers have become “less connected to their customers and business.”

Amazon has never published its underlying role guidelines for software engineers, and it declined to provide a copy of the revised version for this article. Brooker, one of the authors of the accompanying letter, said in an interview that the update emphasizes measuring engineers and teams by outcomes rather than inputs.

“We had seen these anti-patterns in the industry of folks really focusing on how many tokens are you spending, how many lines of code are you committing,” Brooker said, explaining that those kinds of measures don’t equate to customer value.

Asked whether AI use is part of performance reviews or promotions, Brooker said, “Not directly in a metrics sense.” It could factor in, he said, to the extent that it makes someone a stronger performer.

The letter doesn’t dismiss usage metrics entirely. Amazon has found that they “correlate well with the outputs that matter” and expects that to continue “for some time,” it says.

But what’s important, the letter says, is to use them “with care and judgement.”

Amazon engineers have described pressure to use AI tools. Three developers told The New York Times last year that managers were pushing them to use AI, raising output goals and becoming less forgiving about deadlines, with some coders saying their jobs had begun to resemble warehouse work. Amazon has set a goal for more than 80% of its developers to use AI each week, the Financial Times reported.

In May, Amazon shut down an internal leaderboard called KiroRank — which ranked employees by how much they used Kiro, the company’s AI coding tool — after finding that some were using the tool excessively just to climb the rankings, the FT reported.

Asked whether that episode was part of the impetus for the revised guidelines, Brooker said he didn’t recall the leaderboard or the details surrounding it.

“If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing. Document why, share it with your community of peers, bring it to your manager, and work to find solutions.”

Letter accompanying the revised SDE role guidelines

How the work has changed: Two years ago, most Amazon developers were still writing their code by hand, Brooker said. Today, “for the vast majority of our developer population, almost all of their implementation code is agent generated rather than human written,” he said.

“That is a massive change in the way of working for software engineers,” he said.

Brooker said he used to spend much of his time in a traditional code editor or the command line, and now spends a lot more of it working with agent tools such as Kiro, Kiro Crew and Anthropic’s Claude Code, calling it “a complete change of tools.”

For the company, the transition hasn’t been seamless. In March, Amazon acknowledged that an engineer who followed inaccurate advice from an AI agent contributed to one of several outages on its retail site, though the company disputed a Financial Times report that junior and mid-level engineers would need senior engineers to sign off on AI-assisted changes.

Amazon also pushed back on an earlier FT report that a 13-hour disruption to an AWS service in December happened after engineers let its Kiro AI coding tool make changes, and the tool decided to “delete and recreate the environment.” Amazon blamed user error — a misconfigured role — and said it has since added safeguards, including mandatory peer review for production access.

Still, the letter acknowledges that today’s AI tools won’t help in every situation, and it says that’s OK as long as engineers can explain why.

“If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing,” the letter says. “An engineer who can explain where AI does not yet help their work is demonstrating the judgement we are asking for.”

Inside Velocity Labs: The group is part of the agentic AI division led by Swami Sivasubramanian, which has organized much of its work around small teams of six to eight people, as GeekWire reported in June. Velocity Labs also includes the developers behind Strands, AWS’s open-source toolkit for building AI agents.

The model that Velocity Labs released last month, Strands Decider 2B, is designed to make quick, narrow decisions inside AI agents, such as which tool to use or where to route a request. It’s based on Qwen, an open model from Alibaba, and was released Oct. 1 along with the recipe used to train it.

Brooker built the original version on his own GPU at home after the startup TypeSafe AI unveiled Jev, a similar decision model, on Sept. 15, he wrote on his blog. “Every line of code was written by an agent,” he wrote, though he tried to make sure “the core ideas and insights were mine, or at least I understood them.”

Brooker described the team’s turnaround as less than a week from concept to public release. OpenAI announced a similar hosted service, its Decisions API, the same week, TechCrunch reported.

Brooker credited the team’s speed to its small size and a high level of trust: each person takes ownership of a piece of a project and asks for help when needed. He expects Amazon to create more teams like it, though not across the whole company.

Impact on jobs: Amazon has cut roughly 30,000 corporate jobs since late 2025. CEO Andy Jassy has said he expects AI to shrink the company’s corporate workforce over time.

At the same time, AWS CEO Matt Garman said in April that Amazon is “hiring just as many software developers as we ever had,” Business Insider reported. An Amazon spokesperson told the publication that more than 11,000 interns and early-career software engineers would join the company globally this year.

Asked whether AI will mean fewer engineers at Amazon, Brooker said, “I don’t think it changes the number of engineers.” The company wants engineers who take ownership, are curious and can learn quickly outside their areas of expertise, he said.

AI isn’t the right answer for every problem, Brooker said.

If the right tool is “an AI agent powered by a cutting-edge model, fantastic,” he said. However, he added, if it’s “three lines of Python code, that’s great too.”

È un po' speciale, però ma la mia storia è con il Como adesso. Vogliamo vincere questa partita e siamo pronti per questo", le parole rilasciate in conferenza stampa alla vigilia.

Butez ritroverà uno stadio, il Bollaert-Delelis, che conosce bene dopo il passato trascorso nel Lille: "Ovviamente è uno stadio caldissimo. Come giocatore mi piacciono tantissimo queste partite. Sono felice di essere qua e giocare queste gare. Voglio essere in campo per provare questa serata". Poi ha aggiunto: "È vero che sono nato non lontano da qui e ho fatto tutta la mia trafila nel Lille, quindi c'è questo legame forte per me che sono del Nord. Tuttavia, come dicevo poco fa, oggi sono un giocatore del Como, e questa rivalità, o in ogni caso questo legame con il Lille, non esiste domani sera. Direi che è una cosa da mettere soprattutto da parte per serate come questa", ha dichiarato in conferenza stampa alla vigilia.

Se Jean Butez conosce già il Bollaert-Delelis, tuttavia domani sarà la prima volta che ci giocherà in Champions League. E l'atmosfera è rimasta bene impressa: "Ho potuto vivere belle serate da spettatore, in particolare qui, ed è vero che c'è un'atmosfera molto intensa. Da giocatore hai un'unica voglia, ovvero vivere momenti del genere direttamente sul campo".

Questa sfida sarà anche l'occasione per incrociare Robin Risser. I due portieri condividono un legame recente perché Butez era stato convocato in nazionale francese dopo il forfait del portiere del Lens. I due, tuttavia, non si conoscono personalmente: "Non ho mai incontrato Risser, quindi non sarà un ritrovo, ma in ogni caso c'è questo legame con la nazionale francese. È qualcosa in più da lasciare fuori dal campo per una sera, perché se ci si focalizza su questo tipo di cose, credo che si finisca per dimenticare ciò che bisogna fare in qualità di portiere e di giocatore. Ne parleremo sicuramente dopo la partita".

Il portiere del Como poi ha speso parole di grande stima per Risser: "È un portiere che possiede enormemente qualità, che ha un bel fisico ed è un estremo difensore piuttosto moderno. Penso che sia una fortuna per il club del Lens avere un portiere come lui. Immagino un bel futuro per lui anche in nazionale francese".
