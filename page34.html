Steam players will be able to vote for the best game of the past decade as Valve pits previous Game of the Year winners against each other in a new award category.

To celebrate the 10th anniversary of the Steam Awards, Valve has introduced a special Hall of Fame gong for 2026, with the likes of Elden Ring, Baldur’s Gate 3, and GTA 5 all competing against each other.

Valve describes the new category as “a collection of previous Game of the Year award winners now in competition against each other for the title of #1 game of the past 10 years.”

It’s worth noting that both GTA 5 and The Witcher 3 were originally released in 2013 and 2015, respectively, and these appear to be enhanced and/or remastered versions released later.

Concept art for Tate Liverpool redevelopment

Tate Liverpool will reopen next autumn following an extensive four-year redevelopment costing around £30m. The relaunch programme includes a major show dedicated to Surrealism along with the first major retrospective for the Liverpool-based artist Chila Kumari Singh Burman. The key regional UK gallery, located on the city’s Royal Albert Dock, closed in October 2023 and was initially due to reopen in 2025.

Chila Kumari Singh Burman in front of her Winter Commission covering the façade of Tate Britain in London in 2020

Surrealism: Staging Dream Worlds, which will run until spring 2028, will include work by Salvador Dalí, Eileen Agar and Man Ray, among others. “The show will reveal the important role of Surrealism in the history of theatre and dance, from experiments with masquerade, character and role play to major commissions for stage sets and costumes,” says a Tate statement. Works featured include Claude Cahun's photograph of Sheila Legge’s 1936 performance Surrealist Phantom of Sex Appeal, when the artist wore a Dalí-inspired costume in London's Trafalgar Square.

Claude Cahun, Sheila Legge as Surrealist ‘Phantom’, Trafalgar Square (1936)

For the overhaul, Tate Liverpool received £18.6m from the Public Bodies Infrastructure Fund, which is awarded by the UK Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport. It also received philanthropic donations from the Garfield Weston Foundation (£3m), the Wolfson Foundation (£1.25m) and the Ross Warburton Charitable Trust, alongside other trusts and individuals.

The UK Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government also awarded £10m to the gallery, which it secured through a combined £20m bid with National Museums Liverpool. “We have raised more [than £30m] because there are other costs associated with reinstalling and reopening the museum,” a Tate spokesperson tells The Art Newspaper.

According to a Tate statement, the designs for the new gallery, drawn up by 6a architects, “open up spaces to display the incredible variety of Tate’s collection and host world-class exhibitions. These galleries will sit alongside new public spaces for play, relaxation and learning with views across the Mersey, helping to connect the gallery with the city and the communities that surround it.” The New York and Berlin-based artist Asad Raza will create the first major commission for the gallery’s Art Hall.

Tate Liverpool, which opened in 1988, was designed by James Stirling who converted the northwest corner of a seven-story warehouse standing on the Royal Albert Dock into the five-storey gallery. It received 405,000 visitors in 2022, its last full year, and 660,000 in 2019, before the Covid-19 pandemic.

Helen Legg stepped down as Tate Liverpool director earlier this year, with Jemima Pyne taking over in the interim; a new director will be recruited after the new director of Tate, Jessica Morgan, is in post next year, the spokesperson confirmed. Tate comprises four galleries: Tate Modern, Tate Britain, Tate Liverpool and Tate St Ives.

Germany Agrees to Hand Over Photo Archive Chronicling a Family’s Persecution by Nazis

Germany Agrees to Hand Over Photo Archive Chronicling a Family’s Persecution by Nazis

The German government has agreed to hand over an extensive photo archive to the Jehovah’s Witnesses that documents the persecution of a pacifist Christian family under the Nazi regime.

Jehovah’s Witnesses were persecuted by the Nazis for their religious beliefs, particularly their refusal to serve in the military, pledge allegiance to Adolf Hitler, or participate in Nazi organizations. An estimated 4,500 Jehovah’s Witnesses were sent to concentration camps.

According to a report by The New York Times, among those persecuted were Franz and Hilda Kusserow and their 11 children, who lived in Bad Lippspringe, a town in the German state of North Rhine-Westphalia. Two of the couple’s sons were executed after refusing military service because of their religious beliefs. The three youngest children were taken from their family and placed in a Nazi training school, where they were prohibited from contacting their relatives. Both parents and their remaining children were sentenced to prison terms.

One of their daughters Annemarie Kusserow was arrested by the Gestapo in October 1944 and remained a prisoner until May 25, 1945, after the war in Europe had ended. Over the following decades, Annemarie gathered and preserved a collection of more than 1,000 photographs, documents, drawings, letters, and reports documenting her family’s experiences.

In her 2005 will, Annemarie bequeathed the entire photo archive to the German headquarters of the Jehovah’s Witnesses. However, her brother Hans Werner Kusserow, to whom she had loaned the collection for research purposes, sold it to the Bundeswehr Military History Museum in Dresden, Germany, in 2009. The archive has remained in the German museum’s possession ever since.

According to the Jehovah’s Witnesses’ official website, the sale of the photo collection to a military museum was “especially troubling” given that two of Annemarie’s brothers had lost their lives because of their pacifist beliefs. The organization says the sale of the archive “resulted in years of legal battles.”

The Jehovah’s Witnesses filed a lawsuit to recover the archive from the Bundeswehr Military History Museum, which is operated by the German armed forces. On November 19, the museum and the religious organization reached an “amicable agreement” under which the collection will be handed over to the Jehovah’s Witnesses. Both parties have agreed to keep the terms of the settlement confidential.

According to The New York Times, Sebastian Stock, a spokesman for the religious organization in Germany, says that once the transfer is complete, the “Jehovah’s Witnesses will develop and present a concept for making the archive accessible for further academic research.”

In addition to digitizing the archive and making it available to scholars, the Jehovah’s Witnesses plan to display some of the documents in a new exhibition examining the history of the religious group in Central Europe.

Fifth Circuit Dismisses Lawsuit Over Facebook Removal, Supposedly Connected to Stanford Internet Observatory, of COVID-Related Post

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From Hines v. Stamos, decided yesterday by Judges W. Eugene Davis, Leslie Southwick, and Stuart Kyle Duncan:

Jill Hines sued Stanford University, Alex Stamos, and others, alleging they conspired with the government to censor her online speech about COVID-19. The district court dismissed her suit without prejudice for lack of subject-matter and personal jurisdiction. Because Hines has not shown that her injury is fairly traceable to Stanford or Stamos, she lacks standing. We therefore AFFIRM without reaching personal jurisdiction….

This case is closely related to Murthy v. Missouri, which guides our resolution of this appeal. In Murthy, Hines (along with others) sued federal officials and agencies, alleging the government censored her online speech against COVID-19 restrictions in violation of the First Amendment. Here, Hines challenges Defendants' participation in an academic project named the Election Integrity Partnership (EIP). She alleges the EIP and its participants aided the government's scheme to have Facebook suppress her online speech, also in violation of the First Amendment. {Our review is limited to Hines's claims against Stanford and Alex Stamos, the former director of the Stanford Internet Observatory, which was an EIP member.}

Facebook began moderating COVID-19 content early in the pandemic, deleting or demoting posts it deemed false. In April 2020 alone, it labeled roughly 50 million pieces of content. That same month, Hines launched a grassroots campaign to challenge COVID-19 government restrictions and used Facebook to communicate with her supporters. She was aware of Facebook's moderation and took deliberate measures to circumvent it.

The EIP, a consortium of researchers and institutions, was formed in July 2020. It sought to identify misinformation about elections and voting. For about three months in 2020, it monitored elections-related content on social media. EIP participants sometimes recommended actions to social-media platforms and logged their communications when they did. In most instances, the platforms took no detectable action on those recommendations….

To have standing, a "plaintiff must have (1) suffered an injury in fact, (2) that is fairly traceable to the challenged conduct of the defendant, and (3) that is likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision." For the claims before us, the second inquiry—traceability of Hines's past injuries—is "the whole ball game." …

There is one injury that Hines claims is traceable to Defendants: on September 9, 2020, Facebook removed a post from Hines's page that attached an article entitled Masks Don't Work. At the time, Facebook explained the post went "against our Community Standards." It also reduced distribution of Hines's page on COVID-19 restrictions for "repeated" violations of its standards on "Inauthentic Behavior" and for "repeated sharing of false news." Hines does not dispute that these restrictions fit Facebook's own content moderation…

Hines's traceability theory rests on an inference drawn from timing. On September 8, 2020, the EIP received a massive submission of over 200 webpages and social-media posts from the Democratic National Committee. Among those 200 webpages was a single post by Hines where she encouraged her followers to lobby their representatives to end the COVID-19 public-health emergency. In Hines's view, the one-day timing between the DNC's submission and Facebook's moderation of her page is evidence that EIP participants called Facebook's attention to her single post in the 200-webpage DNC submission.

We are not persuaded by her argument. Discovery revealed no communication between the EIP and Facebook about Hines, and Facebook did not remove or moderate the post that the EIP received. {Hines argues there may be evidence of Defendants' communications with Facebook in undiscovered Slack or Zoom messages. But Stanford produced "communications and other documents" mentioning Hines or her account, and Hines neither challenged that production nor moved to compel additional discovery, although the scheduling order allowed for that. The mere possibility of undiscovered communications that were not sought in the district court is not evidence.}

A notation in the EIP's records even says Hines's COVID-19 posts were beyond the scope of the EIP's elections-oriented work. We conclude as a matter of law that timing alone is too attenuated to trace Hines's injury to Defendants.

Murthy also requires us to consider Facebook's own incentives to moderate content. Facebook gave its own reasons for acting when it removed Hines's post and demoted her page, and Hines does not dispute that that moderation aligned with Facebook's COVID-19 policies. We agree with the district court that there's no indication that Facebook, in moderating Hines's post, did anything other than follow its own moderation policy….

I am not at all involved with Stanford's defense of this lawsuit.

GEO visibility can hide gaps that keep buyers from shortlisting your brand, while Decision Coverage helps identify the evidence needed to earn recommendations.

Recent research is raising questions about the commercial value of AI visibility. It also exposes a problem with what we have chosen to measure.

Rand Fishkin recently asked whether AI visibility is becoming a marketing boondoggle. In a Whiteboard video shared on LinkedIn, he discussed several studies questioning the commercial returns from visibility in standalone AI assistants, while distinguishing those platforms from Google’s AI-powered search experiences.

I understand the skepticism. We have seen an extraordinary amount of enthusiasm for GEO measurement, accompanied by new tools, new metrics, and a growing expectation that brands should monitor how often they appear in AI-generated answers. Much of that work is useful. I want to know whether a company appears when prospective customers search for its category, how its capabilities are presented, and which competitors are recommended.

For some time, I have been concerned that we are investing heavily in tracking mentions, citations, and share of voice without giving enough attention to the decisions customers are asking AI systems to help them make. We seem to have accepted that being mentioned is inherently valuable, even when we cannot demonstrate that the mention helped a customer evaluate the company or move toward a purchase.

I’m also concerned about this given the executive attention SEO, GEO, and AEO are receiving. Search professionals have spent years trying to secure the resources and cross-functional cooperation needed to make meaningful changes. Now, in some organizations, we have senior leadership listening, budgets for tools and content, and an unusual willingness to bring marketing, product, sales, and technical teams together.

We risk squandering that opportunity if the principal outcome we report is more AI visibility. Executives are investing because they believe AI is changing how customers discover and choose vendors. We need to show that our work is addressing that change, not simply producing another set of exposure metrics.

The starting point is the customer’s motivation. People generally aren’t going to ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google’s AI-powered search experiences for another page of links. They want help understanding something, comparing alternatives, or reaching a decision. Traditional search left much of that work to the user, who had to visit multiple sites and reconcile the information. AI systems are attractive partly because they reduce that effort.

Yet we still talk about GEO as though the expected journey is a brand mention, followed by a click, followed by a conversion. That sequence may describe some purchases, but it is a poor model for many complex B2B decisions.

Fishkin’s argument drew on research from Marie Haynes, The SEO Works, and Kevin Indig. Although they approached the issue differently, their findings make it difficult to assume that increasing visibility in standalone AI assistants will necessarily produce meaningful traffic or conversions.

In GEO Is a Distraction, Haynes examined analytics data from 100 websites representing more than 145 million sessions. ChatGPT referrals accounted for approximately 0.62% of Google organic sessions, rising to 0.73% when other tracked language models were included. Even when ChatGPT visitors converted at higher rates, the difference in traffic volume meant that Google organic traffic remained far more important to measured conversions.

Her concern is partly one of priorities. Given the changes taking place within Google Search, how much effort should businesses devote to improving visibility in standalone assistants that currently contribute relatively little measurable traffic?

The SEO Works’ AI Referral Traffic Benchmark examined 95 websites and found that organic search generated 39 times as many sessions as AI assistants. For every 100 conversions attributed to organic search, AI referrals generated 3.2. The study also found considerable variation in conversion performance. Although AI referrals converted approximately 18% better overall, the rate for B2B product companies was only 0.62 times that of organic traffic.

That last finding caught my attention because B2B buying behavior is often discussed as though AI-assisted discovery will naturally produce exceptionally valuable visits. I agree it may, but only if the information is available, and I note that it might not be universally applicable.

Kevin Indig explored another possibility in Does AI Drive More Direct Traffic?. Perhaps AI influences buyers who subsequently visit a website directly or search for a brand, leaving the original influence unattributed. In an analysis of 12 analytics properties, he found that some apparent direct-traffic growth was due to bot activity. Additional testing across four brands and 632 tests did not establish a reliable relationship between AI visibility and subsequent direct or branded search activity.

Indig did not argue that AI has no influence on purchasing behavior. Rather, his work demonstrated how difficult it is to establish that influence using the signals available in conventional analytics.

I think these findings deserve more than a debate about whether GEO is worth the investment. They should prompt us to reconsider what we are asking visibility metrics to prove.

If a customer uses AI to compare vendors, rule out unsuitable products, or validate a shortlist, there may be no referral click. The customer could make substantial progress toward a decision without visiting any of the cited sources. That does not mean the AI interaction had no value, but it also does not mean every brand mentioned in the response benefited commercially.

The question I want to answer is what happened during the evaluation, particularly whether the company had enough supporting evidence to be considered a suitable choice.

A recent conversation with an executive brought this into focus for me. He was evaluating outside consulting support and explained that his organization had been disappointed by consultants and vendors three times in the previous year. He was not interested in another polished sales pitch or a broad claim of expertise. Having been burned repeatedly, he intended to make the next selection process considerably more demanding.

What stood out was his sense of responsibility for the decision. He needed to be confident that the next consultant could deliver, but he also needed to justify that confidence to the organization.

We sometimes underestimate how much of a complex purchasing decision is about avoiding risk. Buyers are trying to establish that a product will work in their environment, meet the requirements of different stakeholders, and avoid creating problems they will later have to explain. When budgets are tight, the consequences of getting that decision wrong become even more significant.

A company can be well-known, highly visible, and frequently mentioned without addressing those concerns.

Consider a buyer evaluating specialized software. Knowing that a vendor is a recognized provider may be useful at the beginning of the process. Once the buyer introduces requirements involving integrations, compliance, implementation capacity, or operational constraints, general category visibility becomes much less informative. The buyer needs evidence that the vendor can satisfy those requirements.

This is where I believe the distinction between appearing in an answer and earning a recommendation becomes commercially important.

See also: 3 Steps To Win B2B Brand Consideration, Day Zero Decides The Shortlist

We saw this problem in an engagement with a specialized B2B software company. Across the AI responses we observed, the brand was cited 98% of the time. By most visibility measures, that would be considered an excellent result. The company was clearly being recognized within its category.

At the same time, it was receiving little observable AI referral traffic, and its lead volume was declining. We could not establish that AI had caused the decline, but the situation prompted us to look more closely at what happened when buyers moved beyond broad-category questions.

We began testing more specific decision scenarios, introducing the requirements that a prospective customer might use to determine which vendors to include on a shortlist. Under those conditions, the company’s recommendation rate was 17%. In a separate testing condition where supporting evidence was absent, the rate was 8%.

As we examined the responses, we found that important capabilities were not always supported by information the AI systems could retrieve. In a workshop reviewing those gaps, someone on the client team said, “We have all of that. We just don’t have it on the website.”

I have heard variations of that statement throughout my career, but it takes on a different significance when customers can use AI to evaluate vendors before speaking with sales.

There are often legitimate reasons information is not publicly available. Some details are commercially sensitive. Some capabilities are complex enough that sales or technical teams prefer to explain them directly. Other information simply never makes it onto the website because nobody has recognized its importance to the buying decision.

Historically, a website could generate interest and leave the lead nurturing to sales. As research has shown, especially in B2B, AI-assisted evaluation often takes place before the buyer makes contact, creating a significant disadvantage. The company may satisfy the requirement, yet the available evidence may not be sufficient to establish that suitability.

The answer is not to just publish everything but to clearly identify the requirements customers are likely to evaluate and determine what credible, appropriately disclosed evidence can support those assessments.

See also: B2B Buyers Choose A Vendor Before They Reach Out – 3 Ways To Be Visible When It Counts

This is the thinking behind the Decision Coverage framework I introduced in Decision Coverage: Why AI Recommends Some Brands And Not Others.

In an earlier article, When AI Takes The Click, Click Worthiness Should Guide Your Strategy, I argued that marketers should focus more attention on interactions where direct engagement still adds meaningful value after AI has answered an informational question. Decision Coverage approaches the problem from the other side: before a customer engages directly, have we made enough evidence available for our company to be evaluated accurately?

One useful concept is the eligibility gate, a requirement that a solution must meet to be considered suitable for a particular buyer. This is a way to model purchasing decisions, not a claim that language models use a fixed internal checklist.

A buyer might regard some capabilities as desirable and others as mandatory. If a vendor cannot satisfy a critical requirement, it may not belong on the shortlist, regardless of how visible it is. But there is another possibility that marketers need to recognize: the vendor meets the requirement, yet the supporting evidence is missing or difficult to retrieve.

Traditional content-gap analysis does not always uncover this problem. We may find that a competitor has written about a topic we have not covered, or that our pages need stronger explanations of a product category. Those findings can improve discoverability, but they may have little bearing on whether a buyer can establish that we meet a mandatory requirement.

Decision Coverage starts with the decision itself. What is the customer trying to accomplish? Who is involved? Which requirements are essential, which are negotiable, and what trade-offs are likely to influence the choice? We can then examine whether the information available about the company is sufficient to support an assessment against those criteria.

This requires significantly more cooperation across the business than many traditional SEO activities. Marketing may understand the buyer persona, but product teams often hold the evidence needed to answer them, and most importantly, sales understands the prospects’ objections and evaluation criteria.

That is another reason I am concerned about wasting the current level of executive attention. We finally have an opportunity to bring these groups together around a problem that matters to customers. I would rather use that access to close important evidence gaps than simply produce more category content.

Our research into AI fan-out questions gave us another reason to focus on decision criteria. In one study beginning with a prompt asking for the best solution in a software category, 62% of the observed questions concerned understanding the category, developing evaluation criteria, or comparing vendors.

Those observations do not reveal a model’s complete internal reasoning, and I would not suggest that every AI system follows the same process. What they show is that the observable research supporting a recommendation can involve considerably more than retrieving a list of vendors.

We also found that recommendations sometimes changed when we altered the buyer’s perspective. A prompt framed around an individual’s professional responsibilities did not always produce the same recommendations as one written from the perspective of a department or organization.

In another test, a vendor appeared as the leading recommendation until we changed the emphasis and ordering of the requirements. That vendor then disappeared from the recommended set, replaced by a company that had not previously appeared but was particularly strong on the newly emphasized criterion.

I cannot attribute that change solely to the order of the requirements, as we saw other cases where retests days later prompted different interpretations, putting focus on different criteria. Nevertheless, the results made it difficult to defend the idea that recommendation performance could be represented adequately by a single broad prompt or a static collection of category queries.

That is why understanding decision context matters. A company may be the strongest option for one combination of requirements and less suitable when another constraint takes priority. Unfortunately, we don’t have unlimited budgets for every conceivable prompt, but we can maybe use larger diagnostic prompts that capture multiple prompt turns.

Accordingly, for measurement and alignment purposes, I would rather test a representative set of buyer decisions than simply increase the number of prompts being tracked. The objective is to understand when the company qualifies, where evidence is missing, and which competing vendors are better supported for particular requirements. Those are the critical insights we need to understand to truly close gaps.

Mike King also raises a related measurement issue in How Do We Move Forward if the SERP Is Generative?. As search results become dynamically generated, he argues that conventional assumptions about stable rankings are increasingly difficult to maintain. Measuring visibility requires repeated observations and a probabilistic approach rather than relying on a single result.

I agree, and I think recommendation measurement needs to account for another source of variation: the customer’s requirements.

A company might appear in nearly every broad category response but be recommended infrequently for the decision scenarios that matter most to its business. Another company might have lower overall visibility but be consistently recommended when its particular strengths align with the buyer’s needs. Aggregated mention counts cannot adequately explain either situation.

For our B2B software client, the evidence gaps suggested a testable hypothesis. If the company genuinely satisfies important buyer requirements but has not made that information retrievable, improving the available evidence should increase its recommendation frequency under comparable decision scenarios.

We can establish a baseline, address specific gaps, and repeat the testing. Because generated responses vary, that work needs multiple observations and consistent scenarios rather than a few favorable examples.

I want to be careful about what an improvement would mean, however. A higher recommendation rate would suggest that the available evidence is better supporting the company’s suitability. It would not prove that buyers are choosing the company more often or that the work has increased revenue.

That distinction matters especially for this client, whose sales cycle typically lasts 9 to 14 months. Even if better evidence starts influencing evaluations now, we may not see the corresponding effect on qualified opportunities or pipeline for several quarters. Some buyers may already have made their decisions while important information was missing.

We also have to recognize that the decline in leads could have several causes. Market demand, product competitiveness, sales execution, and other marketing activity may all be influencing performance. The combination of declining leads and weak recommendation performance is a reason to investigate, not evidence of causation.

I would therefore measure progress in stages. First, determine whether the gaps in decision-support evidence have actually been closed. Then test whether recommendation performance changes in the scenarios where those gaps mattered. Over time, examine whether qualified opportunities, pipeline, and eventually revenue show corresponding improvements, while considering other factors that could explain the results.

Customer interviews and self-reported discovery information may help fill some of the attribution gaps. They will not provide perfect answers, but they can help us understand whether AI-assisted research played a role in the buying process. This is consistent with Indig’s broader finding that neither referral traffic nor changes in direct traffic can explain AI’s commercial influence on their own.

I would not make AI referral traffic the primary KPI for this work. If the customer can reach an evaluation or shortlist decision without clicking, referral counts may tell us relatively little about whether our information helped or hindered that decision.

I think Fishkin is right to challenge the industry on business value. The findings from Haynes, The SEO Works, and Indig should make marketers cautious about presenting increased AI visibility as evidence of commercial success. King’s work also reminds us that the visibility we are trying to measure is becoming less stable and more dependent on context.

My concern is that we will respond by building increasingly sophisticated visibility dashboards without addressing what happens when a buyer asks whether a company is actually suitable.

For complex purchases, that question is often more important than whether the brand appeared in an earlier answer. Customers are trying to reduce risk, compare alternatives, and make defensible decisions. If AI systems are helping them do that work, our responsibility is to make sure the information available about our companies supports an accurate evaluation.

This is also an opportunity for search professionals to expand their contribution. The attention GEO is receiving has created access to people and resources that were often difficult to engage through conventional SEO initiatives. We can use that access to work with product, sales, technical, and legal teams to identify what customers need to establish and make appropriate evidence available.

I do need to know whether a brand is being discovered and represented accurately, but more importantly, I want to know whether the brand is recommended when it genuinely meets a buyer’s requirements, what evidence supports that recommendation, and where missing information may be limiting consideration.

If we can improve those leading indicators and then establish, over time, that they contribute to higher-quality opportunities and better commercial outcomes, we will have a much stronger argument for GEO investment than mention counts alone can provide.

The question is not whether AI visibility has value. It is whether we are confusing being present in an answer with being considered in a decision.

More than twenty-five schools turn up and most teachers leave with a flyer, chatting to colleagues about where the bus could park onsite. On a basic level, the charity is telling the story of how technology has iterated. "I guess it’s bridging the gap between the polished and finished latest game," Jason says, "which could be perceived as not achievable for them, to seeing how all that coding came about. This, he hopes, might be the spark for some students to get into coding, especially for PC games, where under-18s are an underrepresented demographic.

Retro-focused videos on YouTube and TikTok rack up millions of views of views, and communities formed on reddit or Discord - some with hundreds of thousands of members - discuss nothing else.

For Luke, though, this misses the point. He likes the click when you put a cartridge in a machine; and he likes that he is just him and the game. And, while the vast majority of young players go online without incident, it is true that those who use chat functions will likely interact with strangers. The same research found that more than half (55%) of children who game online say they play with people they have never met offline at least sometimes, while one in five (20%) do so all or most of the time they play.

By contrast, Jason claims that not one student has asked if they can get online, and he’s also never seen one get out their phone when they’re on the bus. Perhaps it is a welcomed break. "We offer a completely safe environment to come and experience these games and machines. That's really what we're here for," he states.

Luke shares too that he became nervous of the data he was sharing with gaming companies, and conscious that this was all being stored, thus making it vulnerable to breaches. He also baulked at what he felt was a constant money grab from gaming companies, including moves that render games unsupported or completely obsolete. Retro gaming is cheap in comparison and therefore more accessible, especially for younger players. "When you tell them that they can get an old console for less than £100 and often games come free when you buy them second hand, their ears really prick up," Jason says. "For a lot of the youngsters, the cost of modern gaming equipment is unattainable. John W. Champeau is founder of Champ Games, a Connecticut-based venture, which develops retro games system primarily for the Atari 2600. But there may also be something intrinsically different about how these games are designed to be played that is driving their renaissance. Retro games had a start and an end – and with the latter came a big hit of dopamine for achieving victory. Modern games are designed as "loops for addiction" with no real end; and constant prompts to buy. Satisfaction is constantly deferred.
