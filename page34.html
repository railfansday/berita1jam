Between Moonlight and Aurora: Photographing East Greenland After Dark

Photographing East Greenland at night is an exercise in preparation, observation, and adaptation. The landscape is immense, the conditions can be demanding, and the opportunities to make a particular image may be limited to a few minutes. Towering icebergs, drifting sea ice, fog, low clouds, and the changing Arctic sky create an environment in which light can completely transform the way a scene is perceived. Yet having spectacular scenery in front of you does not necessarily mean you will come away with a strong photograph. The challenge is to recognize what makes a moment worth capturing, understand how the available light interacts with the landscape, and make the right decisions before that moment disappears.

During a recent photography expedition in East Greenland, hosted by Secret Atlas this last October 2026, I spent several evenings leading the photographic project targeting enormous icebergs illuminated by the rising moon, alongside the Northern Lights appearing above the surrounding fjords and ice. These two subjects offered very different photographic challenges and a particular one-of-a-kind night operation.

Some photographs begin long before the camera is taken out of the bag. The moonrise series was one of those projects. Across four days, I followed the changing opportunities to photograph the rising moon among the enormous icebergs of East Greenland. The objective was not simply to record the moon above the horizon, but to find a relationship between the moonlight, the architecture of the icebergs, the sense of exploration, and the surrounding landscape.

The first opportunity to photograph the sequence came around 8 p.m. on day three of our expedition, when the timing and the available light finally began to align. In the Arctic, twilight can retain considerable ambient light even as the moon rises. This transition creates an interesting balance: the landscape may still hold detail, while the moon begins illuminating the ice from a different angle.

A rising moon and a monumental iceberg are impressive subjects individually, but putting them together is not enough. Their positions, relative sizes, illumination, and separation from the background all influence how the image is read. Anticipating these relationships is essential, particularly when the window for making the photograph is short.

Photographing from a Zodiac adds another dimension to the process. Polar bear presence in East Greenland limits our possibility to operate on land during low-light conditions. Unlike working from stable ground, the shooting position is not necessarily fixed, and even small movements can affect the framing, particularly when using a long focal length.

The objective was to find a viewpoint from which the iceberg formations could be separated visually and the rising moon incorporated without losing the sense of scale. Icebergs are difficult subjects to photograph because their dimensions can be hard to communicate.

Focal length plays an important role in communicating scale. A wide-angle lens can emphasize the surrounding environment, while a longer lens can compress the scene and bring distant formations visually closer together.

Exposure requires similar care. A gradually brightening moon against a dark sky can make it difficult to preserve highlights without losing detail in the shadows. Rather than trying to make every part of the photograph equally bright, I aim to retain the texture of the illuminated surfaces while allowing darkness to remain part of the image, preserving my highlight and shadow information.

The Northern Lights create a very different set of challenges. The moon’s position can be anticipated, allowing me to plan around a relatively predictable source of illumination. This is great for subject illumination, though distracting when the moon appears in the frame. The aurora, by contrast, can change rapidly in brightness, shape, color, and movement.

From our main vessel, we navigate through the fjord in search of a good-looking iceberg to frame our shot. We carefully position the ship to achieve the greatest possible stability while maintaining the flexibility to adjust our orientation depending on where the aurora is expected to appear. The moment the Northern Lights emerge, close coordination via VHF radio with the bridge team becomes essential. As we prepare to photograph from the ship’s outer deck, the vessel is brought to a complete standstill, reaching zero speed over ground. This allows us to capture the scene with maximum stability, even using exposures of around half a second, while the iceberg and the dancing aurora create the perfect natural composition.

Aurora photography involves a series of compromises between shutter speed, aperture, and ISO. A longer exposure collects more light, which can help when the display is faint, but it can also blur the finer structures of a rapidly moving aurora. A shorter exposure preserves more detail in the curtains and patterns but requires enough light to produce a usable image. There is no universal exposure for the Northern Lights. The right settings depend on the brightness and speed of the display, the available ambient light, the lens, and the amount of detail I want to retain. I adjust according to the behavior of the sky rather than relying on a fixed recipe, as shown below with this powerful curtain of light.

As the sun began to set, we left the comfort of our main vessel and ventured out aboard our Zodiacs for a reconnaissance ride through the surrounding waters. Our mission was to explore the landscape, identify one or two particularly striking icebergs, and select the perfect setting for what we hoped would become an unforgettable night of aurora photography. Capturing the Northern Lights is as much about patience and preparation as it is about being in the right place at the right time. With exposures ranging from half a second to four, five, or even six seconds, stability becomes essential. Even the slightest movement can compromise an image. Out here, surrounded by the vastness of the polar landscape, finding a way to create a stable photographic platform would be one of the greatest challenges of the night.

Our idea was to return after dark, launching the Zodiacs from the main vessel and navigating through the freezing waters in search of newly formed sea ice near one of the selected icebergs. This young ice, approximately one centimeter thick, offered the possibility of carefully positioning the bow of a Zodiac against its edge, allowing us to wedge ourselves into place and create the stability required for long exposures. Finding a sufficiently wide area of newly formed ice would also give us the freedom to reposition ourselves as the auroras shifted and danced across different parts of the sky. But this was about more than simply finding a stable platform. It was about finding the right perspective, in pitch darkness surrounded by the freezing Greenlandic waters.

When planning an operation like this, I always look for ways to approach an iceberg while maintaining a safe distance and shooting from as low an angle as possible. I want the iceberg to become a powerful element in the composition, rising beneath the aurora and giving the image a strong sense of scale and depth. The newly formed sea ice often adds another dimension to the scene. Its delicate patterns, textures, and natural leading lines can guide the viewer’s eye toward the main subject, transforming an already extraordinary landscape into a compelling photographic composition.

By 11 p.m., it was time. We left the main vessel and began our night mission. Alongside my teammates Signe-Maria and Myriam, I led our group of photographers toward the giant icebergs and the newly formed sea ice. Behind us, the ship’s powerful searchlight illuminated our chosen iceberg, providing a guiding beacon across the darkness as we made our way toward our destination. Once we reached the area, we carefully positioned the Zodiacs against the edge of the newly formed ice. Every movement had to be deliberate. We wanted to achieve the stability needed for photography while maintaining a safe distance from the iceberg and the surrounding ice.

Fortunately, we had prepared for this moment long before leaving the ship. During the day, we had already assembled, adjusted, and practiced our photographic setups, making sure everyone knew exactly what to do. Now, under the darkness of the polar night, there was no time to waste. We secured our tripods against the Zodiac’s pontoon and tilted them forward, carefully adjusting their position to keep every part of the Zodiac out of the frame. One by one, our photographers settled into their compositions, pointing their cameras toward the magnificent icebergs.

Everything was ready. I picked up the radio and called the ship’s bridge team, asking them to switch off all the lights. And suddenly, everything changed. The powerful beam that had guided us across the darkness disappeared. We were now completely immersed in the polar night, resting on the newly formed sea ice, facing a gigantic iceberg beneath an extraordinary celestial spectacle. Above us, twenty billion stars seemed to fill the sky. A half-moon gently illuminated the frozen landscape, revealing the magnificent shapes and textures of the iceberg, while the auroras began dancing overhead, bringing movement and color to an otherwise silent world.

For a moment, everything seemed to stand still. There we were, floating between the ocean and the ice, surrounded by one of the most extraordinary landscapes on Earth. Cameras were clicking, shutters were opening and closing, and each long exposure was capturing a fleeting moment of this ever-changing spectacle. It is difficult to describe the feeling of witnessing such a scene. You can plan every detail, prepare your equipment, study the conditions, and carefully orchestrate the entire operation, but nature always has the final word. And when everything finally comes together, the experience goes far beyond photography. That night, we were fortunate enough to experience something truly exceptional. A moment in which preparation, teamwork, adventure, and nature converged beneath the dancing auroras. The photographs will remain on our SD cards, but the feeling of being there, surrounded by the icebergs, the stars, and the silence of the polar night, will remain with us forever.

There is a tendency to describe photographs of the Arctic as the result of being in the right place at the right time. Luck certainly plays a role. The moon must rise in a favorable position, the weather must allow the landscape to be seen, and the aurora must develop in a way that creates an interesting composition. None of these conditions can be guaranteed. But being present when a spectacle occurs is only part of the process. The photographer must recognize the potential of the scene, decide where to position the camera, choose a focal length, balance the exposure, and determine which elements belong in the frame. Preparation does not control nature; it makes it possible to respond when an opportunity appears.

Premium Upgrade? United Airlines Is Replacing Its Remaining Boeing 767s With 787 Dreamliners

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On September 8, 1982, United Airlines brought the Boeing 767 into commercial service, revolutionizing its long-haul network with a twin-engine aircraft that could safely operate transatlantic routes dominated by quadjets. The airline still uses 53 of these legacy twinjets to anchor its transatlantic network from Newark Liberty International Airport (EWR) and Washington Dulles International Airport (IAD) more than 40 years later. The current fleet consists of 16 stretched 767-400ER aircraft with an average age of 23 years and 37 older 767-300ER aircraft with an average age of 29.

United has historically relied on the 767 to serve European cities 'across the pond.' Now, United intends to retire all of these aircraft by 2030. To attract surging transatlantic travel demand without flooding routes with low-margin coach tickets, United is deploying 222-seat Elevated Boeing 787-9s featuring 99 premium seats, including 64 enclosed Polaris suites and 35 Premium Plus recliners, alongside stretched 787-10s for slot-constrained hubs like London Heathrow Airport (LHR).

The post-pandemic rise in demand for year-round leisure and business travel to Europe reshaped United's business model around a 'premiumized' cabin. The Dreamliner's wider composite fuselage accommodates spacious suites in the front of the cabin with a lower 6,000-foot (1,829-meter) cabin altitude and higher air humidity, which softens jet lag regardless of seat number. Combined with 25% better fuel efficiency over the 767, these upgrades let United expand premium capacity and passenger comfort on top European routes and lower operating costs.

Moving from the 1980s-era 767 to the 787 Dreamliner improves passenger appeal but also route flexibility. While the 767's flight range is limited to roughly 5,980 nautical miles (11,075 km), the next-gen 787-9 flies up to 7,565 nautical miles (14,010 km). Its faster Mach 0.85 cruise speed also shaves up to 50 minutes off eastbound Atlantic crossings, helping United meet tight European airport arrival slots while easily rotating aircraft onto Pacific routes on the West Coast for seasonal destinations.

In 2022, following a landmark order for 100 787s and an option to buy 100 more, United CEO Scott Kirby gave these remarks: "United emerged from the pandemic as the world's leading global airline and the flag carrier of the US. This order further solidifies our lead and creates new opportunities ... to connect more people to more places around the globe and deliver the best experience in the sky."

Stan Deal, president and CEO of Boeing Commercial Airplanes, also offered this comment: "With this investment in its future fleet, the 737. The Boeing team is honored by United's trust in our family of airplanes to connect people and transport cargo around the world for decades to come."

Evolving from aluminum to composite jets not only makes the passenger experience better but also opens up more freight revenue for UA. The 767-300ER maxes out at 412,000 pounds (186,880 kg). In comparison, United can transport up to 52,400 pounds (23,768 kg) of high-margin commercial freight in the belly on each transatlantic flight, with the 787-9's increased maximum takeoff weight of 560,000 pounds (254,011 kg). That's before all the other improvements the Dreamliner delivers.

Passengers traveling across the Atlantic will see immediate changes on the flightline and inside the cabin when the outdated 767s are retired in favor of 787 Dreamliners. The wider composite cabin significantly improves personal living space across all seating classes as compared to the tiny 1980s-era 767 configuration. While older 767s had open, doorless business seats and tight overhead bins, the forthcoming Elevated 787-9s feature enclosed Polaris suites with sliding privacy doors, wireless charging, and larger 4K TVs.

Economy class passengers also benefit from wider aisles, sculpted sidewalls, dynamic LED mood lighting to facilitate time-zone transitions, and oversized overhead bins that can carry each passenger's roll-aboard bags. Additionally, the 787's single-piece carbon-fiber body can withstand substantially higher internal pressure without experiencing metal fatigue, enabling the cabin to maintain a comfortable 6,000 feet (1,829 meters) of pressure with 16% air humidity.

Meanwhile, the 767 must maintain cabin pressure at the old standard of 8,000 feet (2,438 meters) in simulated elevation. It must also maintain air humidity below 10% to prevent metal joints from rusting, since a conventional riveted aluminum fuselage bends and stresses under high air pressure. After seven hours in the air overnight, passengers benefit from the increased blood oxygen level that the 787 delivers.

That means fewer headaches and substantially reduced jet lag thanks to the lower cabin altitude and additional moisture. Dreamliner windows are also 65% larger than those on the 767 and use electronic dimming controls rather than plastic pull blinds to manage sunlight. The Dreamliner cabin is also significantly quieter during takeoff and cruise thanks to sophisticated acoustic insulation surrounding the engine nacelles and the lack of noisy high-pressure air lines for sub-systems because of its all-electric architecture.

United Airlines has overtaken ANA as the world's largest Boeing 787 operator, with further Dreamliner expansion planned.

Despite UA's rapid trajectory of 'premiumization,' Boeing must resolve significant assembly delays at its North Charleston facility in order to support the airline's goals. In order to replace its 53 outdated 767s, United Airlines updated its backlog to a firm order for 150 Boeing 787 Dreamliners, with options for a further 50. However, production was delayed considerably by Federal Aviation Administration safety inspections following quality problems with composite materials. Due to these delays, United was obliged to reduce the number of Boeing aircraft it planned to deliver in the near future from 165 to just 63, delaying the majority of new widebody deliveries until after 2026.

In 2023, Kirby gave this update on the UA fleet: "Our planning and focus on the long term have helped us surge past other airlines that stood still. I'm convinced our strategy is the right one as we continue to add new, larger aircraft to take full advantage of our growing flying opportunities both internationally and domestically."

Meanwhile, Deal spoke for Boeing and added these remarks: "United's unprecedented commitment to the 787 Dreamliner family is a testament to the market-leading operating economics and reliability of Boeing's widebody jets. With the 787, United will enjoy all the synergies of operating one of the largest, most efficient fleets in the skies."

United will still be forced to spend millions of dollars maintaining the safe operation of 30-year-old aluminum aircraft while waiting years for delayed Dreamliners. High-time 767-300ER and 767-400ER aircraft are subjected to extensive, costly structural inspections by mechanics rather than being retired to desert storage. In order to make up for the timetable deficit, older aircraft, such as tail N641UA, which has nearly 100,000 flight hours and 20,000 landings, require expensive overhauls on their older General Electric engines in addition to thorough wing and tail scanning inspections.

After taking a $200 million financial hit from factory delays and plane groundings, United CEO Scott Kirby put together a backup plan. Along with leasing 35 Airbus A321neo jets to cover short-term domestic flights, Kirby kept United's delayed order for 45 Airbus A350-900 widebodies alive. Holding onto those European factory slots gives United a real alternative if Boeing's South Carolina assembly delays continue.

Moving from the Boeing 767-300ER to the 787-9 represents a major leap forward in jet engine design, which enables greater range and payload as well as better fuel burn rates. United's older 767s run on Pratt & Whitney PW4060 or General Electric CF6-80C2B6F engines built with 94-inch (239 cm) titanium fans and a 4.8:1 bypass ratio, producing around 60,200 pounds of thrust (268 kN). In contrast, the 787-9 uses General Electric GEnx-1B engines rated up to 75,000 pounds of thrust (334 kN).

The GEnx features 111-inch (282 cm) carbon-fiber fan blades, a lightweight composite fan case that cuts over 400 pounds (181 kg) per engine, and an 8.8:1 bypass ratio that trims fuel burn by over 20% while cutting nitrogen oxide emissions by 55%. Additionally, the two generations also differ in how each aircraft powers its internal systems.

In order to run the cabin air conditioning, start the engines, and heat the wings against ice, the 767 uses a conventional bleed-air configuration that draws hot compressed air above 392°F (200°C) straight from the engine cores, depriving the turbines of useful power. In order to improve engine efficiency and reduce cruise fuel consumption by about 4%, Boeing replaced the titanium air ducts on the 787 with an all-electric system powered by four engine-mounted generators that produce 1.4 megawatts of electricity.

Since the 767's body is made of conventional aluminum sheets welded over metal ribs, it is susceptible to rust and metal fatigue during thousands of pressurization cycles. In contrast, the 787 uses single-piece body sections and long, thin wings that flex upward by roughly 10 feet (3 meters) at the tips during flight to smooth out turbulence and reduce air drag at Mach 0.85. Strong carbon-fiber composites constitute half of the 787's structural weight.

Gemma Arterton's 'Secret Service' Is a Twisty Spy Thriller That Never Quite Sticks | Review

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

There's something so mesmerizing about a good spy story. Double lives, high stakes, tense action, not knowing who to trust; all of these elements keep you on the edge of your seat in all the best ways. The genre has continued to thrive on television, with 2026 being an exceptional year for British spy thrillers in particular. New seasons of Slow Horses, Black Doves, and The Night Manager have kept fans entertained — not to mention the announcement of a new James Bond, and the frenzy that's caused has revealed the level of viewers' investment in these stories.

MGM+ is adding to the year of spy thrillers with its series, Secret Service, starring Gemma Arterton. Like Slow Horses, Secret Service is adapted from the Tom Bradby book of the same name, part of a trilogy centered on its protagonist, Kate Henderson. While the new adaptation succeeds in delivering a season with twists and intrigue, it doesn’t do enough to keep you engaged with its characters beyond what the plot requires.

Secret Service revolves around Kate Henderson (Gemma Arterton) — wife, mother of two, and senior MI6 officer in charge of the Russia Desk. At the start of the series, she's leading an investigation into the head of Russian intelligence. Through her team's work, they uncover that a high-ranking UK politician is working as a Russian asset and is ready to move into the Prime Minister's role. At first, this information is met with skepticism, but when Prime Minister Anthony Fletcher (Steven Elder) announces his pancreatic cancer and his resignation, it becomes a race against time to figure out who the Russian asset is — pushing Kate to the brink and forcing her to question who she can even trust.

Throughout its five-episode mission, Secret Service keeps the narrative moving forward, reflecting the urgency of the situation at hand. The story pops back and forth between London and Malta, as Kate's team keeps tabs on their informant, Lena (Alma Prelec), a nanny to the Director of the SVR. The tension ramps up when Lena is discovered by one of Igor Borodin's (Miglen Mirtchev) operatives as a mole. Kate finds herself split, fighting a war on multiple fronts: uncovering the Russian asset, extracting Lena out of Malta, and being present with her family when she's home. With its short episode count, Secret Service is on a mission to keep its story tight, and the show does a great job of not padding its runtime.

At the center of Secret Service is Arterton, who has proven her acting prowess within the spy genre in films like Quantum of Solace and The King's Man, and it remains true here. Her performance anchors the series, as Kate does what she can to not let the weight of the situation break her, only allowing herself to express vulnerability to her husband, Stuart (Rafe Spall), and her mentor, Rose Trewen (Alex Kingston). Of the characters in the series, Kate is the most well-rounded and complex, yet despite this, moments of depth in her character and relationships are cut short in favor of the plot. There's very little to anchor audiences to her or even remember her after the credits roll in the finale.

Without Kate, everyone else around her falls flat. Kyril Markov (Oleg Levin), one of Borodin's operatives, definitely ratchets up the tension once he realizes that Lena is a mole, but he's also not nearly as intimidating as he could’ve been, especially since Markov has a reputation within the Russian Desk. In the same vein, there's much to be desired from Stuart. Beyond his job and working relationship with the Foreign Secretary, Imogen Conrad (Amaka Okafor), there's very little audiences are getting with him. We’re treated to glimpses of Kate and Stuart's marriage, heavily leaning on the settled domesticity of this point in their relationship. To really bring this home, Alex Kingston isn't given nearly enough when Rose has so much potential. Sure, there are plot reasons as to why this is, but it's not enough to remain attached to the characters beyond Kate.

She starred in 2008's 'Quantum of Solace.'

If the characters aren't entirely deep enough to anchor the story, Secret Service delivers exactly what it promises: a tight spy thriller with a mystery that keeps you invested from start to finish. The show has several good twists to hold your attention as Kate attempts to identify the Russian asset. Like a good mystery, it doesn't overstay its welcome, nor does it dumb itself down for the audience.

In a TV age where there are too many options to choose from, not every show is going to stick with you. Sometimes, all you need to be entertained is a good plot that keeps you hitting play on the next episode. That's the case with Secret Service — it's engaging in the moment for the thriller it is; no more, no less. If this is all you get from the series, it's still time well spent. Viewers won't be left in the dark; the show wraps up its story with very few loose ends.

Should Secret Service be picked up for a second season, one that adapts either of the remaining books in Bradby's trilogy, it would be advantageous to use the show's middle chapter to dive deeper into Kate's psyche and the remaining relationships in her circle. There's potential to enhance the narrative, for the cast to really stretch their acting muscles, and to further explore the political consequences. Otherwise, Secret Service will just come and go as you scroll through your streaming services to find what to watch next.

If Your Social Security Benefit Is Above This Average, You're Ahead of Most Retirees

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Beating the national Social Security average is one thing, but whether that check actually covers retirement is a far tougher test.

By Kailey Hagen, CFP® – Nov 22, 2026 at 10:00AM EST

Where does your monthly benefit check sit compared to the rest of the country? As of October 2026, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA), the average monthly benefit for retired workers is $2,092.17. That amounts to about $25,106.04 per year. This single figure covers everyone from those who just claimed at 62 to retirees well into their 90s.

Averages depend heavily on who you are and when you claimed. For instance, the average for people aged 62 is $1,424.40 per month, while those aged 70 receive an average of $2,274.68. Gender also creates a significant gap in the final numbers.

Averages also vary between men and women across the entire retired-worker population. As of December 2025, according to the SSA, these were the monthly averages for men and women across all age groups. They reflect the combined impact of different career earnings and claiming choices.

Landing above the $2,092.17 benchmark isn't a matter of luck. The SSA calculates your benefit based on an average of your monthly wages across your 35 highest-earning years. It adjusts earlier wages for inflation and runs them through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. Higher lifetime earnings generally result in a larger monthly check.

Claiming age is the other primary lever for beating the average. Full retirement age (FRA) ranges from 66 to 67, depending on your birth year, and it's 67 for everyone born in 1960 or later. Claiming at age 62 can reduce your check by up to 30%, while waiting until 70 earns delayed retirement credits worth 8% per year.

While beating the average is helpful, it's rarely enough to cover a full retirement. Social Security is designed to replace only about 40% of your pre-retirement income. However, the average senior household headed by someone 65 or older spends $61,432 per year, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). That's much higher than the $25,106.04 annual average benefit.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027 to help checks keep pace with inflation. That adjustment adds about $75 per month to the $2,092.17 average. Even with these raises, most retirees rely on savings or other income sources to bridge the gap between their benefits and their total living costs. Beating the Social Security average is not the goal; covering your actual retirement expenses is.

If you want to increase your odds of claiming an above-average benefit, there are three things you must do. First, work at least 35 years before you apply for Social Security. This will ensure you have no zero-income years included in your benefit calculation, which would weigh down your checks.

Second, do what you can to increase your income today. The larger your average income throughout your career, the larger your future Social Security checks will be.

Third, choose your claiming age carefully. Claiming Social Security early could make sense if you have a short life expectancy or don't have much in personal savings. Otherwise, you may get a larger lifetime benefit by delaying Social Security until your FRA or possibly until you qualify for your largest checks at age 70.

Why you should never vacuum the inside of your computer

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

A quick clean could result in a lot of damage.

Dust: the enemy hiding in every PC case. It clogs vents and builds up between heatsink fins, making it harder for your PC's fans to keep things cool. Naturally, you'll want to clean it out. Dealing with debris almost anywhere else in your home, you'd reach for your vacuum cleaner. It's got the power to clean it all out, with narrow attachments to reach in those awkward gaps and corners.

Bringing a vacuum cleaner to clean inside your computer is like bringing a pressure washer to clean a painting. You might clear the dirt, but you risk damaging what you're trying to clean. With sensitive (and expensive) components inside, it's not just physical damage you have to worry about. You've got static to worry about, too. There are better ways to clean your PC case, so if you're thinking about grabbing your vacuum, there are good reasons why you should really give it a miss.

Have you ever felt an electric shock after you've walked across a carpet and touched something metal? That's an electrostatic discharge: a buildup of electric charge finding a path to somewhere with unequal electrical potential. A small, momentary annoyance for you, but inside your PC, even a discharge too small for you to feel can damage sensitive components.

A household vacuum can build up static as dust and other particles travel through its hose, particularly around plastic parts. If you bring the nozzle close to the components inside your PC, that charge is then able to transfer into them.

There are no guarantees. You might get lucky with no damage. Alternatively, your first attempt might be catastrophic. Unfortunately, there's just no way to prevent (or estimate) such outcomes. Electrostatic damage can cause immediate failures, but it can also detrimentally affect a component without you initially realizing. Given how expensive a PC can be, especially high-end gaming PCs, it likely isn't ever worth the risk.

There's an exception, though. You can buy specialist vacuums that are designed to work with static-sensitive electronics. These aren't the same as the household cleaners you're using on your carpets and rugs. They're usually built with materials in the hose and attachments that allow static charge to dissipate rather than accumulate. It'll give that charge a path to safely ground rather than build up around the nozzle, where it could come into contact with your PC.

While static is a big risk, there are other factors involved. Bluntly, a vacuum cleaner is a fairly clumsy tool. In a PC case, especially a small one, there isn't a lot of space to move around. There are cables to think about, along with exposed components that you don't really want to catch with the nozzle. It's very easy to knock a connector off while trying to reach a patch of dust, particularly if the hose is blocking your view.

You may also use attachments. Brush attachments, for instance, might catch on small parts. A hard nozzle could strike against a circuit board or bend small fins on a heatsink. Then there are loose screws; if any drop inside your case, then a vacuum will whisk them away.

You'll have to think particularly carefully about the fans, too. Air pulled through by a vacuum can make the blades spin. Forcing this to happen rapidly can put unnecessary stress on its bearings, but there's also the potential of physical damage.

Laptops don't give you much room to work with, either. You could shove a vacuum nozzle against the cooling vents to clean your laptop fans, but you can't see what's happening inside or hold the fan still to stop the suction from spinning it. If dust has built up between the fan and the heatsink fins, you may need to remove the bottom cover to reach it, if your model allows it. The same advice applies, though: a laptop is just a more compact PC, and a vacuum won't be any safer inside one.

With any luck, you've now been warned off from putting vacuum inside your PC (unless it's a specialized, anti-static one). There are simply too many downsides and too many risks. There are many better options, too. A can of compressed air, or an electric air duster designed for electronics, can help you quickly blow dust out of a PC case.

Whatever you do, you'll want to start by switching your PC off and disconnecting it from an outlet. Take out the external power cables and, if you can, move it somewhere clean. Ideally, somewhere with plenty of air flow, especially if you're using air to blast out the dust. If you can't, use a mask if you have one spare, and make sure to open plenty of windows.

Compressed air is your friend when it comes to PC cleaning. Compressed air cans, or an electric air duster, is a static-free alternative to vacuuming. You're pushing air through the case with enough force to clean the dust out. Short bursts are best, just so you can control where you're directing the air without sending dust further into the case. They're particularly useful for cleaning out any clumps of dust stuck deep inside.

There's no special knack to this; you'll just want to use your judgement. Where you can, you might want to try and hold fan blades still while cleaning around them, especially if you're using an electric duster with a lot of power. You've still got to be careful with delicate components, so don't push tools blindly or force a nozzle into a gap you can't see into. Any inaccessible areas should be considered with care. The same applies to your power supply, too. You don't want to open that up, as its internal components can retain dangerous electrical charges, although you can clear surface dust from its external fan grill without reaching inside.

If you're lucky, your PC might have removable dust filters that you can clean separately. These might sit in front of your case fans as a way to block some of the dust entering. If you're able to remove them, pull them out and give them a thorough clean. A damp cloth should work, but keep moisture away from any circuit boards and let the filters dry completely before you reinstall them. You can use a dry microfiber cloth to wipe down the outside of the case, too.

When you're done, double-check everything inside. Internal cables should be securely connected, with none trailing into the fan blades. Once your case is clean, you'll be in a better position to keep it that way. Keep it off the floor if you can, too, as it'll help to reduce the amount of dust you're drawing in through your fans. You can't stop dust completely, but you can keep it manageable enough, with occasional checks and cleans in place, before it starts clogging up your vents again.

Can a TV be too large? An investigation

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For the past couple of years, I’ve wanted to upgrade my home entertainment situation from the humble trappings of my long sufficient 55-inch TCL smart TV to the model much coveted by cinephiles the world over: the OLED.

The organic light-emitting diode is a specific monitor technology that has really changed the TV industry. Unlike an LED TV, which creates images by shining a backlight through a panel of pixels, OLEDs leverage millions of self-lighting pixels, which provides significantly better pixel control, and can generate a much more nuanced image with deeply vibrant colors and a more intense light and shadows.

They are also significantly more expensive, which is one reason that I’ve held off on investing in one for years. However, this year I couldn’t wait any longer. I finally sprang for an upgrade and, since I was already upgrading on image quality, I decided to upgrade the size, too: I bought a 65-inch LG evo C4, which is the company’s mid-range unit released in 2024.

At first, the experience was amazing. The television rendered colors and shadows wonderfully and, seeing as one of the goals with this purchase was to indulge my physical media habit, I began running through my collection of 4K Blu-ray discs with the madcap energy of a true fanatic.

However, things took a bit of an unexpected turn when, midway through “McCabe and Mrs. Miller,” I was suddenly overcome by a wave of nausea. I’m not particularly sensitive to motion sickness, but this sensation — which made it feel vaguely like I was going to hurl — persisted for several hours after it initially began. It was then replaced by a small tension headache.

I discovered that this is actually a somewhat common experience for new OLED adopters. Indeed, Reddit is rife with posts from new television owners who claim they feel like their big new toy is breaking their brain.

“I got the tv yesterday and still feel sick today,” one Redditor, who said they had recently purchased an LG C2, wrote. “Feel light headed and nauseous a day later. Thinking about taking it back. Is this normal? I’m scared to turn the thing on lol.”

Others blamed the sheer size of the screen. One guy, who bought an 85-inch TLC, claimed it had led his wife to the conclusion that “it’s too big and say that she feels sick watching. Is that a thing that a screen can be too big?”

There doesn’t seem to be a consensus on why this happens. Many Redditors blame certain motion-smoothing settings. However, my TV did not come with those settings enabled. Others blamed viewers for sitting too close to the screen — yet I was sitting a solid nine feet away, which is well within the recommended range for a 65-inch screen.

Finally, others have argued that the screen brightness could be agitating users’ eyes. I dialed down the brightness on mine to around 50 percent, although I had doubts as to whether this actually had anything to do with the feelings I was having.

In considering my nausea, one theory that sprang to mind was that it might just be my eyes’ inexperience with viewing such a big screen. The TV was so large that it often felt like my eye had to trace different parts of the screen to take in the whole picture and, if the scene involved significant motion (most movie scenes do, after all), it conjured the sensation of trying to stare too intently at something out the window of a moving car.

After a few days, however, my eyes finally adjusted. The nausea was gone. All that remained was an immense and beautiful home viewing experience that made every previous viewing experience pale by comparison.

Initial physiological reactions aside, the size of the 65-inch screen (and whether your living space actually has room for it) is still something that needs to be considered carefully by potential buyers. I recently moved into a place with a slightly bigger living room, which has given me the flexibility to install a bigger device. At my old apartment, however, this size of TV would have definitely felt like it was devouring the space.

Another slight frustration is that the C4 does not come with any detailed installation instructions. The screen (which is so wide it’s difficult to move without another person there to help you) comes detached from its base, and the user is just sort of expected to figure out how to effectively move and put together the two pieces. A quick YouTube video is all that is necessary to understand this process, but you have to be careful when moving and assembling the pieces so as not to scratch or damage the screen.

Once the whole thing is set up and assembled, the OLED is a truly beautiful thing to behold. The customization that’s possible, including advanced brightness and color control — and things like “Filmmaker Mode” (which claims to adjust each movie’s color and frame rate settings to those intended by the director) — make the user experience really powerful.

To sum up: The evo C4 is a really spectacular piece of equipment that has a lot to offer the avid home entertainment viewer — although there may be a bit of a learning curve before it makes your cinema dreams come true.
