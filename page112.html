Anker Soundcore Space 2 Pro review: Big bang for less bucks

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The company’s latest headphones rival Apple, Bose and Sony at a much lower price.

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

If you don't like the idea of paying $350 to $500 for a set of flagship headphones, Anker's Soundcore line offers a lot of performance at much lower prices. Over the years, the company has refined its formula by balancing sound and features in various packages so as to compete with the likes of Apple, Bose, Sony and Sennheiser. With the Space 2 Pro, Anker put its new Thus audio chip in headphones for the first time, making lofty claims about the upgraded voice clarity as a result. At $200, these aren't just an affordable alternative to the big names in headphones, they're a worthy contender in their own right.

The first thing that stood out to me about the Space 2 Pro is the design. These do not look like they cost $200, especially not the grey version that I tested. Anker refined the look of the Space line here for a premium aesthetic and a build that's lightweight, but not overly plastic. The sliders for fit adjustments are metal and both the headband and ear pads are wrapped in soft leather. There are also subtle metallic accents to complete the premium design, like the circles on the outside of the ear cups and subtly placed product name.

Even though the Space 2 Pro is the flagship Soundcore headphones, the price tag pits them up against decidedly midrange competition. Design-wise, the Space 2 Pro is vastly superior in that category, and that advantage continues into audio performance. These headphones have more bass and overall clarity than you'll typically find in the $200 range. The 35mm drivers are made with two different materials in order to maximize both high-end clarity and deep bass.

When I listened to Mastodon's Marrow Deep, Brann Dailor's drums boom throughout and Troy Sanders' bass provides the backbone for the guitar riffs. Thanks to Anker's "crystalline highs" here, guitars and synths cut through the mix clearly, so you never miss a note of Bill Kelliher's handiwork. The overall clarity and detail on the Space 2 Pro is really on display with more acoustic-driven genres, like Zach Bryan's Young Manhood and Other Donatable Organs. The piano, acoustic guitar and string section seem to float behind the interwoven vocals of Bryan and Lana Del Rey on "Lies," for example.

Anker is very proud that the Space 2 Pro currently holds the Guiness World Record for the "clearest over-ear headphones for calls." I was immediately skeptical that this would hold any weight in real-world use, but boy was I wrong.

Plenty of headphones manage to nullify background noise, but they do so at great expense to voice clarity. But thanks to that Thus chip and a six-microphone array, the Space 2 Pro offers extremely clear voice performance while effectively blocking background noise. Not only do they combat would-be distractions exceptionally well, they do so without making you sound even the slightest bit robotic. I thought I sounded pretty close to how I did in a quiet room when the processing kicked in, maybe only the slightest bit muffled, but never like a digitized version of myself.

The company promises battery life of up to 30 hours when you have active noise cancellation (ANC) on, or up to 60 hours with it off. During my testing, which I conducted almost entirely with noise cancellation enabled, I got 31 hours before the Space 2 Pro shut off. I did take a call or two a day where I needed to switch to transparency mode so I didn't feel the need to shout to hear myself speaking, but I'd say 95 percent of the time, I was using ANC.

The most disappointing aspect of the Space 2 Pro to me are a number of subpar audio features. The Thus chip powers what Anker calls Hear ID 5.0, a feature that uses a hearing test to customize the sound of the headphones to your preferences. I didn't think the resulting tailored audio profile offered any meaningful improvement over the stock tuning, neither did spatial audio settings or the collection of curated presets. I tested all of them, of course, but every time I used one, I found myself longing for the out-of-the-box sound after just a few minutes of use.

Anker boasts Adaptive ANC 3.0 on the Space 2 Pro, a noise-canceling setup that relies on the Thus chip and eight microphones to combat background noise. The company specifically lists "blocking office chatter and keyboard clicks for instant silence" among the headphones' strengths. While I do think the Space 2 Pro does an above average job of lowering the volume on human voices, and it definitely blocks keyboard clicks, its general noise canceling abilities are just okay. The headphones struggled with constant noise sources, like white noise machines and particularly raucous fans.

On Anker's flagship earbuds, the Liberty 5 Pro Max, the company built an AI note taking assistant into the charging case (which has a touchscreen). Even though the Space 2 Pro doesn't have a case, it does have plenty of microphones, and "AI Note-Taker" is currently listed as "TBD" on the spec sheet. If that feature does eventually arrive, there are two potential issues with it.

First, it will be a lot more awkward to wear over-ear headphones in a meeting than it would be to slide a small earbuds case to the middle of the table. Second, if you plan to regularly use Anker's transcription service and cloud storage, you'll have to pay for a subscription. You only get two hours of transcriptions and 10,000 minutes of audio storage monthly for free, and only for two years. Plans are then either $8.33 or $20 per month (billed annually) depending on your needs. Since it's unclear if this feature will ever arrive, its absence doesn't impact my overall assessment.

It's easy to dismiss Soundcore as merely a budget brand, but that would be a mistake. With products like the Space 2 Pro, Anker proves it can hang with the best, offering comparable performance for significantly less money. In fact, I'd argue if voice clarity is your primary factor in choosing a new set of headphones, this model should be your first choice. For many, though, that's further down the list of priorities.

While there are caveats when it comes to ANC performance, audio features and a (potentially) unnecessary note taker, the Space 2 Pro has a lot of premium traits. The overall sound quality is good, the design is refined, the fit is comfortable and battery life claims hold true. At a time when the best overall headphones you can buy are $460 at full price, Anker's ability to produce a $200 alternative that won't disappoint you is no small feat.

Sovereign AI: What Changes When The Cloud Is No Longer Enough

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Karan Alang, principal software engineer at Versa Networks with 25 years of experience in AI, cloud and big data.

For most companies, cloud computing is a sensible way to start developing AI systems. On-demand resources and managed services make it easier to build data pipelines, try use cases and scale as adoption increases.

However, public clouds don’t always satisfy the legal, operational and security requirements of a sovereign environment.

Gartner defines sovereignty as an organization’s ability to control its data, technology and digital operations. It’s “about who operates and supports your IT systems.” This includes control over provider operations, personnel and access paths, not just where the data is located.​

This is becoming increasingly important in the AI/ML age, especially for regulated and security-sensitive organizations. Financial institutions face privacy, record-keeping and jurisdictional requirements; healthcare systems must protect sensitive workloads, and government agencies may require FedRAMP-authorized or fully air-gapped environments​.

AI makes this complex because data is no longer confined to traditional databases and applications. It can flow through training pipelines, vector databases and inference systems. With agentic AI, autonomous agents may also access and act on it. Organizations therefore need to understand not only where the data resides but where it’s processed, and what systems and agents access it.​​

Sovereignty can be achieved on the cloud as well as an air-gapped on-premise environment. A sovereign cloud environment provides a middle ground between using a cloud-based environment and an air-gapped sovereign environment. The comparison below shows how sovereign cloud and on-premise environments satisfy sovereignty.​

• Sovereign Cloud: High, depending on the provider and region. Data can be constrained to an approved country or jurisdiction, with regional storage or processing and controls over data movement.

• On-Premise: Very high. Data is stored within the organization’s air-gapped controlled environment.

• Sovereign Cloud: Partial to high. The customer has significant controls, but the cloud provider still operates parts of the underlying infrastructure. Support personnel, privileged access, telemetry and management-plane dependencies need be evaluated.

• On-Premise: Very high. The customer controls administrators, physical infrastructure, networking, patching, deployment, monitoring, keys and access.

• Sovereign Cloud: Partial. The customer generally remains dependent on the provider’s hardware, cloud platform, APIs, managed services and service roadmap. For example, GPU availability on the cloud might be restricted to specific regions.

• On-Premise: Potentially very high, but not absolute. The customer selects and operates its compute, storage, networking, Kubernetes/platform stack, models and inference environment. However, dependencies can remain on GPU vendors, commercial software, firmware and externally developed models.​

The sovereign cloud provides on-demand provisioning while satisfying the sovereignty requirement of many organizations. But for highly sensitive data and AI workloads, some organizations require stronger isolation and operational controls provided by air-gapped environments.

Air-gapped sovereign AI has traditionally been cost-prohibitive and difficult to manage. However, technological changes have made air-gapped AI more practical.

• AI compute is increasingly accessible outside hyperscale data centers. Enterprise servers with modern GPUs now support meaningful inference workloads inside a customer-controlled environment.

• Local LLM inference has matured. Open-weight models can run on customer hardware without organizations having to send sensitive data to an external model API. Additionally, quantization techniques help reduce model size to run efficiently on enterprise server GPUs.

• The open-source infrastructure stack is production-ready and portable. Technologies like Kubernetes, Kafka, Airflow and Spark run across cloud and on-premise environments, bringing cloud-native principles to sovereign environments.

I’ve spent the last few years building cloud-native data platforms for network and security intelligence. We started with the cloud, which was the right starting point. It let us move quickly, iterate and scale. However, for some customers, an air-gapped sovereign AI solution is the correct one.

Moving the platform into an air-gapped environment taught us that sovereign AI isn’t simply cloud architecture deployed on-premises. The operational considerations and requirements change significantly.​

There are several key points to be considered while formulating an enterprise AI strategy and in the design/implementation of an air-gapped sovereign AI solution.

• Capacity is now finite. Every microservice and Kubernetes workload must be right-sized upfront—CPU limits, memory requests, persistent and ephemeral storage, and GPU allocation. The elasticity of the cloud isn’t available in an air-gapped on-premise environment; hence, the size needs to account for the peak loads.

• The upfront infrastructure cost may be significant and needs to be calculated appropriately. The lag between the hardware procurement and delivery must be factored into capacity planning.

• Observability is a key part of the design. The pre-built metrics visualizations for managed services like Composer, Mongo Atlas and Dataproc needs to be recreated.

• CI/CD must be redesigned for the air-gapped environment. Internal artifact registries need to be sized and set up to securely store and distribute docker images and other software artifacts.

• Support teams need to be equipped to handle infrastructure and product issues. The cost estimate needs to include 24/7 support.

• Model and hardware selection must match the workload. NetOps and SecOps environments combine high-volume structured telemetry, real-time processing and language-based investigation. Israel has exploited Palestinian divisions, citing the absence of a unified Palestinian political entity as a reason it could not move forward on peace. Most Palestinians have relentlessly called for unity among Palestinian factions. Dahlan’s party, the Democratic Reform Bloc (DRC) is now trying to achieve that elusive aim. However, including Hamas in any political alliance risks rattling Israel, which is suspected of deploying myriad tools at its disposal to influence Palestinian politics.

Born in Khan Younis, the same town as former Hamas leader Yahya Sinwar, who plotted the attack against Israel on Oct. 7, 2023, Dahlan is seen as a son of the soil. He learned the ropes from Yasser Arafat, the architect of the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO), when Israel deported him to Jordan. In 1994, as the Oslo Accords were set in motion, Abbas appointed Dahlan to manage the enclave’s security as head of the Palestinian Authority’s (PA) Preventive Security organization. His reputation and reach soon earned him a reputation, and Gaza was often referred to as “Dahlistan.”

Dahlan dealt with Israeli intelligence officials and quickly made a name for himself as someone who anyone could work with. He fought against Hamas in 2006, but it was Abbas who later exiled him on corruption charges. Abbas was wary of Dahlan’s ambition and access, fearing that he might be a fierce competitor one day.

Dahlan relocated to the UAE and became a security advisor to the ruler of Abu Dhabi, Mohammed bin Zayed. He was close to the sheikh when the Abraham Accords were signed and during the military escalation in Gaza following Oct. 7, when the UAE maintained its diplomatic commitment and didn’t walk out of the deal. His proximity to Abu Dhabi’s leadership made him appealing to Israel. Could he be a Trojan horse in the Palestinian movement?

“Many Israeli intelligence officials, even those close to [Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin] Netanyahu, have met him,” said Eran Lerman, a former Israeli deputy national security advisor and retired intelligence colonel who serves as vice president of the Jerusalem Institute for Strategy and Security.

Dahlan played an instrumental role in convincing Hamas to decommission its weapons during talks with U.S. President Donald Trump’s Board of Peace—although the fate of that proposal hangs in balance as Israel demands full disarmament. Muhammad Shehada, a visiting fellow with the European Council on Foreign Relations, said that Dahlan was brought into the negotiations to build trust with Israel.

Israel has signed peace treaties with both Egypt and the UAE, and it sees them as firm opponents of the Muslim Brotherhood, a pan-Arab movement that Israel views as an ideological fountainhead of Hamas. Dahlan maintains open channels with both Abu Dhabi and Cairo, and those relationships make him an interesting figure for Israeli strategists.

In a 2024 interview with the New York Times, Dahlan laid out his vision for Palestine, declaring, “No Abbas, no Hamas,” and calling for a new generation to take charge of the PA.

Dahlan has funneled Emirati funds into Gaza, helping him revive his standing and expand his reach. He has maintained a political base through the DRC.

Dimitri Diliani, a DRC spokesperson, said that Dahlan was meeting with former Hamas chief Ismail Haniyeh in Doha when the Times article was published, yet Hamas raised no objections. “That was the first sign that Hamas was willing to give up control of Gaza,” Diliani said. “But neither the U.S. nor Israel took notice.”

Hamas might accept a relegated status in Palestinian governance, but observers have questioned whether there is more than meets the eye. Some have argued that Dahlan is throwing Hamas a lifeline, ostensibly to challenge Abbas but also allowing it to survive another day as a political entity.

The DRC has formed an alliance comprising Hamas, Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and Fatah breakaway factions to support a unified electoral list of independent candidates to compete against Abbas. “Hamas agreed they will not participate directly and will support whatever list we announce,” Diliani said. “It will be a national list that represents us, but none of our political figures will be in it.”

That offers little comfort to Israel, which demands Hamas’s complete exit from Gaza rather than political influence by proxy. A former senior Israeli security official, who previously spoke highly of Dahlan as someone who could be acceptable to Israel to lead Gaza, now warned about his motives. The official described him as “a thief” who “can play both sides.”

Is Dahlan playing Israel, or is there a larger strategy at play behind the scenes? When asked whether he could convince Israelis to discuss a two-state solution, Dahlan struck a harsh tone. “It’s not my job to convince the Israelis,” he told the Times. “Let them go to hell.”

Adopting a firm stance against Israel remains a political necessity for any Palestinian leader—lest they be labeled as a puppet. Yet Dahlan has positioned himself as a rare figure capable of liaising between Israel, the United States, and Hamas.

Dahlan maintains he has no desire to officially run the Palestinian territories but that is standard political speak to hide real intentions. However, Lerman noted that with business ventures spanning Belgrade and beyond, Dahlan is financially well-off and may have little interest in returning to Gaza and enduring the privations that come with it.

Israel is also running out of options. It is unhappy with Abbas and sees him as ineffective in managing armed resistance, and it has called on the European Union to pressure Abbas to reform the PA and link European aid to reforms. A different version of PA reforms has been discussed in Palestinian circles. They wish to downgrade the PA, which was formed as part of the Oslo process to pave the path for a Palestinian state, and revive the PLO, which is the umbrella movement for self-determination. Palestinians argue that they didn’t get a state; instead, the PA has eroded the movement. In this vision, the PLO includes Hamas.

Diliani said that it was pointless to seek Hamas’s decimation. Even if Hamas gives up weapons, another armed rebellion will rise. “As long as Israel occupies Palestinian lands, resistance will persist in one form or another,” he said. “Dahlan has an inclusive vision.”

Israel may have to choose between Dahlan’s broader alliance that includes Hamas as a political actor or backing Abbas and his familiar operatives—such as Hussein al-Sheikh, the PA’s second-in-command, or Majed Faraj, the PA’s intelligence chief who recently called on Hamas to apologize for Oct. 7. Dahlan has a year to navigate these competing pressures and decide if he wants to jettison Hamas or risk being outmaneuvered by Israel.

World of Warcraft: Forever introduces a two-part naming system, giving each character a main name and a secondary name. Rather than being unique to an individual realm, your full name must now be unique across your region.

This means you can still use a main name another player has already chosen, as long as the full two-part combination is available. But maybe you picked a random secondary name because you couldn't wait to see how your favorite starting area has changed since 2004. Luckily, you can control how that secondary name appears in-game.

Your secondary name appears above your character's head, in your character's portrait frame, and above your pet or minion. You can hide it from these UI elements if you'd prefer to see only your main name.

After unchecking the box, your secondary name will disappear from the affected displays immediately, without needing to restart the game. If you change your mind, return to the same menu and check the box again to restore it.

However, keep a couple of things in mind.

Your full character name will still appear in chat, even with the setting disabled. The setting also carries over between characters and rulesets. If you want to hide your secondary name on one character but show it on another, you'll need to change the setting each time you swap between your characters.
