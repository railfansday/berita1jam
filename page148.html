Perplexity Cuts Agent API Research Costs and Adds Ultra Effort

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Perplexity announced on November 5, 2026 that every Agent API effort level now costs less per query, and reported higher or unchanged research quality across the presets. Medium improved from 55% to 77% on BrowseComp and DSQA while its cost fell almost 90%, and a new ultra level is available for broad research.

Effort levels package model choice, research-step and tool-call limits, search behavior and available tools into named presets. They let developers select a balance of cost, speed and research depth without configuring each component separately. A request such as effort="fast" receives the revised setup automatically.

At xhigh, Perplexity says the WANDR score held at 76.5% while the configuration ran at about 25% of its August 2026 cost. That level is intended for open-ended agentic work, including executing code in a sandbox, sustaining long tool-use loops and gathering evidence across many sources.

The WANDR paper describes a 500-task evaluation set built around realistic data-collection work. Agents must discover qualifying entities and return records whose sources and excerpts can be checked independently. Task-specific judges retrieve the cited pages again and assess the records against that evidence, avoiding a static gold answer set.

Ultra joins fast, low, medium, high and xhigh in the official effort documentation. Its designated role is broad research and data collection where completeness matters more than latency. The configuration uses anthropic/claude-opus-5-5, and background mode is available for long runs.

Ultra assigns the same research problem to three agents running in parallel, allowing each to assemble evidence independently. A fourth agent consolidates their outputs, removes duplicate findings and writes the answer. Perplexity reports an 85.9% WANDR score at $21.71 per task, the highest result among the configurations it tested.

Spending is constrained at each level through limits on steps, a ceiling on total tool calls and periodic notices about remaining capacity. The separate tool-call ceiling matters because one step can launch several calls across different tools. Those notices help the agent reserve enough budget to check its evidence and deliver an answer instead of stopping mid-task.

One experiment with openai/gpt-6-luna shows why those limits matter. Perplexity reports that moving from 1 to 15 permitted steps raised accuracy on BrowseComp and DSQA from 41% to 80%, while cost per query increased from $0.0017 to $0.0152. Budget allocation therefore shapes both the quality of the answer and the price of obtaining it.

Context compaction addresses another cost driver. After context passes a threshold, the system condenses earlier work into a summary of findings and remaining tasks while preserving the system prompt, original request and any loaded skills. It then tells the agent to keep working and reports the number of compactions, so the summary is not mistaken for a stopping point.

Extraction reduces what the agent carries forward. Instead of giving it an entire page, fetch_url asks a smaller model to select passages relevant to the current task and quote them verbatim for citation. At high effort, Perplexity reports approximately 50% fewer input tokens per query and an 88.0% result at $0.073 per query, compared with 83.5% at $0.094 without extraction.

Related changes target search itself. Each search can now return up to 4K tokens of results rather than 2K, a new snippet engine selects text more closely matched to the query, and Fast Search is priced at $1.00 per 1,000 requests. Together, these changes are intended to locate useful evidence with fewer searches and less irrelevant context.

Workflow selection uses a skill tree with progressive disclosure. A skill initially occupies only its name and description in context; the full instructions arrive after the model decides that the workflow fits the task. Perplexity says preloading every skill increased BrowseComp and DSQA costs by 13% and reduced WANDR scores, supporting the decision to load workflows only when needed.

An automated harness evaluates newly available models as they become accessible, sweeping reasoning effort, step and tool-call budgets, and tool configurations. A candidate displaces the incumbent only if it delivers better quality at equal or lower cost. Perplexity says one candidate scored 13.3 points above the baseline on BrowseComp but fell below baselines on internal enterprise, legal and finance research tasks, illustrating why public benchmark results alone do not determine selection.

The documentation distinguishes dynamic names, pinned versions and frozen configurations. Dynamic calls inherit later evaluated changes without code edits; pinned calls retain one release after the active configuration advances; frozen calls copy public fields but can miss settings outside the documented request schema. Ultra has no frozen configuration and must be called by name.

For developers, the practical distinction is automatic updates versus reproducibility: dynamic levels inherit Perplexity’s evaluated revisions, while pinned versions preserve a known configuration. Ultra extends that system to broader coverage, and its four-agent design makes orchestration part of the cost-quality calculation alongside model selection.

"Right now, investors are questioning that $1.2 trillion of capex that we forecast for next year," Peter Boockvar, chief investment officer at One Point BFG Wealth Partners, told CNBC. "Well, maybe that's not going to happen."

Boockvar believes this could be the start of the end of the AI trade as we know it.

Whether it's completely over or not, investors need to be reassessing how they're planning to play the AI trade going forward, the analysts said.

The iShares Semiconductor ETF dropped 11% on Thursday, its biggest decline since during the Covid breakout in March 2020. The Global X Artificial Intelligence & Technology ETF (AIQ) is down 6% on the day and down 15% over the past month.

"There has been a huge change in the outlook for AI infrastructure buildout," Eric Johnston, head of equities at Cantor Fitzgerald, told CNBC. "The entire economy and the consumer and earnings growth are all levered to that buildout."

The selling Thursday spilled over to non-AI stocks with the Dow Jones Industrial average down more than 1,600 points.

Josh Brown pointed to the 4.8% yield for the 10-year Treasury yield, saying a compelling risk-free rate is causing investors to reconsider taking the risk in these volatile AI stocks.

"You don't need to be at 80% U.S. stocks and have 60% of that exposure be tech," Brown said.

Many investors believe the AI trade isn't over completely, investors just need to get more selective.

"The AI buildout just got harder," said Rob Sechan, Newedge Capital Group Founder & Managing Partner:

"Nobody voted to make compute less scarce," Sechan said, citing Microsoft's Azure cloud business growing 48% year over year in its most recent third quarter. That level of growth suggests its capacity constrained, not demand constrained, he said.

"When new capacity gets harder to build, existing capacity becomes more valuable," Sechan said.

Richard Clode, portfolio manager at Janus Henderson, noted a few bright spots in tech on Thursday despite the highly correlated downward movements in the big tech indices.

"Motorola Solutions had some good results," he said. "We're seeing a little bit of rotation into some of the … internet names...Sony reported some good results, and again they were up strongly overnight."

"So there are some brief pockets of respite from this, but obviously pretty painful across the board today," he said.

Some experts believe the selling was overdone and investors could start nibbling.

This "indiscriminate selling" is a "buying opportunity," said Larry Adam, Raymond James chief investment officer. If investors "take a longer term perspective, which is what we do, I think the valuations at today's levels will tend to prove to be a good buying opportunity," he said, urging investors to look at the fundamentals.

Victoria Greene, chief investment officer of G Squared Private Wealth, said she saw some strong supports around the S&P 500's 200-day moving average and was on the lookout for a market bounce following Thursday's rout.

"Typically, the average return is plus about 180 basis points the next day," she said. "The question we have to ask ourselves: Is this a pullback in an uptrend, which is what I believe? Or is this the end-all, be-all [and] the wheels are coming off and everybody just needs to buy T-bills? I don't think we're there yet."

Google, Meta and TikTok are all suing California to block social media law intended to help kids

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Assembly Bill 1709 demands that platforms remove "psychologically exploitative features."

Google, Meta and TikTok are suing to block a recent California law that's intended to protect kids from some of the more harmful aspects of social media, according to Reuters. Assembly Bill 1709 is part of a larger package that Governor Gavin Newsom introduced earlier this year and it prohibits social media platforms from offering underage kids "psychologically exploitative features" that could lead to compulsive use.

This includes stuff like sending videos through autoplay and giving children under 16 personalized recommendations. The three social media giants say this is in violation of the First Amendment and Section 230 of the federal Communications Decency Act, which has often been used to shield tech companies from liability.

The three companies filed separate suits at various courts throughout California. "The state cannot, consistent with the First Amendment, override Meta's editorial judgments," Meta wrote in its complaint. Google and TikTok made similar arguments.

Newsom said when he signed the law that children's safety should always come first when considering social media platforms in California. The state "will not stand by while unregulated technology puts our children at risk," he noted.

Some organizations agree with the social media companies here. The nonprofit Electronic Frontier Foundation has called the law "a massive privacy and free speech nightmare" and unsuccessfully urged Newsom to veto the policy.

While many disagree on the potential solution, there's not much of an argument to be made regarding the severity of the problem. TikTok, Meta and Google are currently fighting thousands of lawsuits by individuals, school districts, cities and government entities that all claim that social media platforms addict children and have caused a mental health crisis. To that end, many states regulate children's access to social media.

Also, Meta recently agreed to pay up to $18 billion to settle a high-profile case over children's privacy and social media addiction. As part of this agreement, the company says it will make changes to its platforms to make them less addictive to young users. It still denies any wrongdoing.

Court leans toward narrow ruling on inmate’s use of habeas to challenge accelerated transfer to a halfway house or home confinement

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court heard oral argument on Nov. 2 in Maxwell v. Dinis about whether a federal inmate may use a habeas statute, 28 U.S.C. § 2241, to contest the Federal Bureau of Prisons’ refusal to accelerate his transfer to a halfway house or home confinement.

Maxwell, a former attorney, was convicted at trial for his role in conspiring with a mob-related organization, La Costa Nostra, to take over a Texas-based mortgage-loan company, FirstPlus Financial Group, Inc., and drain it of its assets. As Maxwell neared the end of his sentence, he sought to contest the BOP’s calculation of good-time credits that would accelerate his transfer from prison to a halfway house or home confinement. The question for the court was whether Section 2241, which does not clearly spell out what claims may be brought but whose scope has largely been shaped by precedent, allows him to do so.

Maxwell’s attorney, Masha Hansford, pressed for a broad rule that habeas is available to an inmate like Maxwell who is asserting a right to a “distinct and lower level of custody.” But that position almost immediately encountered some pushback. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson questioned “whether we really need to use this case to define every circumstance in which habeas is appropriate.” Hansford agreed that the court need not adopt a test that allows inmates to challenge all changes to the level of custody and was content to have the court resolve Maxwell’s case by applying a narrower test endorsed by the government that would permit an inmate to use habeas to challenge the denial of an accelerated transfer to parole or, as here, a halfway house or home confinement.

Justice Samuel Alito pressed Hansford on whether an inmate may use Section 2241 simply to dispute how BOP calculated good-time credits or whether the inmate must also show that any error in calculating would also entitle the inmate to accelerated transfer to a halfway house or home confinement. After Hansford replied that Maxwell met all the requirements for the transfer and only the calculation of good-time credits was at issue, Justice Neal Gorsuch followed up by pressing whether the court needs to say that an inmate could bring a habeas challenge about the calculation of good-time credits alone. Hansford replied that the court did not need to say a dispute over good-time credits alone would suffice for a federal inmate to bring a claim in Section 2241.

Turning to Hansford’s level-of-custody test, Justice Sonia Sotomayor noted that, in a prior case, Justice Antonin Scalia had referred to habeas as covering a “‘quantum change’ in the level of custody,” but Hansford was asking the court “to create new language, ‘materially less restrictive custody.’ Why don’t we stick to what we have?” Hansford agreed that the court need not broaden its test and that Maxwell’s requested transfer to a halfway house or home confinement amounted to such a quantum change.

Assistant to the Solicitor General Kevin J. Barber, arguing for the government, contended that “there’s no need to adopt” Maxwell’s “amorphous level-of-custody rule.” He explained that the court “has always made clear that habeas is available when your claim is seeking discharge from actual physical confinement in prison to a different kind of custody,” such as a halfway house or home confinement. Here, the government relied heavily on Preiser v. Rodriguez, which held that habeas supplies the sole avenue for a state inmate to challenge the denial of credit toward release on parole.

When Gorsuch pressed Barber on the source of the government’s rule and why an inmate’s transfer to solitary confinement or maximum security would not be covered, Barber pointed principally to history and tradition in the court’s habeas cases. Barber explained, “we don’t have any tradition of claims being recognized as habeas claims challenging just transfers within prison systems.”

Despite agreeing that Maxwell could bring his claim in Section 2241, the government argued that no court could actually grant him relief. Specifically, the government pointed to 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) and its terms barring courts from reviewing “a designation of a place of imprisonment under this subsection.” According to the government, service of a sentence in a halfway house or home confinement is “a designation of a place of imprisonment” governed by that statute.

But the government did not appear to have the votes to prevail on this argument, at least at the Supreme Court. Justice Brett Kavanaugh pressed Barber on whether that issue could be left for the lower court to decide on remand. Barber conceded that the court “absolutely could.” Sotomayor questioned Barber about whether the government is right that Section 3621(b) applies to Maxwell’s claim. But after Barber began responding, Justice Amy Coney Barrett reiterated Kavanaugh’s point that “we don’t have to get into any of this, right?” Barber again agreed.

Jackson pressed Barber on a separate point, that the BOP is expected to transfer Maxwell to a halfway house or home confinement in January 2027, which would soon make his claim moot. Jackson disputed why the court would revisit its decision to hear the case. Barber apologized for not pressing the Section 3621(b) issue or mootness in opposing certiorari, but said these factors spoke in favor of the court dismissing the case as improvidently granted.

The amicus, Brian W. Barnes, was appointed to argue in favor of the ruling by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit that habeas is unavailable for Maxwell’s claim. When pressed by Justice Elena Kagan, Barnes agreed that habeas is available to “encompass a move from incarceration to parole,” as Preiser held. Kagan pointed out that “parole looks an awful lot like what’s going on here.”

The justices’ questions suggest that they are unlikely to limit Preiser and reject an analogy between Maxwell’s requested relief and the parole in that case. The justices also showed little appetite for reaching the government’s Section 3621(b) argument. How they would handle Maxwell’s expected release to a halfway house in January 2027 was less clear, but that circumstance could result in a speedier ruling.

Melania Trump hailed the mission as a success, taking to social media to thank Kyiv and Moscow for their "leadership and cooperation." She said the Oleshky ceasefire could go on to inspire a "lasting peace between the two peoples."

Dmytro Lubinez similarly wrote on the Telegram messaging platform that it is "important to maintain this cooperation mechanism," adding that "we are currently exploring options with Yana Lantratova to continue similar humanitarian efforts."

The Kremlin, however, was quick to dampen any such expectations, making clear that no further plans for humanitarian missions exist at this stage.

British broadcaster Sky News aired drone footage, produced with the support of the Ukrainian army, showing the Russian army transporting heavy equipment, weapons, personnel and food to Oleshky and neighboring villages.

Frozen really didn't start coming together until Christopher Buck, who directed Tarzan, came aboard in 2008, but the film wasn't commissioned until 2011 following Tangled's box office success.

2. The movie was going to be titled The Snow Queen after the original fairy tale, but the filmmakers decided to change it to Frozen "because, to us, it represents the movie. Alex Karpovsky on Returning to Filmmaking (and Maybe ‘Girls’) After More Than a Decade Away

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The actor and director is making the indie festival rounds with his wild jungle comedy ‘Riptides,’ while also reflecting on his ‘Girls’ experience ahead of the revival: “Will I be in it? Who knows!”

Even if Alex Karpovsky was no more familiar to Girls viewers than the rest of its breakout cast, the Massachusetts native was already relatively established by the time of the HBO show’s premiere — only, behind the camera. He’d directed three micro-budgeted features from 2006 to 2009, and another two that released in 2012, the same year as Girls’ first-season run. The work showcased an idiosyncratic filmmaker freely following his heady, sometimes absurdist instincts.

Then Girls became what it became, and Karpovsky suddenly had an acting career coming off of his deadpan turn as Ray Ploshansky: Now 51, he’s gone on to star in Homecoming, Nobody Wants This, Angelyne and multiple Coen brothers movies. He’d tried to get other projects off the ground over the last decade-plus, including several TV ideas that got stuck in limbo, but he also acknowledges that time was an issue — he simply didn’t have the “mojo” to get another movie going. Related Stories TV Sharon Horgan Comedy 'Youth' Renewed for Second Season at HBO TV 'The Birds' Series With Sarah Snook in the Works at HBO

It feels fitting, then, that Karpovsky returned with his first film in 14 years less than two weeks before Lena Dunham’s announcement that Girls would be coming back as a five-episode limited-series revival. Karpovsky hasn’t been announced to be part of it — he won’t confirm or deny in our interview, but he’s open about imagining Ray’s future and his experience making the show — but there’s a full-circle quality to this arc nonetheless. The world, and the industry, have changed quite a bit, but Girls is back, and Karpovsky is back making movies.

The film in question is Riptides, and it is again very low-budget, playful with its humor and dense with ideas. Set in Sayulita, Mexico, where Karpovsky visits regularly, the movie imagines the collision between Toby (Steve Little), an American beach bum who oversees hotel properties in the idyllic town; Paul (Dylan Baker), a renowned self-help author sneaking in a quick vacation before his next big book tour; and Paul’s daughter, Michelle (Bojana Novakovic), an even-keeled manager who spends most of her time keeping her famous father out of trouble.

Tragedy strikes, and an opportunity arises for Toby to make something of himself and find some meaning, inspired by Paul’s writings. He winds up entangled with Michelle in this scheme, going down a “demented” path as Karpovsky puts it in our conversation.

Riptides next screens at the Indie Memphis Festival on Saturday evening. So far, its path is not dissimilar from the low-fi projects that Karpovsky helmed back in the late aughts and early 2010s, despite drastic changes in the surrounding independent landscape. We get into all of that and more.

You premiered about a month ago at New/Next Film Festival. How did it go?

It went really well. I mean, for me and my disposition, it’s terrifying to show this. The theater had 300 people in it. I know six people in the theater. It’s mostly strangers and you just never know how it’s going to go. Perhaps this is obvious, but the thing about a comedy is that everyone knows in real time whether or not the movie is working or not working — a binary thumbs up, thumbs down feeling — at least for me. You’re in the room and then you have to go up there and do a Q&A. But luckily everything played as I wanted it to…. One little test you can have of how a movie plays at film festivals: Do people stay for the Q&A, quite simply? And they did, and they asked good questions. So I was very happy — perhaps, more than happy, relieved that the movie didn’t fall apart.

It’s been 14 years since your last feature came out, the same year that Girls premiered — so the timing there is interesting. What do you attribute the gap to?

I made five movies before this one and they were all really ultra, ultra low-budget affairs. Two of them came out in the same year — 2012, I guess, Rubberneck and Red Flag — and we were in the middle of shooting Girls. It was fun and exhilarating, but really exhausting. Making any movie is exhausting. But making ultra low-budget movies, because you’re wearing so many hats, it exhausts you in a very specific way. I felt very depleted and I didn’t really have the desire to make another movie, at least a low-budget movie, for a while.

I also then got caught up in TV projects. I sold or was adjacent to sales for six TV shows; some of them got close, one of them aired, but it didn’t open a new chapter of my life as much as I wanted it to. But it took a decade of my life developing these six TV shows and shooting some of them and writing scripts for other ones. I thought about, “Well, I have four months here, maybe I can write a script and then shoot it in my next break.” But I just quite honestly didn’t really have the mojo. I didn’t have the drive to make another movie. And yes, I also started acting more after Girls than I’d ever acted before. But about two years ago, I had more free time and, more importantly, a resurrected enthusiasm to make another movie.

The industry has gone through so much since 2012. What felt different about making this scale of movie now versus then?

I expected to feel more of a change than I actually did, to be honest with you. The biggest changes that I experienced were technical. The cameras are a lot better and cheaper. The lights are a lot better and cheaper. The editing software’s a lot better and cheaper. So it’s easier in a sense, financially and technically, to make an ultra low-budget movie that looks decent. When I shot my last movies, we were using these Canon DSLR cameras, which were all the rage back then, but you can’t really do handheld stuff — there were a lot of limitations to it. Now for almost the same price, you can have a camera make an image that I think most civilians couldn’t tell whether it’s Hollywood or the camera you got at Best Buy for $3,000.

Did you consider scaling up, budget-wise, in your return to filmmaking?

I couldn’t get the money to scale up, so I did it this way — very affordably. My approach over the past few years has been to write something that would require funds and resources and patience. While that is hopefully incubating somewhere and getting slow momentum somewhere behind the Hollywood curtain, you’re not waiting for the phone to ring. You’re keeping busy making smaller projects that you can actually control and have agency over. It’s also the approach a lot of my friends and I’m still waiting for some of these other projects to maybe have an opening, but I can’t just hope that that’ll happen. I have to stay busy.

I want to go back to what you asked a moment ago.

Now that I think about it, although I don’t know how conscious of it I was when I was shooting it: The world has become more spicy. I don’t know if that’s the adjective, but there’s a lot more anger, it seems like, in the world than there was 14 years ago. A lot more people seem to take a thrill in criticizing you. There’s a lot of haters, you can say. There were haters around back in the day, but they didn’t seem to be in these numbers or have this type of volatility to them. So back in the day, I think it required less courage to make movies. Now, in addition to everything else it requires to make a movie, you also just have to have the fortitude to endure the arrows that will invariably come your way. I feel like there are more arrows than there used to be.

Even with something as simple as the advent of Letterboxd, the feedback is also more immediate in a way that I imagine is probably terrifying.

Yeah, it’s absolutely terrifying. There’s so much fear involved in making a movie to begin with, and then you add this other very powerful and visceral layer on top, it stops a lot of people from expressing themselves, I think.

Your film is in conversation with this topic to some degree — you play around with the Instagram interface as a way of introducing characters, and the power of an influencer’s support makes for a key plot point. It’s not the most obvious theme for this story, so how did you find your way in there?

Social media and social media influencers are now woven into our new economy. The way that people share and present and market ideas and products in the world — social media influencers now are a huge factor in that process. I wanted the movie to feel relevant in certain ways. Since COVID, there’s been a real surge in self-help interests. They’ve spiked upwards and I don’t know all the answers, but I’m curious why that’s going on. And a lot of people are now making their money through social media influence. I read somewhere that they asked 8-year-old kids, “What do you want to be when they grow up?” — the question they’ve asked for generations — and now it’s like 40 percent?

What else inspired the film? It’s set in Sayulita, Mexico, for starters.

We shot in Sayulita, which is a small town. It’s an hour north of Puerto Vallarta. I’ve been going there for about 10 or 15 years because it’s easy to get to and it’s so beautiful. I started to meet people down there. There’s a lot of stoners and burnouts and yoga people and surf people and local people. When I was talking to the expats and gringos that are down there, they’d spend their days surfing and smoking weed and drinking with their pals. In moments of stress, which is often when I visited, I was filled with a sense of envy for these people because they seemed to be really enjoying their life and they seemed genuinely happy. It wasn’t a front for most people. And then there were other times where I would talk to some of these people and I felt judgy, like I pitied them.

I thought, “Well, these people feel like they’ve abandoned something. They’ve surrendered a dream, they’ve surrendered their ambition, and there’s something sad in that too.” I kept oscillating, and that became an interesting subject for me: Happiness versus meaning is ultimately what it could be crystallized down to. And I wanted to Trojan horse this idea into an absurd, silly jungle comedy.

Can you talk about finding the tone in terms of the casting? Steve is very broadly funny, while Bojana is a grounding force. How did you develop that so it’d feel complementary?

I really wanted to make sure we nailed the tone, and I wanted to make sure everyone was acting in the same movie… When you’re mixing absurd comedy with something kind of grounded, and there’s death involved, and there’s darkness involved, I just wanted to make sure those things can blend together — and if they do blend together, make sure that everyone knows how they blend together and know how to plug into the same movie together. That really starts with the actors, and I feel like, exactly as you said it, Steve really captured the comedy of it, but also the heart of it and the tenderness of it and the innocence of it. Bojana really captured the drama and the tragedy of it.

I didn’t really know if it would work until they started having scenes together. When we shot the movie, I wanted to make sure we shot some of their pivotal interaction scenes early on to establish the movie, so everyone could see the movie we were making tonally.

Going forward, do you hope to work at a more accelerated pace behind the camera now? Does it feel sustainable to you to keep making movies at this scale, given the current mechanisms for distribution and indie financing?

I would like to keep making movies in an accelerated way. What’s really nice about making low-budget movies, to say the obvious, is that you have a lot of control. I don’t have to answer to anyone, for better or for worse. If I want to cast this person, I can cast them. If I want to shoot in this location, I can do so. If I want to make these choices in the editing room, I can do so. You also don’t need to wait very long, relatively speaking, to make these low-budget movies. You don’t need to wait four years to package and then hope that no one drops out at the last minute.

I’m still hoping one of my bigger projects might catch momentum out there in Hollywoodland. It’d be a fun and new adventure to embark upon, but I can’t just wait and do positive visualization exercises all day and hope that that’ll happen. I have to take control of my own life. Also, I should say it’s fun. I had a tremendous amount of fun making this movie, and to me, that’s one of, if not the main goal, to be creative with friends, doing something that you are hopefully proud of in places that hopefully are fun to shoot in.

Let’s talk about the Girls revival. All very exciting. Are you in? What are you hearing?

I hear they’re making it. I hear it’s happening. Will I be in it? Who knows!

I’m very curious what he’s been up to. It’s been 10 or 15 years, so I’m very curious what he’s been up to as the world has changed so much since we last saw all these people. I love Ray and I care for him deeply, and sometimes I’m concerned about him, and I hope he’s doing well. I hope he’s found meaning in his life. He seemed a little bit existentially adrift at times to me, or existentially disoriented. That allowed for him to view things maybe a little bit more clearly than other people, but it also has created an inner turmoil. I hope he’s less tormented than he has been, but maybe he won’t be as interesting if he’s less tormented. Mixed feelings!

You were talking earlier about the explosion in haters online, which made me wonder: Did you read Lena Dunham’s memoir?

I didn’t, no. And I don’t know if I have a good reason for it. All of my friends read it, and they all gave me their own synopses and also their own descriptions of when I was mentioned — which, from what I understand, was very sparing, contained. So I haven’t read it. One day I will.

She’s very open about what she went through on that show, including all that negative feedback.

I can tell you this, David. When we made the show, they were very happy years in my life, those six or seven years. I loved playing Ray, and I loved being in a show that people were talking about. The vibe on set, from my very limited point of view, was really positive and fun. I’ve been on some sets before and afterwards; invariably, there’s tension that brews. It just happens, especially the longer you spend time with people. I just didn’t see too much of it from my very limited perspective, but I’m an actor on the show. I’m not privy to a lot of these conversations.

Lena was just incredibly open and receptive. The vibe on set almost always trickles down from the leader. Lena was definitely our leader, and so the vibe that she at least presented when I was there was just very positive, enthused, and receptive. That shaped a lot of people’s feelings on set. It shaped my feelings on set. I’m getting around to saying that my time during those six years was very positive — and maybe the reason why I haven’t read the book yet is because I don’t want anything to change that. I heard there are things that might change that, so I kind of want to stay in my happy place.
