77 points, more than 1,000 yards: Dripping Springs bests Cedar Ridge in playoff battle

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

DRIPPING SPRINGS - Tripp Wiese thought the play was dead. The Dripping Springs running back believed he was destined for the turf after a powerful hit by Cedar Ridge defensive lineman TJ Daniel in the red zone with less than two minutes left in Friday's Class 6A Division II bi-district round contest. But right guard Holden Yeldell held Wiese up, springing him for a 14-yard go-ahead touchdown in Dripping Springs' 42-35 victory at Tiger Stadium.

"Honestly, I thought I was going to get tackled," Wiese told the American-Statesman. "One of my linemen picked me up, I still don't know who it was, dragged me to the end zone. It was amazing."

Dripping Springs Tigers linebacker Tripp Wiese (13) runs the ball in the second half of the bi-district round game against the Cedar Ridge Raiders in the Texas UIL State football playoffs at Tiger Stadium in Dripping Springs, Nov. 13, 2026. (Sara Diggins/Austin American-Statesman)

Wiese's touchdown with 1 minute, 52 seconds left was the final points of a shootout that saw eight lead changes, nearly 1,100 yards of combined offense and 49 second-half points. Both starting quarterbacks easily surpassed 300 yards and each team had a 100-yard rusher.

"It was a paycheck. We worked all four years," said Wiese, who ran for 136 yards and two touchdowns on 16 carries. "It was our last game here. It meant the world to me to be able to ball out like that, step up whenever the team needed me."

Dripping Springs Tigers wide receiver Dodge Reid (3) celebrates after beating Cedar Ridge 42-35 in the bi-district championship in their first game of the Texas UIL State football playoffs at Tiger Stadium in Dripping Springs, Nov. 13, 2026. (Sara Diggins/Austin American-Statesman)

Wiese's second score gave Dripping Springs (9-1) the lead, but it didn't secure victory. Cedar Ridge marched down the field on the ensuing drive and got down to the 10-yard line. But on fourth-and-5, Dripping Springs defensive back Anthony Delgado intercepted a pass near the goal line to seal the win.

"That final play, it's ingrained in my mind now," Delgado said. "I was just reading the quarterback. … Then I saw him stare at the receiver, so I broke on the route and just made a play."

The result ends Cedar Ridge's (9-2) best season since the Raiders made the state semifinals in 2017. Cedar Ridge, which won just three games two years ago, won its first eight games this season and notched wins over prestigious Central Texas programs in Lake Travis and Round Rock.

The Raiders led on multiple occasions but failed to score three times in the red zone and struggled to get off the field against the Tigers.

"It just kinda became a little back-and-forth chess match there," Cedar Ridge coach Bryan Vardeman said. "They had got a couple more than we did there, which led to them being ahead by one more (score) than us."

Dripping Springs Tigers defensive back Anthony Delgado (21) smiles with the trophy after beating Cedar Ridge 42-35 in the bi-district championship in their first game of the Texas UIL State football playoffs at Tiger Stadium in Dripping Springs, Nov. 13, 2026. (Sara Diggins/Austin American-Statesman)

Cedar Ridge began Friday's contest with a nearly seven-minute drive that ended with a 21-yard touchdown pass from Dylan Snoga to Jace Allen. Dripping Springs responded with a long series of its own that ended with a 1-yard rushing touchdown by Jackson Saafir, a starting defensive lineman who's sometimes deployed at fullback.

The score was knotted 14-14 when Cedar Ridge got the ball with 1:27 left in the second quarter. The Raiders drove inside the 5-yard line with less than 10 seconds left. The Dripping Springs defense stifled running back Devin Patterson, who had scored earlier in the half, twice to ensure the game was tied at halftime. Earlier in the quarter, Dripping Springs forced an incompletion on fourth-and-3 from the 8.

"We've been working our butts off all season working on situations," Tigers defensive lineman Colby Hans said. "Every situation we did tonight, we practiced that, we repped that."

The Dripping Springs Tigers defense tackle Cedar Ridge Raiders wide receiver Jace Allen (2) in the second half of the bi-district round game of the Texas UIL State football playoffs at Tiger Stadium in Dripping Springs, Nov. 13, 2026. (Sara Diggins/Austin American-Statesman)

After a defensive stand to end the first half, there was little defense to be found in the third quarter. Dripping Springs quarterback Chase Ames, battling through a first-half ankle injury, ran in a 1-yard touchdown to put the Tigers up by six - the extra point missed - with 7:24 left in the period.

Snoga, who was 26 of 34 for 362 yards, three touchdowns and two interceptions, found Allen for a 74-yard touchdown a minute later to give Cedar Ridge a 21-20 lead. That advantage was short-lived as Ames connected with Harrison Rohrer for a 73-yard touchdown two plays later. Cedar Ridge running back Devon Crank, who rushed for 126 yards, ran for an 8-yard score that put his team up 28-27 for the final score of the quarter.

"It's like the can opened up in the second half and there was just fireworks," said Vardeman, Cedar Ridge's second-year head coach.

Just five seconds into the fourth quarter, Dripping Springs took a 35-28 lead on Wiese's first score of the game, as well as a pass from Ames to Ryder Mustain on the 2-point conversion. Snoga connected with Tyler Burchett for an 8-yard touchdown to tie the score with 7:02 left.

Dripping Springs' next drive was a 12-play, five-plus minute drive that ended with Wiese's game-winning score. The play before the touchdown, Ames limped to the line of scrimmage, then threw a 21-yard completion to Judah Satterfield on fourth-and-7. The UTRGV commit completed 20 of 31 passes for 337 yards and a touchdown.

"He's just tough," Dripping Springs coach Galen Zimmerman said of his quarterback, who tossed his 35th touchdown of the season. "He's a football player. I know he's a great quarterback, but he's a football player."

This was arguably Dripping Springs' most impressive win of the season. The Tigers cruised to the District 26-6A title - their closest margin of victory was 28 points. Their lone loss was a nondistrict loss to Round Rock, a team Cedar Ridge beat last week.

Werner Holzer Prize awarded to Katja Gloger and DW's Jaafar Abdul Karim

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

DW journalist Jaafar Abdul Karim and Russia correspondent Katja Gloger have been awarded the prestigious Werner Holzer Prize for international journalism.

At a time when press freedom is under pressure and international newsrooms are shrinking, the Werner Holzer Prize celebrates a profession that many consider indispensable to democracy: foreign correspondence.

Presented in honor of Werner Holzer, the veteran reporter and former editor-in-chief of the German daily newspaper Frankfurter Rundschau, this year's award coincided with what would have been his 100th birthday. Holzer spent decades reporting from some of the world's most important hot spots and championing international journalism.

At the award ceremony in Frankfurt's Römer, the city's historic town hall, Mayor Mike Josef recalled that fundamental rights such as freedom of speech and freedom of the press were first enshrined for all Germans by the Frankfurt Parliament in the nearby Paulskirche in 1848.

The 2026 winners reflect the breadth and ambition of the award. First prize went to Katja Gloger, a veteran Russia correspondent who moved to Moscow in 1989 and accompanied Vladimir Putin during the early years of his presidency.

In her recent nonfiction book, "The Failure: An Investigative Report of Germany's Russia Policy," Gloger and co-author Georg Mascolo offer a searing critique of the misjudgments and illusions that shaped German policy toward Russia for decades.

Second prize was awarded to DW journalist and presenter Jaafar Abdul Karim. His talk show JaafarTalk reaches millions of viewers across the Arab world and in Germany. Whether in Tunis, Beirut or Cairo, Abdul Karim tackles subjects that are often considered taboo.

A research grant went to freelance journalist Luise Land, whose current project explores why her German-Namibian family still owns extensive landholdings in Namibia today. By tracing that history, she examines the enduring legacy of the genocide against the Herero and Nama peoples by German colonial forces in the early 20th century.

"Katja Gloger is one of the leading foreign policy reporters. For decades, she has been among the foremost experts on Russia and speaks out on uncomfortable truths," the jury stated on Friday, explaining its decision to honor Gloger. "Like almost no one else, she has gained access to the very heart of the Kremlin's power elite — from Mikhail Gorbachev to Vladimir Putin."

At the prize-giving ceremony, Gloger delivered a passionate plea for more journalism — and more international coverage — in the German press. "We must defend trustworthy, reliable and fact-based journalism with everything in our power," she said.

Gloger worked for Reporters Without Borders for 12 years. "The latest rankings show that only one in 100 people can get their information from free, independent journalism and a free, independent press. That shows what's at stake," she said.

The prize winner also said she would dedicate the award to journalists in exile and to her colleagues at Novaya Gazeta, the independent Russian newspaper long known for its critical reporting on the Kremlin. Earlier this week, the paper was forced to shut down its operations in Russia and leave its Moscow newsroom.

In his acceptance speech, Jaafar Abdul Karim said journalism has become more challenging and exhausting than ever. The DW host spoke of hatred and incitement, attacks and threats, both online and offline, in the regions he reports on as well as in Germany.

Such pressure, he said, takes a personal toll — yet giving up is not an option. "When I listen to my heart, I know I have to keep going," he said. The answer, he argued, is a form of journalism that fosters dialogue, embraces multiple perspectives and helps bridge divides rather than deepen them.

"I firmly believe in the power of dialogue, today more than ever. That's why we must talk to one another. Even when it gets difficult. Even when we disagree. Or perhaps precisely because of that," he said.

Listening doesn't mean agreeing, and understanding doesn't mean being in agreement. "Especially now, in times of polarization, when those who believe they're always right are often the loudest, we as journalists have a special responsibility. When everyone is just shouting into their own bubbles or communities, we need journalists who connect these bubbles with one another," he said.

The jury praised Abdul Karim for creating programs at DW that reach millions of young people across the Arab world and in Germany. "His journalism fosters dialogue between countries, between generations, and between religions. He has set new standards," the jury stated.

So far in 2026, JaafarTalk has already garnered more than 1.1 billion views across all platforms, and its community has grown to more than 9 million followers. Its motto: "Diversity fosters dialogue."

"We need fact-based journalism that promotes dialogue and reflects the diversity of our society," Abdul Karim stressed. "By that, I mean an honest and open dialogue in which we do not look away or remain silent. On the contrary, we must take a clear stand against racism, antisemitism, Islamophobia, discrimination, sexism, and hate speech — no matter which side they come from."

Why Bruce Buffer Traded the Octagon for the Steakhouse in New Bud Light Campaign

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The UFC ring announcer stars in a new Bud Light spot, as the beer brand and Omaha Steaks launch a new fan engagement play.

As the UFC returns to Madison Square Garden Saturday night, UFC ring announcer Bruce Buffer is trading the octagon for a steakhouse in a new Bud Light spot.

The campaign sees Buffer swap places with a steakhouse server so they can watch the big UFC fight, leaving him to serve the customers.

“I love when I perform or even when I’m doing commercials to be self-deprecating,” Buffer tells The Hollywood Reporter about the spot. “I like making fun of myself and just having fun for comedy purposes, and the creative people at Bud Light, you know, when they send me the storyboards, I go, ‘This is going to be awesome.’ So I totally dug my teeth into it. The Bud Light spot was released ahead of UFC 334, which will take place tonight at Madison Square Garden in New York City. Bud Light is an official sponsor of UFC, with its spots typically running during the broadcast both for viewers and those in the arena.

“Bud Light’s creative team is just really fantastic at what they do, and looking at that, I trust them to come up with what they came up with, and they also give me the freedom to give feedback,” says Buffer. “It’s really, really cool, and it’s great when you work with people that are that comfortable to work with and respect the fact that they’re not damaging your brand, but yet enhancing it.”

In addition to the spot, Bud Light is partnering with UFC and Omaha Steaks on a “Fight Night Feast” package, which will bundle tomahawk steaks, Bud Light beer money, a Paramount+ gift card and UFC training gloves for $99.

“We know the best part of fight night is getting together with friends over an ice-cold Bud Light and some great food,” said Todd Allen, senior VP of marketing for Bud Light at Anheuser-Busch. “And with UFC 335 set to close out the year, we wanted to create something for UFC fans that makes fight night easy to enjoy. Fight Night Feast delivers everything needed for an unforgettable night of UFC action directly to fans’ doorsteps.”

But it’s also a testament to how far UFC has come. What was once an outcast sport now counts America’s biggest beer brand as a sponsor.

“When I started with the UFC 30 years ago, we were a spectacle. We weren’t what I would consider a sport,” Buffer recalls. “It was like a blood sport, like the movie. It was what style is the best? It grew with the rules and became mixed martial arts seven or eight years into its evolutionary process, to now where it’s a mainstream sport, to where we’re accepted by the Paramount networks and all the other big networks of this world, and to get to the point now where we’re working with Bud Light and mainstream sponsors that work with the other major sports in the world, it shows that UFC is truly the biggest fighting event in the world and is a mainstream sport. It’s like we made it. It’s a clarification that we’re there.”

At the "What’s Next" gathering in Denver, Democratic leaders weighed the party’s future and whether they could see themselves in the 2028 presidential race.

DENVER — Democrats are already making their case for 2028.

Fresh off sweeping midterm victories, party leaders and rising stars descended on Denver for the "What’s Next" conference. Several potential presidential contenders tested their 2028 messaging before a crowd of activists, strategists and operatives.

The conversations offered an early look at who may be considering a run and who is already stepping aside.

Less than 24 hours after announcing a challenge to longtime Senate Democratic leader Sen. Chuck Schumer, D-N.Y., Sen. Chris Van Hollen, D-Md., told reporters he was "no longer pursuing" a presidential bid.

"I believe that the size and scale of these majorities, provides an opportunity for the United States Senate to answer the call of the American people," Van Hollen told reporters in Denver, as he pointed what will be at least a 55-seat Democratic majority in the chamber.

Van Hollen’s comments came as Sen. Cory Booker, D-N.J., weighed whether to make another run for the White House in 2028.

Sen. Cory Booker, D-N.J., is among the Democratic figures drawing attention as the party begins looking toward 2028. (Paul Steinhauser/Fox News Digital)

Booker said he is giving "a lot of hard thought" to a potential 2028 presidential campaign, though he said his focus now is pushing for what he called an "American renewal."

"My purpose right now, I want to see America in the next five years have the biggest renewal, the biggest resetting of the economic table so it works for all Americans," Booker told Fox News Digital.

He pointed to the cost of housing, healthcare and childcare as evidence that many Americans are struggling to get ahead. "The gap I’m concerned about is not the gap between left and right anymore, it’s a gap between the American people and the American dream, and I want to see that gap close," he said.

Asked what he might do differently if he ran again after his unsuccessful 2020 campaign, Booker said candidates should be "their authentic self." He also warned that political divisions are pulling the country apart, saying, "We are not each other’s enemies. We are our fellow patriots. And we all need to start pulling in the same direction."

"Mezzo secolo di storia al servizio del calcio italiano". La nota per i 50 anni dell'ADISE

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Domani, 15 novembre 2026, l’ADISE (Associazione Italiana Direttori Sportivi) celebra i suoi primi cinquant’anni di storia. Di seguito il comunicato integrale:

"Mezzo secolo durante il quale ha accompagnato l’evoluzione del calcio italiano, contribuendo in maniera determinante al riconoscimento, alla qualificazione e alla tutela di una delle figure centrali nell’organizzazione delle società calcistiche: il direttore sportivo. Bisogna tornare agli anni Sessanta e Settanta per comprendere le ragioni di quella iniziativa. Il calcio italiano stava attraversando una profonda trasformazione e, accanto alla tradizionale figura del segretario, storico collaboratore del presidente nella gestione delle attività societarie, cominciava ad affermarsi una nuova professionalità, chiamata a occuparsi della programmazione tecnica, della scelta dei calciatori e delle trattative di mercato.

Fu proprio un gruppo di questi dirigenti, consapevole della necessità di dare identità, dignità e riconoscimento a una professione emergente, a costituire il 15 novembre 1976 l’ADISE, originariamente Associazione dei Direttori e dei Segretari delle società sportive. Il primo presidente fu Carlo Montanari, dirigente del Bologna, affiancato dai vicepresidenti Franco Manni della Fiorentina e Giulio Cappelli del Lecco. Il primo Consiglio Direttivo comprendeva anche Sergio Sacchero, segretario del Monza, Giacomo Randazzo dell’Atalanta e numerosi altri esponenti della dirigenza calcistica nazionale, tra i quali Giancarlo Beltrami, Giorgio Vitali, Piero Aggradi, Giancarlo Fiumi e Arturo Silvestri. La prima sede dell’Associazione fu stabilita a Monza, in via Manzoni 9.

Da quella iniziativa prese avvio un percorso destinato a lasciare un segno profondo nell’organizzazione del calcio italiano. L’ADISE divenne progressivamente l’interlocutore delle istituzioni calcistiche per il riconoscimento della figura del direttore sportivo, sostenendone la regolamentazione professionale, la definizione delle competenze e la tutela dei rapporti di lavoro con le società. Un impegno che ha trovato importanti risultati nella disciplina federale della professione e soprattutto nella stipulazione degli accordi collettivi con le Leghe, fondamentali per assicurare garanzie contrattuali e giuridiche a una categoria sempre più qualificata. Una storia fatta anche di uomini che hanno segnato il calcio italiano. Dopo Carlo Montanari, alla guida dell’Associazione si sono succeduti Franco Dalcin, Pierpaolo Marino e Carlo Regalia, fino all’attuale presidente Giuseppe Marotta, oggi presidente dell’Inter e tra le personalità più autorevoli della dirigenza calcistica internazionale.

Presidenze che hanno attraversato stagioni diAerenti, contribuendo a consolidare il prestigio e la rappresentatività dell’ADISE. Ma parlare dell’ADISE significa inevitabilmente parlare anche del Calciomercato, con il quale l’Associazione ha costruito un rapporto storico e privilegiato. Fin dagli anni Ottanta, e in particolare con l’aAidamento uAiciale della sua organizzazione nel 1989, l’ADISE è stata protagonista degli appuntamenti milanesi dedicati alle trattative. Erano gli anni dei grandi alberghi, dei corridoi aAollati, dei salotti e dei celebri box delle società, nei quali si incontravano presidenti, direttori sportivi, allenatori e operatori di mercato. Un mondo che ha alimentato pagine indimenticabili della storia del calcio italiano e che l’Associazione ha accompagnato nella sua progressiva trasformazione verso un’organizzazione moderna e strutturata.

Ancora oggi, attraverso la collaborazione con Master Group Sport, l’ADISE continua a essere protagonista dell’organizzazione del Calciomercato, appuntamento che rappresenta non soltanto il luogo delle trattative, ma anche un’importante occasione di confronto, aggiornamento e incontro tra le diverse componenti del sistema calcistico. Altrettanto significativo è stato, nel corso dei decenni, l’impegno per la formazione dei dirigenti sportivi. Dalla storica esperienza dei corsi federali di Coverciano alle più recenti iniziative promosse dalle università statali e telematiche, l’Associazione ha sostenuto la necessità di una preparazione sempre più elevata, adeguata alle responsabilità tecniche, giuridiche, economiche e manageriali richieste dal calcio contemporaneo. Un’attenzione estesa anche al mondo dilettantistico, attraverso la valorizzazione dei collaboratori della gestione sportiva, figure che operano nelle società e nelle strutture della Lega Nazionale Dilettanti.

Una realtà alla quale l’ADISE dedica particolare attenzione anche attraverso la propria organizzazione territoriale e i rapporti con i Comitati Regionali. Oggi, con sede operativa a Bologna e un’intensa attività istituzionale che si sviluppa anche a Milano, l’ADISE continua a rappresentare un riferimento per i propri associati, impegnandosi nella tutela professionale, nell’aggiornamento degli accordi collettivi e nel confronto con la FIGC e le Leghe. Le trasformazioni introdotte dalla riforma del lavoro sportivo, l’internazionalizzazione del mercato e la presenza di nuove figure professionali rendono ancora più attuale la necessità di difendere l’identità e le competenze del direttore sportivo, evitando sovrapposizioni di ruoli e garantendo una preparazione all’altezza delle nuove esigenze. Meatballs are an excellent option because you can stuff the flavors of Thanksgiving turkey and stuffing into them, minus the hours of cook time. Comments by both Putin and Kremlin officials suggest that Russia's position on the war has not shifted, and that Moscow's demands for territory and neutrality continue to far exceed what Ukraine is prepared to consider.

Moscow has previously proposed limits on the size of Ukraine's military, territorial concessions and Kyiv's guaranteed neutrality as conditions of a peace deal.

Ukraine has rejected these, arguing that such constraints would hamper its ability to stave off any future invasion.

Russia has now raised the stakes further. It has revived 2021 demands for Nato to halt any expansion in Russia's vicinity and to roll back military infrastructure from countries of central and eastern Europe that became Nato members following the dissolution of the Soviet Union.

rune bosse puts living trees on life support in a vision of ecological collapse

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

equipped with respirators and artificial lighting, rune bosse's 'tree lungs' imagines a future where nature itself may need life support.

Danish artist Rune Bosse places living trees on artificial life support in Lung Trees, an installation presented at the Danish Architecture Center in Copenhagen as part of its Age of Nature exhibition, which ran through September 2026. Each tree stands inside a transparent, inflated plastic enclosure, and is surrounded by mechanical equipment and artificial lighting. With their branches reaching toward overhead lamps, each tree seems to have been removed from the forest and placed under intensive care.

The installation imagines a future in which trees might depend on technology to survive, even as humans continue to depend on the ecological systems that trees help sustain. It reverses a relationship we tend to take for granted. Forests have long been described as the lungs of the planet, absorbing carbon dioxide through photosynthesis and releasing oxygen. Here, Bosse gives those lungs a mechanical counterpart, and suggests a world in which the natural systems supporting human life might themselves require artificial support.

The installation brings together living vegetation and an exposed network of machines. Transparent plastic sheeting stretches over metal frames, enclosing the trees while allowing visitors to see the soil and branches inside. Artificial lights illuminate the foliage, while equipment supplies air to the enclosed environment. The temporary structures have the practical appearance of makeshift greenhouses, although their association with medical equipment gives them a more unsettling character.

In a related 2025 presentation at Berlin’s Neue Nationalgalerie, titled Lungetræ / Lungenbaum, Bosse made the breathing movement particularly visible. A living tree and a section of forest floor were enclosed within a flexible PVC membrane attached to a metal framework. At regular intervals, a respirator pushed air into the structure, causing the plastic to expand and slowly collapse around the tree. The movement resembles the inflation of a giant lung, giving physical form to the invisible exchange of gases between a tree and its surroundings.

Bosse’s interest in trees extends well beyond the gallery. Alongside his training at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts, the artist studied forestry and nature management, developing a practice that follows the growth of living plants over time. At Oremandsgaard, an estate in southern Denmark, he has established an outdoor forest laboratory where he experiments with shaping trees as they grow. Some are bent into unusual forms, while others are guided to grow together. Their final shapes emerge over years, influenced by the conditions of the surrounding landscape.

This work grew partly from his frustration with bringing natural materials into museums, where plants often have to be cut, dried or preserved before they can be exhibited. His outdoor experiments allow trees to remain alive and continue changing long after the artist’s initial intervention. Lung Trees approaches the same relationship from a different direction. Inside the gallery, keeping the trees alive becomes part of the artwork itself, exposing the equipment and artificial conditions that make their temporary existence possible.

The installation touches on an uncomfortable contradiction in the way humans approach ecological preservation. By enclosing the trees and supplying them with artificial conditions, Bosse creates an image of protection that also suggests dependency. The machinery appears to care for the living organisms, yet the entire arrangement places their survival within a system designed and operated by humans. The trees remain living participants in the work, although their relationship with the surrounding environment has been fundamentally altered.

Bosse first developed Lungetræer in 2019, and its more recent presentations give the work a continuing relevance amid growing concerns about biodiversity loss and the deterioration of ecosystems. The installation offers a speculative glimpse of what technological preservation might look like when ecological conditions can no longer support life on their own. Its most unsettling quality comes from the simplicity of the scene.

A living tree stands inside a plastic chamber while a machine pushes air around it. The visitor watches the artificial lung expand and contract, confronted with the possibility that the relationship between humans and forests could one day become this fragile.
