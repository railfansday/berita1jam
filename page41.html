Kristen Bell Gives Sneak Peek at What's Thawing for Frozen 3

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Kristen Bell shared an update on Frozen 3 after reprising her role as Princess Anna in the upcoming Walt Disney Animation Studios sequel.

Kristen Bell is sharing a glimpse into the unknown.

As production for Frozen 3 heats up, the Nobody Wants This actress detailed stepping back into Princess Anna’s shoes for the third installment in the beloved Disney animated film franchise.

“Honestly, I’m living my dreams. I'm really living my dreams right now,” Bell shared during an appearance on the Nov. 4 episode of The Jennifer Hudson Show. “At 46 years old, you can still live all of your dreams.”

And she also opened the door into singing what will surely be magical musical numbers.

“I recorded two songs on Friday of this past week,” Bell revealed to Jennifer Hudson. “I love them so much.”

Though fans will have to try to let go of any impatience since the third film won’t be hitting theaters until Nov. 24, 2027, the Veronica Mars alum looks forward to the finished product.

“I just am very proud of what's being written,” Bell shared, “and I'm very excited to share it with the world.”

And while it may seem like this is the first time in forever since there has been an update on Frozen 3, Bell and her costar Idina Menzel—who voices Elsa—recently shared how they made sure magic would flow once again for a third film immediately after Frozen 2 was released in 2019.

“The second [Frozen 2] was over I was waiting by the phone and/or texting everyone, ‘When are we gonna do it?’” Bell told E! News in August. “And so I was in an anticipatory place.”

But Menzel emphasized that the Mouse House is always intentional with its moves and will “wait for the right time.”

“They're super patient with it,” she shared. “I guess we were a little impatient because we really like to get back to the process. We love being together. We love revisiting these characters.”

As for what more fans can expect when it’s time to build a snowman once more? Disney Animation head Jared Bush proved that some things may never truly change as Frozen 3 will tell “a very special story” like its predecessors.

That said, he teased to Collider in an interview published Nov. 3 the tale is “one that will surprise people.”

Until Frozen 3 soars into theaters next year, don’t hold your excitement back anymore and read for Frozen secrets…

1. An adaptation of Hans Christian Andersen's 1845 fairy tale The Snow Queen, Frozen was actually in development at Disney for almost a decade (and was briefly considered for a hand-drawn animated feature back in 1937).

The first modern attempt happened in 2002, and when 2003 also proved unsuccessful, more stalled attempts followed over the years. Frozen really didn't start coming together until Christopher Buck, who directed Tarzan, came aboard in 2008, but the film wasn't commissioned until 2011 following Tangled's box office success.

And in 2012, Jennifer Lee, who initially came on as a screenwriter, was named co-director, making her the first woman to direct an animated film by Walt Disney Animation Studios.

2. The movie was going to be titled The Snow Queen after the original fairy tale, but the filmmakers decided to change it to Frozen "because, to us, it represents the movie. Frozen plays on the level of ice and snow but also the frozen relationship, the frozen heart that has to be thawed," producer Peter Del Vecho explained to Bleeding Cool, denying it was inspired by Tangled. (The Snow Queen title was still used overseas.)

3. Idina Menzel and Kristen Bell auditioned and met with the producers of Tangled for the role of Rapunzel, which ultimately went to Mandy Moore. (Santino Fontana, who gave life to Anna's temporary fiancé Hans, originally auditioned for the role of Flynn in the 2010 film as well).

Bell, whose dream since she was a little girl was to voice a Disney character, was the "very first actress" to audition for Anna, with Lee saying, "We did audition a lot of people for that but she was the first and the best. We just fell in love with her."

4. Menzel's audition for Elsa, meanwhile, was actually a table-read with Bell before both had been officially cast, according to Lee.

"They read the whole script and at the end—we didn't have any songs written yet—and what they did was sing a duet and they sung 'Wind Beneath My Wings,' but they sung it like sisters and what you mean to me," she explained. "And there wasn't a dry eye in the house after they sang...that is when they knew, that was the whole potential and understanding the power of music and of what this emotional story can be."

5. In the early versions of the project, Elsa was actually the villain of the story (with one of the early concept art designs being inspired by Bette Midler and her "showgirl stage presence"), according to former Disney animation designer Claire Keane, who released the image on her web site.

"Originally, she was much more villainous," Buck allowed to Nerdist. "It's very easy to go in that direction for that type of character, but as the time went and we evolved the story, she became much more three-dimensional. Before, I thought she was one-dimensional but a very fully fleshed out character."

The moment the character really changed, though, was after they watched earlier cuts and realized it wasn't exactly working: "Someone came up with the brilliant idea...'what if the protagonist, Anna, and Elsa were related?'" art director Michael Giaimo revealed. "Because they weren't always siblings, they weren't always related, and that was the beginning of finding these characters."

6. The hardest character for the Frozen team to nail down aside from Elsa was actually Olaf, who was originally then-villainous Elsa's first guard.

"She created these snowman guards and Marshmallow was kind of a holdover from that because he was this scary snowman guard. Olaf was the very first guard she created," Buck explained, adding he was still funny at that time.

After changing the character of Elsa completely, it wasn't until the directors first sat down with Josh Gad that they found the character. "Josh adlibbed the line 'it's like a baby unicorn' and there was something about that innocence," Del Vecho told Kidz World.

7. One main character who wasn't originally in the movie was Hans, Anna's suitor-turned-villain, with Giaimo explaining, "Hans was never originally in the story in older versions...Kristoff always was in the story or a version of the Kristoff character and Anna. And then Hans was brought in at one point but he wasn't always a villain who turned initially, and then someone came up with the idea of him turning. He was always potentially a villain, but we gave it away much sooner."

8. Anna's oft-repeated "Wait what?" catchphrase? An ad-lib by Bell. She also slipped in one slightly off-color joke. "We slid it under the radar," she detailed to Vanity Fair of Anna's singing, "For years I've roamed these empty halls / Why have a ballroom with no balls?" The NSFW lyric almost got snipped, she admitted, "But then we were like, 'What are you talking about? That’s not what it means. Don’t be a perv.'"

11. After having a few of the first songs they originally composed for the film not make it in, songwriting couple Robert Lopez and Kristen Anderson-Lopez were feeling a little low, so they went for walk in Brooklyn's Prospect Park.

Listening to songs by Adele and Avril Lavigne and being surrounded by nature, Anderson-Lopez told Playbill, "We were in this kind of emo place of imagining what it would be like to be this perfect person who messed up once, and was chased away from everything she has known and all the people she has been sacrificing for."

Within a day, they would write the film's most iconic song that would go on to sell over 10 million copies in 2014 and win the Oscar for Best Original Song. All together now, "Let it go, let it goooooooo."

12. And after hearing "Let It Go" for the first time, Lee said she knew they had to rework the character of Elsa and her motivations completely.

"Half of us were crying. And then I just went, 'I have to rewrite the whole movie,'" she said on the Scriptnotes podcast. "I really, it was—I was just like I'm going to go lie down for a couple minutes. But it was the best thing. We knew we had the movie."

13. On the same podcast, Lee admitted there was "a lot of push and pull" when it came to Elsa's look after her transformation via "Let It Go" and how sexy to make her after this awakening.

"Everyone was seduced by her. And so there was this tug of war I think, a bit, of letting people have a little—people who wanted to have that a little and not be afraid of it, but not make it a sexual statement," the co-director explained. "It's more a moment of, for me, it was like you strut and you say nobody is looking, this is what I'm going to—I'm not going to be afraid of my sexuality. I'm not going to be afraid of who I am. I'm not going to be afraid of anything about myself."

14. After eagle-eyed fans spotted one of many easter eggs in the film—Tangled's Rapunzel (post haircut) and Eugene briefly appearing in a scene during the morning of Elsa's coronation—a compelling fan theory that tied together Frozen, Tangled and Little Mermaid surfaced: Anna and Elsa's parents died when their boat sank...what if it was when they were on their way to the Tangled pair's wedding? And what if the sunken ship in The Little Mermaid is the ship they were sailing on?!

15. This theory only works if you believe Anna and Elsa's parents actually did die in the shipwreck, something Buck speculated about in a Reddit AMA, saying, in his mind, the King and Queen of Arendelle, as well as their "baby boy," did not actually die after being shipwrecked. (Who knew they had a son?)

He said they washed up on the shore of a jungle and built "a tree house" before being "eaten by a leopard." That baby boy? Tarzan, of course.

"That's my fun little world," he later told MTV. While the theory isn't official (and definitely takes a hit when you factor in the events of Frozen II), Buck said, "Whatever people want to believe, go for it. If you want to tie them all together, then do it."

16. The original ending of the film was drastically different, with the earlier versions, of course, presenting Elsa as the villain and not having Anna and Elsa related.

Beginning with a prophecy about "a ruler with a frozen heart will bring destruction to the kingdom of Arendelle," viewers would learn Elsa became the Snow Queen after she was left at the altar and decided to freeze her own heart, according to EW.

By the end of the film, Anna convinces Elsa to use her powers to save Arendelle from an avalanche started by Hans, with the ultimate reveal being the prophecy was actually about the two-faced prince. (Elsa's heart would then unfreeze, making her capable of love again.)

"The problem was that we felt like we had seen it before," Del Vecho told EW of the original ending. "It wasn't satisfying. We had no emotional connection to Elsa—we didn't care about her because she had spent the whole movie being the villain. We weren't drawn in. The characters weren't relatable."

Real Madrid's Nico Paz decision in focus after Como record

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

By Naim Beneddra | 05 Nov 2026 18:54

Real Madrid's decision to allow Nico Paz to remain at Como for another season has come under renewed scrutiny after the Argentine midfielder inspired another remarkable Champions League performance.

The 22-year-old scored once and provided two assists as Cesc Fabregas's side demolished Lens 9-1 on Wednesday evening, equalling the biggest winning margin in Champions League history and setting a new record for the competition's largest away victory.

Paz has now registered three goals and five assists in just four Champions League appearances this season, making him the competition's most productive player in terms of direct goal involvements.

His extraordinary form is particularly significant for Real Madrid, who agreed to let the midfielder continue his development in Italy during the summer but retained the right to bring him back in 2027.

With Como emerging as one of Europe's most exciting attacking teams, the decision to leave Paz under Fabregas's guidance is attracting fresh attention.

Como's astonishing victory in France featured a hat-trick from Anastasios Douvikas, but Paz once again demonstrated why he has become the creative heartbeat of Fabregas's team.

The Argentina international scored Como's third goal in the 40th minute, arriving in the penalty area to finish emphatically after being set up by Douvikas.

He then turned provider shortly after half time, releasing Lucas Da Cunha with a perfectly weighted pass for the visitors' fourth goal.

Ten minutes later, Paz supplied another assist for Douvikas, who completed his hat-trick as Lens's defence continued to unravel.

By the time the Argentine was withdrawn in the 58th minute, Como had scored six times, with their number 10 directly responsible for three of those goals.

His contribution followed an equally impressive performance against Manchester United on Matchday 3, when he scored twice and created another goal in a commanding 4-1 victory over Michael Carrick's side.

Those displays have propelled Paz to the top of the Champions League goal-involvement standings.

According to Opta, his eight direct contributions place him ahead of every other player in this season's competition, including Bayern Munich striker Harry Kane and Barcelona sensation Lamine Yamal.

Paz also leads the tournament's assist chart with five, having supplied two against Manchester United and another two against Lens.

Only two players have registered more assists in their first four appearances of a single Champions League campaign: Real Madrid legend Raul in 2002-03 and former Barcelona forward Neymar in 2016-17, who both recorded six.

The figures illustrate how quickly Paz has established himself among Europe's most influential attacking midfielders.

His outstanding performances have not been limited to continental competition, either.

Across Serie A and the Champions League, the Argentine has contributed six goals and eight assists in 13 appearances this season, underlining his importance to Como's increasingly ambitious project.

Fabregas's team currently sit fourth in the Italian top flight and occupy the same position in the Champions League standings, having recovered emphatically from their defeat to Feyenoord.

For a club competing in Europe's elite competition for the first time, that progress has been exceptional.

However, Paz's individual development also raises an intriguing question about the plans of the European champions who originally brought him through their academy.

Real Madrid faced an important decision over Paz's future during the summer transfer window.

Having sold the midfielder to Como in 2024, Los Blancos retained contractual mechanisms allowing them to regain control of his future.

Reports initially suggested that the Spanish giants were preparing to activate an existing buy-back clause, potentially paving the way for a return to the Santiago Bernabeu.

However, the two clubs eventually reached a different agreement.

On June 29, Real Madrid officially announced that Paz would remain at Como for the 2026-27 season, a decision made at the player's own request.

Crucially, the agreement also confirmed that Madrid would retain a unilateral option to buy back his registration rights for the 2027-28 campaign.

That arrangement allowed Paz to continue playing regularly under Fabregas while preserving a route back to the Spanish capital.

The financial details were not disclosed in Madrid's official announcement, although Spanish newspaper AS reported that the agreement involved a €60m (£52m) transaction and an €80m (£69m) buy-back option for summer 2027.

Madrid's decision was understandable given the competition for attacking midfield positions in Jose Mourinho's squad.

Jude Bellingham, Arda Guler and Brahim Diaz are among the players capable of operating in similar areas, leaving no obvious guarantee that Paz would receive the minutes he currently enjoys at Como.

His Champions League performances nevertheless strengthen the argument that he is developing into a player capable of competing at the highest level.

Real Madrid have not announced any intention to bring him back ahead of schedule, and a recent report from AS indicated that the club would not make a decision on its buy-back options before next spring.

For now, Paz remains central to Fabregas's plans, with Como preparing to host AEK Athens in their next Champions League fixture on November 24.

Madrid's decision to allow him another season in Italy has already helped the Argentine reach new heights. His continued progress will only make the question of what happens in 2027 more compelling.

Alex Karpovsky on Returning to Filmmaking (and Maybe ‘Girls’) After More Than a Decade Away

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The actor and director is making the indie festival rounds with his wild jungle comedy ‘Riptides,’ while also reflecting on his ‘Girls’ experience ahead of the revival: “Will I be in it? Who knows!”

Even if Alex Karpovsky was no more familiar to Girls viewers than the rest of its breakout cast, the Massachusetts native was already relatively established by the time of the HBO show’s premiere — only, behind the camera. He’d directed three micro-budgeted features from 2006 to 2009, and another two that released in 2012, the same year as Girls’ first-season run. The work showcased an idiosyncratic filmmaker freely following his heady, sometimes absurdist instincts.

Then Girls became what it became, and Karpovsky suddenly had an acting career coming off of his deadpan turn as Ray Ploshansky: Now 51, he’s gone on to star in Homecoming, Nobody Wants This, Angelyne and multiple Coen brothers movies. He’d tried to get other projects off the ground over the last decade-plus, including several TV ideas that got stuck in limbo, but he also acknowledges that time was an issue — he simply didn’t have the “mojo” to get another movie going. It feels fitting, then, that Karpovsky returned with his first film in 14 years less than two weeks before Lena Dunham’s announcement that Girls would be coming back as a five-episode limited-series revival. Karpovsky hasn’t been announced to be part of it — he won’t confirm or deny in our interview, but he’s open about imagining Ray’s future and his experience making the show — but there’s a full-circle quality to this arc nonetheless. The world, and the industry, have changed quite a bit, but Girls is back, and Karpovsky is back making movies.

The film in question is Riptides, and it is again very low-budget, playful with its humor and dense with ideas. Set in Sayulita, Mexico, where Karpovsky visits regularly, the movie imagines the collision between Toby (Steve Little), an American beach bum who oversees hotel properties in the idyllic town; Paul (Dylan Baker), a renowned self-help author sneaking in a quick vacation before his next big book tour; and Paul’s daughter, Michelle (Bojana Novakovic), an even-keeled manager who spends most of her time keeping her famous father out of trouble.

Tragedy strikes, and an opportunity arises for Toby to make something of himself and find some meaning, inspired by Paul’s writings. He winds up entangled with Michelle in this scheme, going down a “demented” path as Karpovsky puts it in our conversation.

Riptides next screens at the Indie Memphis Festival on Saturday evening. So far, its path is not dissimilar from the low-fi projects that Karpovsky helmed back in the late aughts and early 2010s, despite drastic changes in the surrounding independent landscape. We get into all of that and more.

You premiered about a month ago at New/Next Film Festival. How did it go?

It went really well. I mean, for me and my disposition, it’s terrifying to show this. The theater had 300 people in it. I know six people in the theater. It’s mostly strangers and you just never know how it’s going to go. Perhaps this is obvious, but the thing about a comedy is that everyone knows in real time whether or not the movie is working or not working — a binary thumbs up, thumbs down feeling — at least for me. You’re in the room and then you have to go up there and do a Q&A. But luckily everything played as I wanted it to…. One little test you can have of how a movie plays at film festivals: Do people stay for the Q&A, quite simply? And they did, and they asked good questions. So I was very happy — perhaps, more than happy, relieved that the movie didn’t fall apart.

It’s been 14 years since your last feature came out, the same year that Girls premiered — so the timing there is interesting. What do you attribute the gap to?

I made five movies before this one and they were all really ultra, ultra low-budget affairs. Two of them came out in the same year — 2012, I guess, Rubberneck and Red Flag — and we were in the middle of shooting Girls. It was fun and exhilarating, but really exhausting. Making any movie is exhausting. But making ultra low-budget movies, because you’re wearing so many hats, it exhausts you in a very specific way. I felt very depleted and I didn’t really have the desire to make another movie, at least a low-budget movie, for a while.

I also then got caught up in TV projects. I sold or was adjacent to sales for six TV shows; some of them got close, one of them aired, but it didn’t open a new chapter of my life as much as I wanted it to. But it took a decade of my life developing these six TV shows and shooting some of them and writing scripts for other ones. I thought about, “Well, I have four months here, maybe I can write a script and then shoot it in my next break.” But I just quite honestly didn’t really have the mojo. I didn’t have the drive to make another movie. And yes, I also started acting more after Girls than I’d ever acted before. But about two years ago, I had more free time and, more importantly, a resurrected enthusiasm to make another movie.

The industry has gone through so much since 2012. What felt different about making this scale of movie now versus then?

I expected to feel more of a change than I actually did, to be honest with you. The biggest changes that I experienced were technical. The cameras are a lot better and cheaper. The lights are a lot better and cheaper. The editing software’s a lot better and cheaper. So it’s easier in a sense, financially and technically, to make an ultra low-budget movie that looks decent. When I shot my last movies, we were using these Canon DSLR cameras, which were all the rage back then, but you can’t really do handheld stuff — there were a lot of limitations to it. Now for almost the same price, you can have a camera make an image that I think most civilians couldn’t tell whether it’s Hollywood or the camera you got at Best Buy for $3,000.

Did you consider scaling up, budget-wise, in your return to filmmaking?

I couldn’t get the money to scale up, so I did it this way — very affordably. My approach over the past few years has been to write something that would require funds and resources and patience. While that is hopefully incubating somewhere and getting slow momentum somewhere behind the Hollywood curtain, you’re not waiting for the phone to ring. You’re keeping busy making smaller projects that you can actually control and have agency over. It’s also the approach a lot of my friends and I’m still waiting for some of these other projects to maybe have an opening, but I can’t just hope that that’ll happen. I have to stay busy.

I want to go back to what you asked a moment ago.

Now that I think about it, although I don’t know how conscious of it I was when I was shooting it: The world has become more spicy. I don’t know if that’s the adjective, but there’s a lot more anger, it seems like, in the world than there was 14 years ago. A lot more people seem to take a thrill in criticizing you. There’s a lot of haters, you can say. There were haters around back in the day, but they didn’t seem to be in these numbers or have this type of volatility to them. So back in the day, I think it required less courage to make movies. Now, in addition to everything else it requires to make a movie, you also just have to have the fortitude to endure the arrows that will invariably come your way. I feel like there are more arrows than there used to be.

Even with something as simple as the advent of Letterboxd, the feedback is also more immediate in a way that I imagine is probably terrifying.

Yeah, it’s absolutely terrifying. There’s so much fear involved in making a movie to begin with, and then you add this other very powerful and visceral layer on top, it stops a lot of people from expressing themselves, I think.

Your film is in conversation with this topic to some degree — you play around with the Instagram interface as a way of introducing characters, and the power of an influencer’s support makes for a key plot point. It’s not the most obvious theme for this story, so how did you find your way in there?

Social media and social media influencers are now woven into our new economy. The way that people share and present and market ideas and products in the world — social media influencers now are a huge factor in that process. I wanted the movie to feel relevant in certain ways. Since COVID, there’s been a real surge in self-help interests. They’ve spiked upwards and I don’t know all the answers, but I’m curious why that’s going on. And a lot of people are now making their money through social media influence. I read somewhere that they asked 8-year-old kids, “What do you want to be when they grow up?” — the question they’ve asked for generations — and now it’s like 40 percent?

What else inspired the film? It’s set in Sayulita, Mexico, for starters.

We shot in Sayulita, which is a small town. It’s an hour north of Puerto Vallarta. I’ve been going there for about 10 or 15 years because it’s easy to get to and it’s so beautiful. I started to meet people down there. There’s a lot of stoners and burnouts and yoga people and surf people and local people. When I was talking to the expats and gringos that are down there, they’d spend their days surfing and smoking weed and drinking with their pals. In moments of stress, which is often when I visited, I was filled with a sense of envy for these people because they seemed to be really enjoying their life and they seemed genuinely happy. It wasn’t a front for most people. And then there were other times where I would talk to some of these people and I felt judgy, like I pitied them.

I thought, “Well, these people feel like they’ve abandoned something. They’ve surrendered a dream, they’ve surrendered their ambition, and there’s something sad in that too.” I kept oscillating, and that became an interesting subject for me: Happiness versus meaning is ultimately what it could be crystallized down to. And I wanted to Trojan horse this idea into an absurd, silly jungle comedy.

Whether you’re wanting to build knee strength to enhance your everyday functionality or you’re recovery from a knee injury and finally at the point where you can practice yoga, you need strengthening exercises that support all the muscles that support and stabilize your knees. Basically, you want to work on the hip adductors, the quads, the glutes, the hamstrings.

The following poses literally work all of the muscles that contribute to knee stability. You’ll also get to play around a little with some dynamic movement and try some balancing exercises that help you understand how to properly engage the muscles that support the knee. Then you can take that awareness into other yoga poses as well as your everyday movements.

Regularly practicing this yoga for knee strength routine will help anyone build knee strength. I know from personal experience just how frustrating a knee injury can be, so if that applies to you, know that the more you dedicate time towards rehabilitating yourself, and the more diligent you are with your practices, the more likely you’ll feel a difference in your ease of movement. And remember that rest is equally as important as stretching and strengthening.

You will need a block or a block-like item for several of these poses. If you experience pain in any of the poses, slowly come out and skip ahead to the next pose. Always consult with your physician or physical therapist prior to introducing new exercises.

Start lying on your back and bring your feet about hip-distance apart and your heels pretty close toward your glutes. Place the block directly in between the inner thighs and squeeze the block as much as you possibly can. Then push down into all four corners of both feet and slowly lift first the pelvis off the mat, then the lower back, and then the mid back in Bridge Pose. The higher you lift, the harder it becomes to squeeze your block, but keep squeezing it anyway. This helps train your inner thighs, or adductor muscles, to contract. Building inner thigh power can also help you become better at balancing poses.

Take about 3 breaths here, pushing down through your big toes. Then exhale and slowly roll down, inch by inch. Move your block to the side.

From lying on your back, lift your right leg toward the sky and straighten it without locking your knee or hyperextending it. Interlace your fingers behind your right hamstrings. Instead of making this a passive stretch, push your leg against your palms as much as you can, and feel your arms resist. Keep pushing, pushing, pushing with your hamstrings. This should be challenging. You’ll also get a little arm workout. Try to keep your lower back neutral and close to the mat. Continue for a few more breaths.

You might find that your leg starts coming a little closer toward your chest, and that’s fine, but don’t force the stretch. Then release and switch legs.

Re-extend your right leg and bring your hands behind the back of your calf muscles. If that means you need to bend a little in your knee, that’s perfectly fine. If you can’t reach that far, bring your hands to the hamstrings again, but shift them closer to the knee, which creates a different sensation. Same thing, you want to push against your hands and resist. Then repeat on the other side.

This is not a yoga pose, but it’s absolutely amazing for your adductors. Grab your block and place it between your inner thighs again. Instead of keeping your heels close to you as in Bridge, walk your feet out so it feels almost as if your legs are almost straight and there’s about 6 inches between the mat and the backs of your knees.

According to a Nov. 5 announcement on the league’s website, in April CEO Scott O’Neil and other LIV Golf leadership began working with independent advisors to “identify the best, value-maximizing path forward for the Company and its stakeholders.”

In his own statement, O’Neil reflected on building the league up from the rough.

"LIV Golf was fun, demanding, inspiring, at times challenging—but always rewarding,” he said. "We created memories that I hope will inspire a new generation of fans to follow, play, and fall in love with this great game.”

And though the league is headed towards a wind down, the announcement did note the company will remain open to and consider any “value-maximum alternatives” that might arise.

“We are heading into the heart of our 2026 schedule,” he continued, “with the full energy of an organization that is bigger, louder, and more influential than ever before.”
