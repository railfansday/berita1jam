EXCLUSIVE: Theatrical tech, distribution and management corp Qube Cinema has hired Imax and 20th Century Fox distribution vet Craig Dehmel as their new Head of Studio Relations.

Dehmel’s 35 years in the motion picture industry includes serving as Head of Global Distribution at Imax, where he led the planning, programming, and distribution of Hollywood studio, local-language, documentary, and special-content releases across more than 1,700 Imax screens worldwide. 2 Montgomery County magnet stories raise 1 question about access

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

“Minorities in Magnets” was the winner of the ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition.

A high school filmmaker saw a problem in Montgomery County, Maryland’s selective programs: Lack of representation of students of color had an impact on their educational experiences. At nearly the same time, a lawsuit challenging admissions to the county’s middle school magnet programs reached the U.S. Supreme Court.

Although these stories involve different programs and raise different questions, they share an important issue: How do we determine whether an admissions system provides fair access to educational opportunities, and what evidence should matter?

The ABA Section of Civil Rights and Social Justice recognized Minorities in Magnets, directed by Toftaa Fedhaa, as the first place winner of the section’s 2026 High School Civil Rights Film Competition. Her film explores the experiences of students of color in the Montgomery County Public Schools’ high school magnet programs and the effects of their underrepresentation.

Separately, on Oct. 19, the Supreme Court agreed to hear Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education. The case concerns admissions to four selective middle school magnet programs, not the high school programs discussed in the film.

While the court did not rule on whether the district’s admissions policy is discriminatory, it agreed to consider a narrower legal question about the evidence plaintiffs must present when challenging a facially neutral admissions policy as racially discriminatory.

The Association for Education Fairness alleges that Montgomery County’s admissions policies intentionally disadvantaged Asian American students. The school district maintains that its admissions system is designed to provide equitable access to rigorous educational opportunities.

After initially challenging a “field test,” the plaintiffs challenged a revised lottery-based system introduced for the 2021-2022 school year. That system screened students for their potential to benefit from enriched instruction and then used a lottery to assign students to magnet programs or enriched instruction at their neighborhood schools.

The federal district court dismissed the challenge to the revised plan, finding that the plaintiffs had not adequately alleged discriminatory effect or intent. The 4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals at Richmond, Virginia, affirmed, applying circuit precedent on disparate impact allegations.

The Supreme Court will now consider whether a plaintiff challenging a facially neutral admissions policy must demonstrate that the affected group experienced an aggregate “disparate impact” compared with its share of the applicant pool.

Put simply, “disparate impact” refers to a policy that produces unequal outcomes for a particular group, even when the policy does not expressly classify individuals by race. An equal protection challenge also raises the question of discriminatory purpose.

The court’s decision will address the legal standards governing these types of claims and whether a particular showing of aggregate impact is required before such a claim may proceed, not whether Montgomery County’s policy ultimately violates the Constitution. The court has not yet decided the merits of the case.

The film underscores why the conversation should not only focus exclusively on admissions policies, statistics and legal standards but should also consider the voices and experiences of students.

The file and the lawsuit should not be confused or treated as evidence of one another. They involve different grade levels, programs and legal questions. The experiences described in the film do not establish what happened in the middle school admissions process, just as the allegations in the lawsuit do not resolve the concerns raised by high school students.

Although selective educational programs can open doors to advanced coursework, specialized instruction and opportunities that shape a student’s academic future, simply making a program available to everyone does not necessarily mean everyone has an equal opportunity to participate.

We must also consider who knows these programs exist, how students are identified and encouraged to apply, what barriers may discourage participation, and whether students feel represented and welcome once they are admitted.

These are important questions for educators, families, policymakers and communities to consider. They may also inform legal analysis, although the applicable legal standards determine what evidence is relevant to a particular claim.

For lawyers, the Supreme Court case highlights the importance of understanding how statistical evidence, applicant pools and allegations of discriminatory intent intersect when challenging facially neutral policies. It also reinforces the need to examine the specific admissions process and how that process may have changed over time. The court’s decision may affect how courts evaluate statistical evidence in challenges to facially neutral admissions policies.

For school officials, families and advocates, the broader questions are equally important:

• How are students identified and informed about selective educational opportunities?

• What criteria determine eligibility and admission?

• Are admissions outcomes regularly examined across schools and student populations?

• Are the policies and measures used to assess access transparent and understandable?

These questions deserve attention regardless of how the Supreme Court ultimately resolves the legal issue before it.

The case is also a reminder that we must distinguish between allegations in a lawsuit, a school district’s stated reasons for its policies, procedural court decisions, and a final ruling on the merits. Understanding those distinctions is essential to an informed discussion.

The winning film, Minorities in Magnets, brings student perspectives into a debate that is often reduced to admissions criteria and demographic statistics. The Supreme Court will address a specific legal question about the evidence required in an equal protection challenge.

Neither alone answers the broader question of what meaningful educational opportunity should look like. But they invite a broader conversation about how communities define opportunity, how they measure access, and how they respond when students say the system is not working for them.

As lawyers and members of our communities, we have an obligation to listen, ask difficult questions, and examine whether the systems we create are providing genuine opportunities for all students.

Ultimately, educational access is about more than who gets admitted. It is also about who gets the opportunity to succeed, who feels that they belong, and whether every student has a fair chance to reach their full potential.

What Cloudflare Doesn't Tell You

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cloudflare has arguably done more to push modern internet technologies than any other company. The work they have done to enable TLS, IPv6, post quantum encryption, and other modern standards for millions of websites deserves a tremendous amount of credit.

While this is great, Cloudflare also hides many of the shortcomings of the underlying infrastructure from both users and website owners. Yes, you can connect to a Cloudflare website and it'll allow for IPv6, post quantum encryption, and TLS. But Cloudflare's connection to the origin server might be entirely unencrypted and rely exclusively on IPv4. This creates a huge mismatch between what is being presented to users and the reality of the connection.

One of my biggest problems with this is the fact that Cloudflare doesn't do enough to notify website administrators about these issues. While Cloudflare provides tools to inspect these, comprehensive visibility is lacking. The Cloudflare dashboard should notify you about each of these issues where Cloudflare is following better practices than your origin server, without website administrators having to dig through and specifically look for this information. Clear warnings and proactive alerts to administrators would help greatly.

Not only that, but according to Cloudflare's own documentation, they actively prefer IPv4 over IPv6 when making origin server requests, which is particularly disappointing for a company that has done so much to advance IPv6 adoption:

For proxied DNS records that have both an IPv6 and IPv4 origin address, Cloudflare will prefer the IPv4 address when connecting to your origin server.

Even when website administrators invest in deploying IPv6 on their origin servers, Cloudflare still prioritizes IPv4.

I also believe the industry should consider adding functionality so CDNs and other intermediate networks can expose origin TLS connection information alongside their own. This would allow users and network researchers to better understand the encryption state of the entire path and hold website owners accountable for bad security practices. A similar mechanism could expose which IP version is being used to communicate with the origin server.

Cloudflare has an opportunity to not only make modern internet technologies accessible to users, but also encourage adoption of those technologies throughout the underlying infrastructure. Cloudflare should be a catalyst for modernizing the internet, not a mechanism that allows website owners to avoid modernization indefinitely. Improving transparency would be a meaningful step toward achieving that goal.

FAA Projects Busiest Thanksgiving Travel Week in 16 Years

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

More than 417,000 flights will operate in the U.S. this week and early next week as Americans travel ahead of and after Thanksgiving, according to the FAA.

If accurate, that figure would translate to the busiest Thanksgiving travel period in 16 years.

Tuesday is expected to be the single busiest day of the interval, with more than 52,000 flights.

Air traffic will dip only slightly on Wednesday (49,831 flights) and nearly halve on Thanksgiving Day (27,619 flights) itself, the FAA said, with a rebound following Friday to Tuesday, Dec. 1. There will be over 50,000 flights per day on the Sunday, Monday, and Tuesday after Thanksgiving.

Perhaps aiming to assuage concerns from the public after a turbulent couple of weeks, transportation officials said they are prioritizing traveler safety and taking “unprecedented action” to limit flight delays and cancellations.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy noted that the Trump administration has assembled a “tiger team” of experts to prevent another mass ground stop.

“Our air traffic controllers and safety professionals are working around the clock to keep Americans moving during what is expected to be the busiest Thanksgiving travel week in 16 years,” FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said in a statement. “The growing demand for air travel makes it more important than ever to continue modernizing our air traffic control system and build a national airspace system for the 21st century.”

The FAA is also advising travelers to get to the airport early and check with their airline for updates if bad weather seems likely to impact their trip. The agency noted the high likelihood of thunderstorms in the Southeast, Mid-Atlantic, and Northeast this week.

Can a Solar Generator Actually Keep Your Lights On During an Outage—or Is It Just Expensive Hype?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Winter storm season is fast approaching, with even Thanksgiving being primed for power outages across the country.

If you’d like to limit its disruption on your everyday life, generators may be top of mind.

As you do your research, you may come across solar generators and wonder how they compare to other options.

“A solar generator uses sunlight as its fuel. It also happens to be clean, quiet, and quick,” explains Ben Zook, founder and owner of Belmont Solar, a solar installation company in Gordonville, PA.

While a solar generator will likely be more expensive upfront, it could be a smart investment, depending on your location and goals.

By understanding how it works as well as its limitations, you can decide whether it’s the right backup solution for your home.

The name “solar generator” is a bit misleading.

This device is actually a portable battery system you can refill with solar panels and use when you don’t have power.

It includes a built-in inverter that converts DC electricity into AC electricity, which most household appliances use. In addition, it might come with USB ports to charge electronic devices such as cellphones.

So, how does the recharging process work?

“Put simply, solar panels convert sunlight into electricity. When sunlight hits the panels, it produces an electric current,” explains Fox Swim, senior industry researcher at Aurora Solar in Nanaimo, British Columbia.

Keep in mind that a solar generator doesn’t provide unlimited power. It’s limited by your battery’s capacity and how much electricity your panels can produce.

Fortunately, solar generators have evolved over the years and started to incorporate more powerful inverters and larger-capacity batteries.

“Some of the more expensive portable solar generators can now be wired into a home’s main wiring with a transfer switch and provide enough power to run a home's critical loads, like refrigerators, lights, and internet modems for multiple hours or even days,” says Daniel Moyer, a senior trainer with Briggs & Stratton Energy Solutions in Arcata, CA.

Portable gas and propane generators are usually cheaper than portable solar generators.

"For a high-quality portable gas or propane generator, budget around $700 to $1,000. Comparable solar generators are going to cost you closer to $1,000 to $1,500,” says Mike Feazel, CEO of Roof Maxx in Westerville, OH.

While portable gas and propane backups could save you some cash, they do come with drawbacks that are important to consider.

“These options are noisy, produce exhaust that must be vented properly, and demand regular maintenance. In addition, they require you to buy and safely store fuel,” Swim says.

Solar generations generally cost more to buy and install, but there are a few reasons for this.

“The panels are quieter as they have no moving parts, require less frequent maintenance, and don’t rely on fuel,” Fox adds.

One of the major downsides of solar generators, however, is that they may produce significantly less electricity during winter, depending on where you live.

Fox’s simulations show just how much production can vary.

In Boise, ID, a 1-kilowatt solar generator produces about 176 kilowatt-hours in a typical June compared to just 66 in December.

In Huntington Beach, CA, the same setup generates 156 kilowatt-hours in June and 127 in December.

So, how do you know whether solar backup is a smart resilience investment or overkill for your needs? It comes down to your unique situation and goals.

To make the most informed decision, ask yourself what you’d need to power if the electricity goes out and how often that would probably happen.

“If you only lose power a couple of times a year and mainly want the fridge, internet, and some lights on, a solar generator makes a lot of sense,” explains Feazel.

However, if your goal is to power your entire home and you live somewhere with frequent, prolonged outages, a solar generator may not be powerful enough. You might be better off with a whole-home generator.

“If you’re unsure, consult a reputable solar installer about your budget and your expectations for backup time and what you need powered during an outage. They can point you in the right direction,” adds Moyer.

Google has filed a patent application for a personalized television channel that uses artificial intelligence to generate shows starring the person watching.

Streaming platforms such as YouTube and Netflix already use viewing habits to recommend content, while companies have also started experimenting with broadcasting entirely AI-generated entertainment.

Google’s patent application, titled “Creating a Channel of Video Experiences Tailored to a User,” was originally filed on May 13, 2025, before being published by the US Patent and Trademark Office on November 19, 2026.

First spotted by Patentlyze, the application describes a feature called “My Channel” that would appear within a television app and offer a selection of videos created specifically for individual viewers.

Hong Kong wants banks to secure new approval before holding customers’ crypto

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The proposed legislation would extend SFC oversight to crypto custodians as financial institutions grapple with security and regulatory challenges.

Hong Kong plans to require banks to register with its securities regulator before offering crypto custody services to customers.

The government published the Virtual Asset Services Legislation (Amendment) Bill 2026 on Nov. 20, expanding its regulatory framework to cover businesses safeguarding, trading, advising on or managing virtual assets.

Under the legislation, banks and stored-value facility operators already supervised by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) would need additional registration with the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) to provide covered virtual asset services. Independent providers would generally need an SFC license.

Hong Kong’s bill would generally require a license for standalone custodians and registration for HKMA-supervised banks and stored value facility licensees.

Both forms of authorization would come from the Securities and Futures Commission.

The bill, scheduled for its first reading in the Legislative Council on Dec. 2, would amend Hong Kong's Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance. Applicants would face fit-and-proper assessments and other regulatory requirements designed to strengthen investor protection.

The additional requirements build on existing regulatory obligations for banks entering the digital asset market.

In May, the HKMA updated its guidance requiring financial institutions offering digital asset custody to maintain appropriate security, governance and operational controls.

Banks intending to launch such services must consult the monetary authority beforehand and demonstrate compliance with its custody standards.

The regulatory expansion comes amid growing institutional interest in digital assets, although security concerns and regulatory uncertainty have slowed adoption.

A February report by Ripple and Quinlan & Associates found that 77% of 65 surveyed Hong Kong financial institutions had either adopted digital assets or planned to do so. However, only 25% had adopted them at the time of the survey.

The research found that 65% of respondents preferred outsourcing custody, rising to 80% among buy-side institutions.

Bank-affiliated custodians attracted the strongest institutional interest, with 64% of respondents expressing willingness to engage them. However, only 40% rated their readiness highly.

Hong Kong's proposed custody framework targets the security risks associated with controlling customers' digital assets.

In their December 2025 consultation conclusions, the SFC and the Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau identified the safeguarding of private keys and similar instruments that enable asset transfers as the central activity requiring authorization.

Regulators said businesses controlling these instruments face technological and operational risks that could result in the loss or unauthorized transfer of customers' assets.

Fund managers and other institutions that delegate custody to third parties without safeguarding private keys would generally fall outside the specific custody licensing requirement.

Similarly, noncustodial wallet providers that let customers retain independent control of their assets would generally not require authorization. Shared-key arrangements would be assessed based on the provider's ability to transfer assets and customers' ability to retain or recover access independently.

The proposed virtual asset custody regime would exclude tokenized securities, which remain subject to existing securities regulations.

Existing crypto custodians could face more immediate compliance pressures once the legislation takes effect.

During the consultation process, some industry participants requested transitional arrangements lasting between three and 18 months, citing potential operational disruptions and the time needed to meet regulatory requirements.

However, authorities rejected automatically granting existing operators temporary authorization, citing concerns about investor protection and confusion over their regulatory status.

Instead, regulators proposed expedited approvals for certain banks, bank subsidiaries and entities associated with licensed crypto trading platforms that had already undergone supervisory assessments.

Authorities also encouraged existing custodians to begin engaging with the SFC or HKMA ahead of the new regime.

While the bill is scheduled to reach the Legislative Council on Dec. 2, the government has yet to announce a commencement date. That decision will determine how much time existing custodians have to secure authorization and avoid disruptions to their businesses.
