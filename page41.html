Apple HomeView Review (2026): Should’ve Been Better

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I never thought Apple would launch a smart display.

Apple has been historically late to the game in the smart-home world, if it bothers showing up at all. It’s had the most minimal smart speaker (and general smart home) offerings of any of the major brands, with just the HomePod and HomePod mini compared with the variety of speakers and smart home products Amazon and Google have launched in the past decade. A limited selection isn't necessarily a bad thing, but the Apple HomePods have also never excelled in our testing, either, and Apple's overall ecosystem has had the most limited compatibility compared with other devices (though Matter is helping expand that).

With the HomeView's arrival, Apple is making its first foray into the smart display category, nearly 10 years after Amazon’s first Echo Show model. While the HomeView manages to do some things well, it's once again a product made for only the biggest of Apple enthusiasts, and will shine best for users who have an iCloud subscription, love Apple Music, and will invest in Apple’s new, larger smart-home ecosystem, which has been designed in collaboration with LG and Schneider Electric. The HomeView does feel handy to use for making calls and sending texts—more so than any Amazon Echo Show ever was—and I love that I can use it to find my iPhone when I lose it around the house.

Still, I'd love to have been more impressed at this price point. After nearly two weeks of using the HomeView in my house, I find I use it most as a smart-home controller and to answer quick questions, and occasionally to FaceTime someone. Siri struggled to complete some of my music requests and took a long time to think about simple questions. Plus, some features on the device overall were roadblocked by my not having a full commitment to iCloud and Apple Photos. It's a device primarily for Apple devotees—especially the ones who will buy all of Apple's new smart-home products.

The most surprising thing to me is how small this device is. Maybe I've been overly influenced by the announcement of Amazon’s Echo Show 16, which has a generous 16-inch display (hence the name), but the HomeView screen is just 7 inches and square, giving it a different look from most other smart displays, which go for a rectangular design. My colleague Julian Chokkattu described it as a “giant Apple Watch,” and I agree. The swiping side to side to switch between 10 different preloaded faces and the menu bar also feel incredibly similar to the Apple Watch.

Pressing and holding on the face of the HomeView allows you to customize your various “faces” (or remove them—it's a lot of options to have preloaded, in my opinion, and I found myself swiping through too frequently to find the smart home page). You can also click on the plus button to add other faces or informational pages like weather and views of your home cameras. But that's all you can add, at least for now. There's no app store, but the device can reflect certain apps from your phone, along with general apps you'd find on an Apple device like Safari, FaceTime, Messages, Find My, Alarms, and more. I was surprised to see Spotify, Libby, and Yoto specifically ported over from my phone, but no other non-Apple apps have made it over so far, at least from my own collection of downloaded apps.

There isn't a Siri AI app on the HomeView like on my iPhone, but the HomeView does use Siri AI to power its intelligence and answer your questions. You'll get a more conversational response than regular Siri, but I didn't find its answers to be that much more impressive. Siri ran into issues playing my music—it asks me whether the music should come from Books, TikTok, or Spotify if I don't specify, and then most of the time it still can't play the song on Spotify, even with my account set up. It also couldn't use Find My to find my iPhone when I asked. Both of these applications worked perfectly fine on the HomeView screen itself, but the on-device Siri isn't blowing me away thus far.

In fact, most things worked better by touch than voice. Spotify voice requests didn't seem to work, but the Spotify app on the device worked fine, as did using the handoff feature to send Spotify music from my phone to the HomeView. The Find My feature also didn't work over voice command—when I called my phone the same name I had seen it referred to on the HomeView while playing music, it claimed I had no device under that name. However, tapping the Find My app on the HomeView let me find my phone in a list of devices and tap it to make it start going off. While I'm bummed Siri won't let me just voice-request it, I am thrilled to have a faster way to find my iPhone lost in my house than pulling up iCloud.com.

I tested the HomeView with both an iPhone Air and an older iPhone 16 Pro, but you'll want a newer phone like the Air (or 17 Pro, 18 Pro, or iPhone Duo) to get the most out of HomeView and Siri, since that's what gives you access to the expressive, customizable Siri voices. Switching to my original phone left me stuck with a boring Siri voice that I can't customize like I could when using the iPhone Air. No matter which phone you use, you'll also need to update your phone's operating system to OS 27.2.1 in order to set up and connect to the HomeView (which also brings Siri AI onto your phone, much to my husband's chagrin).

The main appeal of the HomeView, at least in my opinion, is the fact that it can control your smart-home devices with touch or voice. This is especially relevant since Apple is rolling out a larger smart-home ecosystem, with several devices coming later this year as part of its Apple Home Collection, including indoor and outdoor cameras, a smart thermostat, a video doorbell, a smart lock, and a couple of sensors. You can already control any Apple Home–compatible device on the HomeView, though, without needing Apple's lineup to release.

The HomeView provides a similar experience to other smart displays, with a page you can swipe to for accessing all of the devices set up through your Apple Home app. It's easy to see the devices and quickly control them. Apple Home's biggest issue, though, continues to be compatibility. Many smart devices are only Amazon- and Google-compatible, though Matter is fixing some of that issue; I was able to connect the Ikea Varmblixt ($100) smart lamp to my Apple Home app thanks to the Matter compatibility. It was fast and seamless to control once set up, both over voice and touch.

You can also touch to access your texts and FaceTime your contacts with the HomeView, which has been a pretty smooth experience for me. What's most interesting, though, is how the HomeView will verify that it's only you looking at your text messages. If it sees that someone else in your family is trying to look at or access your texts, it'll prompt that family member to switch over to their own account. It only works if they're alone in range of the device's camera, though; if they're peeking over your shoulder, access won't be denied.

Apple isn't using FaceID for this, surprisingly enough. It uses biometric data to figure out who's asking via a protected part of the processor Apple calls the Secure Exclave. It's a new technology that's also on the Apple Watch; we'll have to see how it pans out in real time.

Of course, we can't talk about a smart display and speaker without talking about one of the key features: the speaker.

Sound is a big part of the enjoyment from this device, and while I enjoyed the audio quality, I found it frustrating to voice-request a song. When I asked for music without prompting which app it should come from, Siri asked me if I'd like it to come from Books, TikTok, or Spotify. And when I would request a song on Spotify, Siri would say I needed to “unlock my device,” but didn't specify if that needed to happen on my iPhone or my HomeView. Or it would simply tell me there was a problem and tell me to try again.

This is a huge bummer for my day-to-day use: I have a 4-year-old who loves making his own music requests and has gotten excellent at asking Google to play his latest favorite song (we're finally coming off of him requesting “Ghostbusters” and "This Is Halloween" on a daily basis).

Ultimately, the easiest way to play music as a Spotify user was using the handoff feature. The HomeView has a proximity-sharing feature similar to when you tap two iPhones to AirDrop a photo or other information, and it was much, much easier to pull up a song on my iPhone and simply place it near the HomeView to send what I was listening to to the speaker. I did really like that experience, but I think Spotify users will prefer other smart speakers if music playback is a primary feature they’re looking for.

The sound itself was pretty good; I was happy jamming from a range of music, whether an autumn lo-fi mix or “The Call of the Mountains,” by Eluveitie. My long-time favorite song, “Dark Blue,” by Jack's Mannequin, sounded as clear with its piano keys and background drums as it did back in 2007 when I first started listening to it. The HomeView’s audio comes from the cylinder-like base that's only about 3 inches wide, giving it a slimmer profile than most smart display bases and even just stand-alone small smart speakers (the Google Home Speaker is about 5 inches at its widest point, for comparison). Inside that base sit two full-range drivers, a down-firing woofer that promises Spatial Audio, and two force-canceling passive radiators to build out the sound.

So, would I tell you to buy this? Only if you're a total Apple devotee who would use all of your preferred, Apple-run apps in tandem with the Apple HomeView. But it's really best if you're looking to invest in an Apple-run smart home (or already have one) and want a touchscreen to control it with. Or love FaceTiming so much you want an extra device for it. Otherwise, you'd be just as happy with cheaper smart displays or a simple smart speaker.

Scattered Spider Hacker Sentenced to 6 Years Over $11.6M in Stolen Crypto

Scattered Spider Hacker Sentenced to 6 Years Over $11.6M in Stolen Crypto

A Scottish hacker who was part of the Scattered Spider hacking collective has been sentenced to six years in U.S. federal prison for his role in a scheme that stole at least $11.6 million in crypto.

Tyler Robert Buchanan, 24, of Dundee, was sentenced to 72 months by a federal judge in Santa Ana, California, on Tuesday, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Central District of California said. The judge set a restitution hearing for December 11. Buchanan began hacking as a teenager and was part of Scattered Spider, the group of young, English-speaking hackers blamed for a string of high-profile cyberattacks, the BBC reported.

Buchanan pleaded guilty in April to conspiracy to commit wire fraud and aggravated identity theft. At the time, prosecutors said the scheme had stolen at least $8 million in crypto. That figure has since risen to at least $11.6 million.

Between September 2021 and April 2023, Buchanan and his co-conspirators sent hundreds of phishing text messages to employees of at least a dozen companies, including telecoms, technology firms and crypto companies, according to the indictment and Buchanan's plea agreement. The messages linked to fake websites that captured employees' login details, which were sent to a Telegram channel run by Buchanan and another co-conspirator.

The group used information stolen from those companies to identify individual crypto holders, then carried out SIM swaps, tricking mobile carriers into moving victims' phone numbers to devices they controlled so they could intercept two-factor authentication codes and empty victims' wallets.

In the largest single theft, one victim lost $1.67 million in December 2022, according to the BBC. When authorities searched Buchanan's home in Scotland in April 2023, they found the names and addresses of numerous victims, and a text file containing crypto seed phrases and login details for one victim's account, prosecutors said.

"Those thefts had severe, life-altering consequences for many victims," prosecutors argued in a sentencing memo.

Buchanan is the fifth and final defendant to be sentenced in the case, which the Justice Department charged in November 2024. A Justice Department spokesperson confirmed at the time that the defendants were suspected members of Scattered Spider, the group behind high-profile attacks including the 2023 breach of MGM Resorts. Buchanan was arrested at Palma de Mallorca Airport in Spain in May 2024 while trying to board a private flight to Naples, the BBC reported, and has been in U.S. custody since April 2025.

His co-defendant Noah Urban, of Palm Coast, Florida, is serving 10 years in prison, while Ahmed Hossam Eldin Elbadawy and Evans Onyeaka Osiebo were each sentenced to 45 months, and Joel Martin Evans to two years.

U.S. authorities have continued to pursue alleged Scattered Spider members. In July, a 19-year-old suspect was extradited from Finland over an $8 million crypto ransom demand against a luxury jeweler.

The Best Photos From NASA’s Voyager 1 Spacecraft, Which Is Now 1 Light-Day From Earth

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Best Photos From NASA’s Voyager 1 Spacecraft, Which Is Now 1 Light-Day From Earth

NASA’s Voyager 1 spacecraft, which launched on September 5, 1977, is officially one light-day away from Earth.

Now about 16 billion miles (around 26 billion kilometers) from Earth, Voyager 1 is the first human-made object to reach this distance. While space enthusiasts are very familiar with the concept of a light-year, a “light-day” is far less commonly used. However, that doesn’t make it any less impressive.

To put Voyager 1’s accomplishment into perspective, if someone flashed a light on Earth right now, it would take an entire day to reach the spacecraft. By comparison, light needs just over 130 milliseconds to circle the globe and about 500 seconds to travel from the Sun—which sits nearly 93 million miles (about 150 million kilometers) away.

Voyager 1 is at a truly mind-boggling distance from Earth. As NASA puts it, “Nothing in the universe travels faster than light. Moving through space about 186,000 miles every second, it crosses enormous distances in a given time period, whether it’s a second, a day, or a year.”

NASA also shared other interesting light-distances. The Moon is 1.3 light-seconds away, even though it took Artemis II astronauts about three days to get there. Neptune, the most distant planet in the Solar System (sorry, Pluto), is about four light-hours and 10 light-minutes away.

Voyager 1 will continue traveling toward AC +79 3888, a star also known as Gliese 445. This obscure star lies more than 17 light-years away, and Voyager 1 is projected to pass within 1.7 light-years of it in a staggering 40,000 years—despite the spacecraft’s cruising speed of nearly 38,000 miles per hour (61,000 kilometers per hour).

As for Voyager 1’s sibling spacecraft, Voyager 2, it will reach one light-day from Earth in November 2035. Despite its name and current position behind Voyager 1, Voyager 2 actually launched a couple of weeks earlier. However, Voyager 1 took a faster path through the Solar System. Voyager 2 will never catch up.

It’s unclear just how far Voyager 1 will ultimately travel. Theoretically, it will never stop as long as the universe exists. While interstellar dust could slow the spacecraft or potentially even grind it down to nothing, Voyager 1 would have to hit a lot of dust just to cut its speed in half. Something human beings made, touched with their own hands, and launched into space will travel out into the cosmos forever. Humanity will never truly know what happens to Voyager 1, though, as communications are expected to be lost in about 10 years.

Why Qantas Picked A 'Proven' Rolls-Royce Engine Over A Theoretically Better One For Project Sunrise

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ultra-long-haul flights have exploded in popularity over the past decade, largely because the current generation of long-range widebodies is exceptionally fuel-efficient. Recent advances in efficiency and capability are now allowing Qantas to launch Project Sunrise, two ultra-long-haul nonstop routes that will both be longer than today's longest flights (Singapore to New York JFK and Newark) by a wide margin.

The longest of these, Sydney to London Heathrow, will last over 20 hours. Qantas placed its official order for the Project Sunrise aircraft in 2022, selecting the Airbus A350-1000ULR after an intense competition between the A350 and the Boeing 777X.

What's unusual is that the 777X has been relatively popular while being equipped with the world's most advanced jet engines. However, Qantas still selected the older A350-1000 to operate the world's longest flights, a move that solidifies the Airbus A350's position as the world's ultimate long-range airliner.

The core of Project Sunrise is the launch of new ultra-long-haul nonstop flights connecting Sydney to London Heathrow and New York JFK. The longer of the two, Sydney to London Heathrow, will be 9,188 NM (17,016 km), and will also be the first Project Sunrise route to launch in October 2027.

The first A350-1000ULR will arrive in April and will operate short-haul crew familiarization flights, while the London route will launch once Qantas has three examples to hand. Sydney to New York will be the second route to launch, and Qantas will subsequently place the A350-1000ULR throughout its current route network, mainly displacing Boeing 787s to launch new routes.

While Qantas has previously stated that it will also connect Melbourne to London and New York, these routes appear to be less of a priority based on Qantas's most recent announcements. They may only come with the final A350-1000ULR deliveries as the carrier increases its focus on Sydney. Flight times from Sydney to London will last over 20 hours, or about two hours longer than the Singapore-JFK service.

Some have expressed skepticism over whether passengers would prefer a 20-hour nonstop over a 24-hour one-stop route. However, there was similar skepticism a decade ago about the commercial viability of 17 to 18-hour flights, like Singapore-JFK or Qantas's own Perth-London route. By and large, these routes have proven popular with passengers and have become quite lucrative.

Technically, the Airbus A350-900ULR could likely operate the Project Sunrise routes, but Qantas desired more capability. The A350-900ULR is a fairly minimal modification to the A350-900, featuring weight reductions and software changes to enable more fuel to be carried in the plane's center tank. With a deactivated forward cargo hold, it essentially trades payload for fuel.

Qantas desired a larger aircraft that could carry significantly more payload than the A350-900ULR. Airbus offered the A350-1000ULR, while Boeing offered the 777X. Initial discussions centered around an optimized version of the smaller 777-8. It's unclear what exactly Boeing planned for the 777-8, but as delays with the program continued, the 777-9 also came into the picture.

While the plane's current brochure range is too short for Project Sunrise, the 777-9 could have received a higher gross weight and auxiliary fuel tanks to make the route, albeit with a reduced capacity. It's generally believed that this was offered as an interim solution until the 777-8 was ready, and it was even reported that Boeing also offered the 777-200LR as another alternative solution.

Ultimately, Qantas announced the A350 as the preferred choice for Project Sunrise in 2019 for flights to commence in 2023, and then placed its official order in 2022 (the order was delayed due to the COVID-19 pandemic). The timeline is crucial because the 777X is one of the most delayed aircraft programs in recent history.

At 10,573 miles (17,015 km), the journey is no mean feat.

The Boeing 777X will be exclusively powered by the General Electric GE9X. While the Rolls-Royce Trent XWB on the A350 is a remarkable engine, the GE9X will be unrivaled in terms of thermal efficiency, in large part due to the extensive use of advanced materials like ceramic matrix composites. It can almost be considered half a generation ahead of the Trent XWB/Trent 1000/General Electric GEnx.

However, the GE9X and the 777X are also unproven. Qantas's desired timeline for Project Sunrise would have made it one of the first operators of the 777X. While General Electric has a proven track record in delivering dependable large turbofan engines, any new engine experiences teething issues upon introduction, which can be problematic to deal with.

​​​​​​​What's more, early production of the GE9X and the 777X will be limited (as is the case with any new aircraft), resulting in slow deliveries during the 777X's first few years. In comparison, the Trent XWB is a proven engine that has been remarkably trouble-free, while the A350 is an established type.

While deliveries of the A350 have currently been slow due to supply-chain constraints, this has been affecting the entire airline industry and is hardly unique to the A350. As a whole, it's less risky to go with the aircraft and engine type that has been in service for nearly a decade compared to the brand-new plane/engine.

The GE9X will be an excellent engine once it enters service, but this doesn't mean that the 777X will burn less fuel than the A350-1000. The aircraft still retains a heavy-metal fuselage, while also featuring massive wings. It's a fundamentally larger, heavier plane that will be significantly thirstier than the A350-1000, and the 777-9 addresses this through its massive capacity, giving it competitive per-seat economics.

The 777-8, however, is smaller than either, while costing almost the same to operate as the 777-9, making it an uneconomical aircraft. The advantage of the 777-8 is its field performance and range, but for Project Sunrise, the A350-1000 is more than capable with the ULR improvements.

As a whole, the 777-8 has been an extremely slow seller due to these issues. In addition, the 777X's delays were also very problematic. In 2019 (the same year as when Qantas publicly selected the A350), Boeing planned to start delivering the 777-9 in 2021, while the 777-8 was delayed to 2023, the same year that Qantas intended to start Project Sunrise flights at the time. At the time, there were already concerns about whether the timeline would stick.

These concerns have proven valid, as the 777-9's entry into service is now set for 2027. Meanwhile, development of the 777-8 has been paused due to a lack of demand, with Boeing instead focusing on the 777-9 and then the 777-8F, as these models are more popular. For Qantas, relying on the 777-8 would have been significantly riskier than going with a derivative of the in-service A350-1000.

A key factor the airline can capitalize on is that Qantas will be the only carrier to offer non-stop services on these competitive routes.

While it has the same 'ULR' designation as the A350-900ULR, the engineering to create the A350-1000ULR is closer to how Airbus created the A321XLR. Key to the plane's range is the addition of a new 20,000-liter integrated Rear Centre Tank, derived from the design of the RCT on the A321XLR. In addition, the A350-1000ULR features a higher Maximum Takeoff Weight of 322 tons.

This will be available on all future A350-1000s. However, Qantas has an exclusivity deal on the A350-1000ULR variant specifically. To further extend the plane's range, Qantas is installing just 238 seats, making it the lowest-capacity A350-1000 configuration in the world. There will be six first class suites built by Safran, 52 business class seats based on the Safran Unity, 40 premium economy seats based on the Safran Z535i, and just 140 economy seats based on the Recaro R3.

In addition, the A350-1000ULR will also come with a new crew rest facility for the pilots, of which there will be four on each flight. Some have questioned Qantas's decision to install economy seats on the A350-1000ULR, considering that Singapore Airlines' A350-900ULRs lack this cabin.

However, Qantas is catering to a much larger leisure and visiting friends/relatives market than Singapore Airlines, and it specifically bought the A350-1000ULR for its size as well as its payload capacity. Economy was always part of the plan, and Qantas will be configuring the economy seats with more legroom while also offering an onboard 'Wellness Zone.'

While VOO and SCHD both pay dividends, one produces three times as much income per dollar invested.

While the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD -1.53%) is a fraction of VOO's size, with almost $123 billion in AUM, it's the largest ETF focused on dividend stocks.

Both of these top ETFs pay dividends, making them potential options for investors seeking to generate passive income. Here's a look at how much you'd need to invest in each one to generate around $1,000 of dividend income each month.

The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF tracks the S&P 500 Index, which measures the return of the 500 largest publicly traded U.S. companies. Many of these companies pay dividends, which VOO collects and then distributes to investors each quarter. For example, its top holding is Apple, which currently pays a quarterly dividend of $0.27 per share. However, while many of its holdings pay dividends, the ETF currently has a relatively low yield of around 1%, matching the S&P 500's dividend yield. That's because many of its holdings have lower dividend yields (Apple's is a meager 0.3%).

At the ETF's current yield of around 1%, you'd need to invest almost $1.2 million into VOO to generate $12,000 in dividend income each year, or around $1,000 per month. That's a hefty investment. On a positive note, the dividend income should grow. The S&P 500 has delivered mid-single-digit annualized dividend growth over the past three years.

The Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF focuses specifically on dividend-paying stocks. It tracks an index (Dow Jones U.S. Dividend 100 Index) that screens companies based on several dividend-quality characteristics, including yield, five-year dividend growth rate, and financial strength. As a result, the fund holds 100 high-quality, high-yielding dividend growth stocks.

The ETF currently has a dividend yield of around 3%, based on its payments over the last 12 months. Thanks to its much higher yield, you can generate more income with less capital from SCHD. For example, you'd only need to invest around $400,000 to generate $12,000 of dividend income each year to hit the monthly target of $1,000.

Further, this ETF should deliver faster dividend growth in the future, due to its dual focus on yield and dividend growth. Over the last five years, its average holding has delivered more than 9% annual dividend growth. However, dividend growth has slowed considerably over the past year, with SCHD's payout rising only about 2% over the last 12 months.

VOO and SCHD both provide investors with passive dividend income. However, thanks to its currently higher yield, you'd need to invest one-third as much capital in SCHD to generate the same amount of dividend income. That makes it the better option for investors seeking to maximize their passive income.

SAP opens AI to on-prem customers, but cloud commitments could shut smaller firms out

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

User group says access terms favor enterprises that can migrate one ERP system at a time

SAP's commercial terms could put its latest AI tools out of reach for many customers, particularly smaller businesses running a single ERP system, the new chair of its German-speaking user group has warned.

Thomas Henzler, appointed chair of DSAG last month, told The Register that Joule Studio 2.0 – SAP's upgraded platform for developing and deploying AI agents to automate business tasks – is a leap forward.

Yet DSAG, which represents SAP users in Germany, Switzerland, and Austria, remains in talks with the vendor about the commercial conditions attached to access.

In July 2023, CEO Christian Klein told investors that SAP's "newest innovations and capabilities" will only be delivered in the public or private cloud using RISE with SAP, the vendor's lift-shift-and-transform program launched with partners and cloud providers in early 2021. Customers running ERP software on-premises, or hosting it with a hyperscaler outside RISE, will miss out, he said.

However, in May this year, SAP appeared to perform a U-turn. It said on-premises customers could access Joule 2.0 if they commit to a cloud "journey" through RISE with SAP, changing their support and commercial arrangements while migration projects are still underway.

Henzler told The Reg: "There is a gap between the customer's reality and SAP's strategy for the autonomous enterprise. A lot of customers are still planning to migrate to S/4HANA, and now the question is, 'what do we have to do to use SAP AI, and what does it mean regarding the cloud strategy?'

"Now it's a completely new strategy, a new approach, and a new underlying technology. The question is, what does it mean for existing projects, S/4HANA migrations, cloud migrations and so on? Is it necessary to rethink all the projects? Is there a big shift in the technology and architectures?"

The DSAG chair said the biggest challenge in connecting legacy on-prem systems, such as ECC, to the new Joule suite is the commercial model.

"Access requires a substantial RISE commitment and a minimum cloud-to-on-premise maintenance ratio. In practice, this creates a problem for customers with only one ECC system. To meet the cloud threshold, they have to convert their entire on-premises maintenance into a cloud subscription, which defeats the purpose of an on-premises connector. Consequently, the model only works for large enterprises with multiple SAP ERP systems that can, for example, migrate one system after the other to unlock AI for the rest," he said.

"In the end, to get the benefit you have to start a RISE with SAP migration. Therefore, the connector doesn't make sense for the majority of SME customers. From my perspective, this approach works only for large customers with multiple ERP systems, for example, if they take one system, move it to the cloud, and then another one. Then they can use AI, because they reach the needed ratio to use the on-prem connector. That is more or less a commercial approach. We need a solution for all customers, independent of the commercials."

The Register asked SAP to respond to Henzler's concerns. A spokesperson said: "The offering separates a customer's commercial commitment to cloud ERP from the timing of its operational migration. Customers that meet the applicable commercial and technical requirements can use supported AI capabilities with eligible ECC or on-premises SAP S/4HANA systems while modernizing their landscape. Eligibility is not determined by the number of ERP instances and can therefore also extend to single-instance customers."

There are thousands of SAP ERP users worldwide, including some of the world's largest businesses. Despite efforts to get them onto the latest iteration – S/4HANA – and in the cloud, where the vendor promised it could gather a greater "share of wallet" from customers, there has been a dogged resistance to these complex and time-consuming migrations.

Customers still running older ERP systems face pressure to embrace AI-supported automation. Whether SAP's terms help them do that – or simply help move them onto RISE – remains the question. ®
