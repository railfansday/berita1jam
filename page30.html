Gnosis Pay breach exposes card details, personal data and crypto wallet information

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The crypto debit card provider said customer funds remained safe despite the exposure of sensitive financial and personal information.

Crypto-linked Visa debit card provider Gnosis Pay disclosed a security breach on Friday that exposed customers’ payment card details, home addresses and cryptocurrency wallet addresses.

Gnosis Pay said it detected unauthorized access to its legacy consumer card system on Nov. 3 and began containing the breach within two hours. The company said customer funds and its separate enterprise platform, which powers white-label card programs, were unaffected.

According to breach notifications reviewed by Cointelegraph, the company identified exposed information including full payment card numbers, expiration dates, CVV codes, customer names, home addresses and wallet addresses linked to affected accounts. The compromised data also included email addresses, phone numbers, nationalities and, in some cases, banking details such as IBAN and BIC information.

Gnosis Pay customer shares a breach notification detailing exposed personal and financial data. Source: Lefteris Karapetsas

One notification also confirmed that the customer’s card was canceled on Nov. 3, with no suspicious transactions or movement of funds detected.

Gnosis Pay said it replaced compromised security credentials, addressed the vulnerability and made 3D Secure verification mandatory for online payments until the legacy system’s planned retirement on Dec. 20. It also warned customers about phishing attempts using their personal or transaction details and said it was arranging monitoring for potential misuse of the stolen data.

According to data from OpenRate, a third-party platform tracking crypto card activity, Gnosis Pay had around 2,400 daily active cardholders and processed $263,200 in transactions over the previous 24 hours.

The company did not disclose how many customers were affected and did not immediately respond to Cointelegraph’s request for comment.

10 screen addiction ideas on forms, harms and fixes, from one explainer

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Ten ideas on screen addiction from one explainer: the forms it takes, the harms to eyes, sleep and mood, and ways to cut back or get help.

Most people know the small routine of putting a phone down in irritation and picking it back up a minute later. Phone use has become a reflex as well as a block of time. U.S. adults' screen time jumped an estimated 60% to 80% during the pandemic, according to a review reported by TIME in 2022. People reach for their phones more than 100 times each day, according to TIME. That constant reaching helps explain why people call the habit an addiction in ordinary conversation, even though the word covers everything from a bad habit to a clinical disorder.

Most heavy phone use never reaches a diagnosis. A clinical diagnosis of tech addiction would apply to a small group of teens, between 2% and 4%, according to an estimate reported by Psychology Today. For most people, then, the useful question is whether their screen use is causing harm, not whether it deserves a medical label. Those harms do not all follow the same measure. For the eyes, and to some degree for sleep, the hours spent at a screen appear to do damage on their own. For mood, the reason someone picks up a screen and what they do once they are on it matter more than the time it takes. That difference gives a test for any coping advice. A cap on daily hours protects against some harms and misses others entirely.

Addiction Center set out to explain screen addiction in its several forms, from phone dependence to gaming, along with the damage it does, such as lost sleep and depression, and the ways people can cope, down to the common question of when daily screen use becomes excessive. For each form of the habit and each harm, a reader can ask whether the hours or the compulsion is doing the damage. The same question applies to every suggested fix. Each one either limits the time spent on a screen or addresses the urge that keeps a hand reaching for it, and the right choice depends on which of the two is causing the harm.

Dependence on the phone is a mass problem before it is a personal one. Compulsive reliance on phones is growing, most of all among younger generations, and the world counts 6.92 billion smartphone users, according to Addiction Center. With a device in that many hands, even a small rate of dependence reaches a great many people. Heavy use is also ordinary among students. Semmelweis University reports that about one third of the students aged 18 to 35 in its study were heavy users who mostly consumed content online. A count of users only shows who holds a phone, though. It cannot show who has lost control of one.

Dependence shows in what someone does, not in who they are. "Our main finding is that personality alone -- such as a high level of neuroticism -- does not make someone addicted to their phone," Johanna Takács, a senior research fellow in the Faculty of Health Sciences at Semmelweis University, said. The behavior to watch is someone taking out the phone where reaching for it has a price. Addiction Center names work meetings and driving as examples of dangerous use. In both settings a person loses nothing by waiting and can lose a great deal by looking down. Readers can check this sign in themselves by asking whether they take out the phone in moments when they would rather not.

For this form of the habit, the moments when someone reaches for the phone without deciding to matter more than the hours on a screen-time report, and any fix has to aim at that reflex rather than the clock.

Internet addiction shows in what web use pushes aside, not in how long a browser stays open. Addiction Center describes compulsive web use as a habit that disrupts a person's responsibilities, bringing anxiety, depression, isolation and weaker performance at school or work. Read as a list, those costs look like separate side effects of one cause, and the easy conclusion is that more hours online bring more of each. Psychiatric researchers who studied how the habit connects to mood found a different pattern.

Researchers at the Mental Health Center of Yangtze University studied junior high students in their own city of Jingzhou, China. They found that teens who would rather interact online than go out with others were the ones whose web habits were tied to losing interest and pleasure, according to Frontiers. That makes isolation a path into low mood rather than a separate harm. Escape showed up as the second link: students who used the web to block out upsetting thoughts were the ones most directly tied to feeling down. In both cases the harm follows why a teen goes online, to hide from people or from thoughts, rather than how long the teen stays there.

Among ordinary students, the tie between web habits and low mood is real but faint. Nervousness and feeling down were bound far more tightly to each other than either was to how the students used the web, according to Frontiers. Anxiety and depression are possible costs of compulsive web use, not certain ones, and the typical student in the study reported no symptoms of either. The findings capture a single moment, so they can't show which comes first. Compulsive use may lead to low mood, or teens who already feel low may go online to escape it, and each may feed the other.

If the harm comes through escape and withdrawal, a parent who sets a time limit is aiming at the wrong target. Useful help would teach teens better ways to handle upsetting feelings and help them rebuild the offline company they had been replacing with the web.

The question to ask about anyone's internet use is what they are using it to avoid, not how many hours it takes.

Gaming addiction has a clinical name of its own. Addiction Center reports that the DSM-5 classifies the habit as internet gaming disorder. That gives parents and players a medical term to work with, and the signs behind it describe how a person behaves, not how many hours the console stays on.

The signs of the disorder center on what a player gives up for the game and how the player reacts when it is threatened. A player with the disorder lets the game come before a job, schoolwork and plans with other people, and grows short-tempered with anyone who complains about it or tries to limit it. The clearest sign is a player who keeps up chronic gaming despite the harm it is doing. Together these signs point to compulsion, because the play has turned into something the person feels a need to protect. A long weekend session is not the alarm. Parents and players should look at what the game is pushing aside and whether stopping it starts a fight.

The disorder reaches past the teenage years into young adulthood. Medical Xpress reports that about 6.1% of 18- to 35-year-olds have it, and the share climbs to 8.1% when studies look only at people who game. People who play regularly face higher odds than the overall figure suggests. Researchers have not settled on a firm rate, however. Individual studies have put it anywhere from 0% to 63%, so any single number is a rough estimate.

With the rates that uncertain, a player's own behavior is a surer guide than any percentage.

People who want one number for "too much" screen time will find that the clearest one concerns the eyes, not the mind. Looking at a screen for two or more continuous hours a day carries the highest risk of computer vision syndrome, according to American Optometric Association guidance reported by Addiction Center. The danger lies in long, unbroken stretches of close focus rather than in the hours a person adds up over a day. Someone who picks up a phone in many short bursts may face less risk than someone who sits through one long session. For the eyes, the practical step is to break up the stretch, not to cut the daily total. That rule is real, but it covers only one part of the body and says nothing about mood.

When the question turns to mental well-being, experts reject the clock as the measure. Dimitri Christakis, a pediatrician and epidemiologist who directs a center at Seattle Children's Research Institute, sees the daily total as the wrong yardstick. "We can't simply count all screen time as the same," Christakis told TIME. An hour spent on one activity is not the same as an hour spent on another. That helps explain why experts have not settled on one daily limit for adults: the total hides what the time was spent doing.

Where researchers have put numbers on screens and mental health, the numbers limit one kind of use rather than screens as a whole. TIME reports that in a 2018 study, Melissa Hunt of the University of Pennsylvania's psychology department capped college students' social media use at 30 minutes a day over a three-week period. That test covered one category of apps, and results from students may not hold for all adults. Hunt's own advice is narrow as well, since she considers an hour of social media a day probably the best target. The most specific figure experts offer applies to a single use, and it is modest. It is not a cap on screens overall.

The useful question is which hours, spent doing what. Break up long stretches to protect the eyes, and limit particular uses to protect the mood.

Strain on the eyes is the cost of heavy screen use that many people are likely to feel. Between 50% and 90% of those whose jobs keep them at a computer daily report eye strain, according to Addiction Center. The range is wide, but even its low end shows that eye strain is a common complaint among people who spend their workdays at a screen, not a rare one. Strain that continues over a long period can lead to lasting problems with vision. Eye strain is therefore a harm that daily screen users have reason to take seriously, whether or not they would ever meet a clinical bar for addiction.

Eye strain depends on how long someone looks at a screen, not on why they look. The people most affected are those who sit at screens for work, and few would call answering email or building a spreadsheet a compulsion or an escape. They develop sore eyes anyway. A report due Friday and a late-night scroll through social media put the same strain on the eyes.

This is where the habit and the hours separate. A person can have a healthy relationship with their phone, pick it up only when they mean to, put it down easily and still end up with tired, aching eyes after long days at a monitor. Treating their screen use as an addiction would do little for them, since the time itself causes their trouble.

For the body, the clock matters more than the motive.

Of all the worries about screens, depression is the most personal, because it changes how someone lives, not just how tired their eyes feel. It is the mental health condition most often linked to screen addiction, and it is the harm where a daily number seems to matter. The risk is higher once screen use passes four hours a day, according to a 2017 study in Preventive Medicine Reports reported by Addiction Center. That gives readers a concrete line to measure their own days against, though it marks where the risk rises rather than where harm begins. The finding shows that heavy use and depression appear together, not that the hours alone cause it. Crossing four hours is a reason to look more closely at the habit, not proof that the screen is doing the damage.

What a screen replaces in a day deserves as much attention as how long it stays on. Adults who spend too much time on screens also tend to report anxiety and isolation, according to TIME, a pattern of the two showing up together. If heavy use and isolation go hand in hand, part of the risk of depression may lie in the company a screen pushes out of someone's day. In research on screen use and depression in adults, women showed higher rates than men, which gives women more reason to watch for the pattern. For anyone counting hours, the more useful question is whether the screen has started to take the place of other people.

A short time outdoors is a free way to improve mood, and it takes less time than a lunch break. Jean Case, a former AOL executive and former National Geographic chairman, writes in TIME that as little as 10 to 20 minutes spent in a natural setting can do the mind some good. Those benefits involve mood, stress and thinking, not treatment for depression. A walk can help someone feel better, but it cannot cure the illness.

A time limit and a walk outside can each help, but lasting depression calls for more than either.

Sleep is where a screen habit costs the body most directly, because the price is a night that starts later. Addiction Center reports that exposure to blue light delays melatonin and that users stay alert on stimulating content, which pushes sleep later. In that account, the light and the material carry the blame, not the minutes. Poor sleep is common among young people in school, and 33.8% of the students in a Norwegian study reported symptoms of insomnia, according to Frontiers. If light and content were the main cause, dimmer screens and calmer apps would be the fix, and that assumption is worth testing.

The minutes appear to matter more than the material. Frontiers reports that the tie between time on a screen in bed and both insomnia and hours of sleep did not change with the activity the Norwegian students chose. Researchers at the Norwegian Institute of Public Health count lost sleep time among the ways screens may disturb sleep, along with light, arousal and notifications. A link that stays the same whatever is on the screen fits lost sleep time best. Sleep was in fact better among students whose time in bed went entirely to social media. Of them, 27.6% reported insomnia symptoms, compared with 37.1% of students who spent that time on other screen activities. Social media is the kind of stimulating content that would be expected to keep people awake, yet it did not go with the worst sleep. That result doesn't prove feeds are harmless, but it leaves the question of content open.

Even the link to time stops short of proving that screens cause sleepless nights. The Norwegian data show only that screen time in bed and poor sleep go together, according to Frontiers. People who already sleep badly may reach for a phone to fill the hours they lie awake. The students all lived in one country, and the pattern may not hold elsewhere.

The practical step follows the clock rather than the app. Since the students' choice of activity made no difference, putting the phone down sooner after getting into bed is a better bet than swapping one app for another. Yet the advice on insomnia carries no hours figure, unlike the advice on depression and stress. That leaves readers without a number for the harm where time seems to matter most.

At bedtime, the question worth asking is how long the phone stays on after lights out, more than which app is open.

Screen stress depends less on how long someone stays on a screen than on why they picked it up. A 2019 study in the Journal of Medical Internet Research by Khalili-Mahani, Smyrnova and Kakinami found that people felt 19% more stress during entertainment and social screen use than during professional use, according to Addiction Center. The scrolling people do to relax is the scrolling that strains them most. "Some people claim to use screen time to zone out and take a break from the day’s stressors, but the reality may be that this time is a source of stress," Amber Biello-Taylor, a licensed clinical social worker, said. A time cap does little for this harm. What helps is changing what someone does on the screen in the moments they turn to it for relief.

Plenty of Americans feel no such strain from their phones. Pew Research Center reports that 51% of U.S. adults find the device does nothing to their mood in either direction. The extra stress from leisure use describes how some people use their screens, not what owning a phone does to everyone. A reader shouldn't assume the phone is the cause of their stress. The better check is whether their own downtime scrolling has started to feel like a chore.

Young adults report both heavy phone use and harm to their mood. Pew Research Center reports that among Americans ages 18 to 29, 31% feel worse because of their phone, while 42% are on it almost constantly. Those answers alone don't show that leisure scrolling is what lowers their mood. Still, for a young adult who is on the phone nearly all day, the finding on entertainment and social use offers a place to start: asking whether the time meant for unwinding has become another source of strain.

Every way of cutting back offered here works by limiting time. Addiction Center recommends three steps: regular 20-20-20 breaks, a daily limit on personal screen time and a digital detox. With them, a reader can turn a vague worry about screens into steps to measure and track. Limits on minutes and hours suit the harms that build up with time at a screen, such as strained eyes, better than the harms to mood. The break is aimed at the eyes, not at the habit. The American Optometric Association's 20-20-20 rule has people at a screen shift their gaze to a point at least 20 feet off, giving eyes held at close range a rest. It takes no willpower at all.

The daily limit is a target a reader can adjust, not a firm medical line. The recommended cap of 30 minutes of personal screen time a day rests on "many studies" that go unnamed, and the change may be drastic for some people. For anyone who finds that too steep, one hour a day of personal screen use is offered as a looser ceiling. The same advice on time limits includes a screen-free break of three to four hours, short enough to fit into an ordinary day. A reader can choose the limit that fits work, family and the rest of the day. Staying above 30 minutes does not mean the effort to cut back has failed.

No length is set for the digital detox, which leaves each reader to decide how long to step away. None of these steps asks why a person picks up the phone in the first place. Someone following them counts the minutes but never has to look at the reason behind each reach.

If the hours drop but the reaching doesn't, the trouble lies in the pull itself, and cutting back has done all it can.

Treatment for a screen habit rarely rests on a single fix, and that is by design. Recovery usually blends counseling, peer groups and changes to daily habits because needs differ from person to person, according to Addiction Center. Each part of that blend has its own target. Habit changes, such as setting device-free hours, cut the time spent on a screen, while counseling and peer groups address the urge that keeps someone reaching for one. The right mix depends on which harm a person faces. Someone losing sleep needs different help from someone who uses screens to escape distress, even if both would call themselves addicted.

Counseling for this problem has a concrete form taken from addiction care. Psychology Today reports that for people whose use of technology has become an addiction, Petros Levounis, professor and chair of psychiatry at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School, favors motivational interviewing and cognitive behavioral therapy. A reader can ask a clinician for those methods by name. Both treat heavy screen use as a compulsive behavior rather than a scheduling problem. Someone who only sets a limit may cut the hours and keep the urge that drives the harm to mood, and therapy is built to address that urge.

Taking the phone away has a place, but a narrow one. Phone-free hours cut time on a screen, so they help most against harms tied to the clock, such as lost sleep. Removing phones for good is a different matter. "I don't think the answer will ever be to take away their phones completely and never let them access social media, because it is here to stay, and that kind of action would not be realistic,” Jessi Gold, chief wellness officer of the University of Tennessee System, said, as reported by Psychology Today. Set phone-free hours and a complete ban sit far apart, and a family has to choose a point between them. That range of choices is part of why no single recovery rule fits everyone.

The most useful question to bring to any treatment plan is whether it targets the hours or the habit behind them, and for the habit, help comes from a therapist or a group rather than a timer.

That on-court figure combines ATP prize money and a tour bonus. It is the best on-court total ever recorded on the Forbes list. His overall earnings put him about $4 million ahead of Carlos Alcaraz, who led the list for the previous two years.

Sinner is just the fourth player to reach No. 1 in the list's 19 years. Roger Federer held first place for 15 consecutive years, a streak that ended when he retired in 2022. Novak Djokovic took the top spot in 2023.

The prize money follows a long winning run. Sinner won Wimbledon in July 2026 for his fifth Grand Slam title, beating Alexander Zverev in the final. Across three seasons, he has a 175-15 record in ATP singles matches. That stretch includes a record 34 consecutive wins at Masters 1000 events.

A knee injury has ruled him out of the 2026 U.S. Open. He keeps the ATP No. 1 ranking regardless, holding a 5,010-point lead over Zverev.

His on-court haul came despite a gap in one revenue stream. Sinner earned nothing from the fixed bonus pool for 1000-level events in 2025. Forbes ties that to his skipping five of the nine tournaments at that level.

Exhibitions added to his income as well. He won the Six Kings Slam in Saudi Arabia in both 2024 and 2025, taking home a $6 million check each time.

Away from competition, his sponsor roster leans on Italian brands alongside a global insurer. Allianz joined in a deal announced in January 2026. He also partners with pasta maker De Cecco, fashion house Gucci and coffee company Lavazza. Those endorsements sit within the $35 million Forbes estimates he earned off court.

Carlos Alcaraz earned an estimated $53.7 million in the 12 months dating to the 2025 U.S. Open. That puts the 23-year-old Spaniard second on Forbes' 2026 list of the highest-paid tennis players, with $15.7 million on court and $38 million off it.

No player in Forbes' top 10 earned more away from matches. His off-court figure tops the $35 million estimated for Jannik Sinner, who ranks first overall. Sinner still finished about $4 million ahead in total, helped by a $23 million on-court year.

Alcaraz had led Forbes' ranking in each of the previous two years. This year Sinner replaced him at No. 1.

His 2026 season opened with a career landmark. Alcaraz won the Australian Open and became the youngest man to complete the career Grand Slam. That feat requires a singles title at each of the four majors: the Australian Open, the French Open, Wimbledon and the U.S. Open.

A wrist injury then kept him out of the French Open and Wimbledon, the next two majors on the calendar.

He was set to return at the 2026 U.S. Open, which had not started when Forbes published its ranking on August 28, 2026. A title in New York would be his third there and his eighth Grand Slam title overall. He also entered the tournament's mixed doubles event as a wild card. His partner was Serena Williams, who ranks third on the same Forbes list.

Two new partners joined his sponsor roster over the past year: Ant International and Infosys.

Exhibition matches also filled part of his calendar. He played exhibitions at the Miami Marlins' ballpark and at the New Jersey Devils' arena. In January 2026, he beat Sinner in an exhibition in South Korea. That win came against the player who now sits one place above him on Forbes' list.

The racket he used in the 2023 Wimbledon final sold at auction in October 2025 for $173,066.40. The sale set a record.

Serena Williams earned an estimated $40.1 million in the 12 months dating to the 2025 U.S. Open. All but $0.1 million of that came from deals and ventures off the court.

The 44-year-old American is No. 3 on Forbes' 2026 ranking of the world's highest-paid tennis players, behind Jannik Sinner and Carlos Alcaraz. She spent most of that earning period away from professional play.

Williams returned to competitive tennis in June 2026 after nearly four years away. Before that break, she had won 23 Grand Slam singles titles. Her comeback included singles at Wimbledon and doubles at three other events. Those four tournaments, all played before the U.S. Open, paid her $131,932 in prize money.

She chose not to enter the U.S. Open singles draw. She took a wild-card spot in the tournament's mixed doubles event alongside Carlos Alcaraz. She was also set to play women's doubles with her sister, Venus Williams.

Forbes puts her net worth at $400 million. That estimate makes her the richest woman whose fortune came mainly from athletics.

Her investing runs through Serena Ventures. The venture firm she leads invested in Esusu, a fintech start-up. Esusu sends renters' payment histories to credit bureaus. That reporting helps tenants establish and improve their credit.

Williams has described her goal for Serena Ventures as being an inclusive investor. In her telling, that means backing founders who bring diverse points of view.

Her endorsement roster pairs a long-running relationship with newer names. Nike $NKE has sponsored her for 23 years. Recent partners include the Dutch brewer Heineken and the meal-delivery company Factor. She also fronts a GLP-1 campaign for Ro, a U.S. telehealth company.

Her business roles go beyond sponsorship. She is entrepreneur in residence for Reckitt Catalyst. She was also the model for a new Barbie doll dressed in a business suit.

Aryna Sabalenka earned an estimated $36.1 million in the 12 months dating to the 2025 U.S. Open, according to Forbes. The 28-year-old Belarusian made $13.1 million on court and $23 million off it.

That total ranks fourth on Forbes' list of the 10 highest-paid tennis players. It sits slightly above the estimated $35.5 million earned by Coco Gauff, who ranks fifth.

She won three titles in 2026, in Brisbane, Indian Wells and Miami.

She entered the 2026 U.S. Open seeking a third straight title in New York and a fifth Grand Slam title overall. The tournament also carried ranking stakes. She needed to win it to keep the WTA No. 1 ranking, which she had held for nearly two straight years. Short of a title, Elena Rybakina, Jessica Pegula or Gauff could take the top spot, depending on results.

She signed about a half-dozen new brand deals, among them Emirates, Material Good and Stella Artois. Her wider roster of sponsors also includes Nike $NKE and Gucci, according to Time.

Exhibitions added to her year. She lost the Battle of the Sexes match to Nick Kyrgios in Dubai in December 2025. She also played exhibition events in Hong Kong, Atlanta and New York. She has said she would play another match like the one against Kyrgios. Her condition is that she not be limited to a single serve or a smaller court on her side.

Sabalenka has also been part of the dispute over Grand Slam pay. She reportedly warned that top players could boycott the majors as they pushed for larger prize money. Wimbledon and the U.S. Open later raised their purses.

Time reports that she raised the boycott idea at the Italian Open in May 2026. She lost in the third round of that tournament. Novak Djokovic, Jannik Sinner and Gauff have each backed her view that players should get a bigger share of Grand Slam revenue.

Coco Gauff earned an estimated $35.5 million in the 12 months dating to the 2025 U.S. Open, according to Forbes' 2026 ranking of the world's highest-paid tennis players. The 22-year-old American made $28 million of it off the court and $7.5 million on it.

That total puts her fifth on the Forbes list, just behind Aryna Sabalenka's estimated $36.1 million. Gauff's off-court figure is larger than the $23 million Forbes estimates for Sabalenka. It also tops the $25 million credited to Novak Djokovic.

Her sponsor roster runs to more than a dozen brands. One recent addition is Oura, the smart ring maker, which unveiled its deal with her in August 2026.

Gauff has moved into production as well. In summer 2026 she served as executive producer on two short documentaries for Tennis Channel.

Her on-court story over the period has been uneven. She won the 2025 French Open, the second Grand Slam title of her career. Early losses followed in 2026 at Indian Wells and at the French Open, where she was the defending champion.

One tactical adjustment came from social media. In March 2026, Gauff credited a user on X $TWTR for suggesting she come to the net more often. She said the advice helped her win at the Miami Open.

Her summer results showed a rebound. She reached the semifinals at Wimbledon and at the Canadian Open. She then won the Cincinnati Open title in August 2026.

Gauff has a stake in the debate over how tennis divides its money. She is among the players who have backed Sabalenka's call for athletes to receive a bigger cut of Grand Slam revenue. Djokovic and Jannik Sinner have voiced the same support.

She is in contention for the top of the women's rankings, too. Sabalenka must win the 2026 U.S. Open to keep the WTA No. 1 ranking. If she does not, Gauff is one of three players who could take it, along with Elena Rybakina and Jessica Pegula, depending on results.

Novak Djokovic earned an estimated $30.2 million in the 12 months dating to the 2025 U.S. Open, ranking sixth on Forbes' list of the world's highest-paid tennis players. The Serbian, 39, made $5.2 million on court and $25 million off it.

His most recent major title came at the 2023 U.S. Open, where he beat Daniil Medvedev in the final. That win was his 24th Grand Slam singles title, a men's record. He has not won a major since. In 2026, he reached the Australian Open final in February and the Wimbledon semifinal in July.

His 24 majors include 10 Australian Open titles, seven Wimbledon titles, three French Open titles and four U.S. Open titles. He won men's singles gold at the 2024 Paris Olympics, completing a career Golden Slam.

The 2026 U.S. Open puts two milestones within reach. A 25th major would move him three titles clear of Rafael Nadal on the men's all-time list. Nadal finished his career with 22. The tournament's $5.5 million winner's check would also make him the first player to pass $200 million in career prize money. Neither outcome was known when Forbes published its ranking on August 28, 2026, just before the tournament began.

His preparation for the tournament included an early exit. After losing his first match at the Cincinnati Open in August 2026, Djokovic said he has had "a health issue" for many years. He did not say what the issue is.

Off-court income makes up the bulk of his total. In June 2026, he became a "global strategic advisor" to the investment firm General Atlantic. His other sponsors include the Swiss watchmaker Hublot, the French clothing brand Lacoste and Qatar Airways.

Amazon $AMZN Prime Video released The Wolf in Winter, a documentary on Djokovic, in August 2026.

Djokovic topped the Forbes list in 2023. This year, five players rank ahead of him: Jannik Sinner, Carlos Alcaraz, Serena Williams, Aryna Sabalenka and Coco Gauff.

Qinwen Zheng, the 23-year-old from China, earned an estimated $22.6 million in the 12 months dating to the 2025 U.S. Open, according to Forbes. Off-court income accounted for $22 million of that total, and prize money added $0.6 million.

Zheng holds the No. 7 spot almost entirely because of her commercial deals. Among the seven players in this selection from Forbes' list, only Serena Williams earned less on court over the same period.

Zheng's off-court estimate nearly matches that of Aryna Sabalenka. Sabalenka has won multiple Grand Slam titles and, as of August 2026, had held the WTA No. 1 ranking for nearly two years.

Zheng had elbow surgery in July 2025, shortly before the window Forbes measured. She has not regained the form that won her the Olympic gold medal in women's singles at the 2024 Paris Games. That title made her the first Asian player to win Olympic singles gold in tennis.

She is ranked No. 121 in singles. That position left her outside direct entry to the 2026 U.S. Open main draw, and she had to play qualifying to try to get in.

Most of her named sponsors are Chinese companies. Her partners in China include Alipay, the mobile payments platform, along with the tea chain Chagee and the dairy producer Yili Group. That lineup covers three separate consumer categories: payments, tea and dairy.

Two deals signed in 2026 extend her reach. Qwen, the artificial intelligence platform from Alibaba, ties her to a major Chinese technology company. Her off-court earnings make up nearly all of her estimated total. Her place on Forbes' list shows how endorsement income can outweigh current results. A No. 121 singles ranking and a No. 7 earnings position point in opposite directions.
