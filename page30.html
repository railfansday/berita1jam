Why Micron Stock Bounced Back Friday

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At 5x forward earnings, Micron stock is too cheap to sell.

Micron (MU +7.01%) investors had a rough week, no doubt about it -- but things are getting better.

A major player in both DRAM and NAND flash memory production, Micron stock shed 26% through Thursday's close. As of 10:25 a.m. ET Friday morning, however, the stock is bouncing back 6.3%.

Election 2026 put Democrats in control of both the U.S. Senate and the House and installed Democratic governors in all four states where data center construction is most active. If investors are operating on the theory that Democrats as a whole are hostile to AI and data centers, then it makes sense to worry that data center construction will slow down, the sale of memory chips needed to build those data centers will slow also -- and Micron stock will fall.

But there's also a chance that none of this will happen -- or that if it does, it still won't hurt Micron that much.

After all, according to Citigroup, 2027 will see memory companies increase production by 22%, while demand grows by 26%, maintaining a supply-demand imbalance that should spur sales and keep memory prices high.

This morning, analyst KC Rajkumar at LYNX Equity Strategies seconded that sentiment, confirming that supply shortages, particularly in memory and flash markets, remain tight, and that Asian memory and flash markets aren't showing any drop-off in sales post-election -- at least not yet.

Granted, in the long term, there's a risk that the AI tide could turn against data centers, slowing Micron's growth. In the near term, however, at least through 2027, Micron's future looks secure. Trading for less than 5x the profit it's expected to earn in 2027, Micron looks almost too cheap not to buy.

Why Omarion Miller's breakout game for ASU could come against former team

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Towards the end of practice on Nov. 3, right after Arizona State senior wide receiver Omarion Miller finished talking to local reporters, there was a fun moment between Miller and his teammate, senior safety Adrian "Boogie" Wilson. Wilson is one of the more lively characters on the roster, and this moment was an example of that.

Pretending to be a reporter himself, Wilson began to ask Miller questions ahead of this weekend's home game against Colorado on Nov. 7. The first one was in jest, with Wilson asking, 'Who is your favorite teammate?' Miller responded, 'Probably Boogie Wilson.' Then Wilson got into the meat of his questions.

"'What're you most excited for playing your former team?'"

The answer was short but came with conviction. Miller is set to go against the same team he suited up for from 2023 to 2025.

While Miller did his best not to provide Colorado with bulletin board material, it's only natural to feel more pressure going against your former team.

"You're going to feel something, right?" coach Kenny Dillingham said. "People are going to take that and potentially twist that. They twist everything. But you can't say no. But you can't say yes. Every person is going to handle things different.

"I know he's been the same guy this week for us, same attitude, same everything. You wouldn't know the difference. But there has to be something in your head, right? That's human nature."

Miller still has a bunch of friends that he keeps in contact with in Boulder, but not for this week. It's a place where Miller saw himself grow up, maturing into a leader for younger players in his time with the Buffaloes.

"Man, I ain't gonna lie, this week I'm pretty excited going against my old team," Miller said. "A lot of emotions going on right now, for sure. I played there for three years, going against my former team; those were my guys. Anxious to get on the field and ball."

It's been an up-and-down season for Miller, who started off with eight receptions for 102 yards and one touchdown in the season opener — but that was against lower-level competition, FCS Morgan State. Miller, a priority target in the transfer portal for ASU, recorded five receptions for 85 yards and one touchdown against Baylor, although that came in a 55-19 blowout loss, one of the worst of the Dillingham era.

Miller just broke a three-game spell without a touchdown by hauling in a 20-yard score to cut the lead to 17-10 against BYU on Oct. 31. The senior wide receiver feels like he's on the verge of a breakout game — "Most definitely," he said with a smile — and so do his coaches.

"It's close," Dillingham said. "Last week there were multiple opportunities that (if) one of those balls is caught, that's his breakout game. There it is, just one of those four or five other opportunities. It's going to happen. It's just a matter of when, because we're all around it, we're dancing with it."

Heading into the season, Miller and fellow transfer Reed Harris, coming over from Boston College, knew it was unlikely they were both going to have huge seasons.

So far, it's been Harris (48 receptions for 804 yards and eight touchdowns) who has broken out and is gaining traction as a first-round NFL draft pick.

"Coming here, both of us knew we both couldn't be the guys," Miller said. "So we knew one of us was gonna have to be lower than the other one."

Scouting report: How Arizona State football and Jake Fette can beat Colorado

Many thought it would be Miller, who was ranked No. 2 in the transfer portal among wide receivers. But Miller has taken it all in stride and is happy for his teammate.

Miller said that probably wouldn't have been the case a few years ago.

"As a young guy, I probably would not be happy," Miller said. "That's part of learning as a college player. When it's all said and done, we're a team. I'm always happy for any one of my teammates. Whether they're doing good or bad, I stay motivated now, and they stay motivating me. We stay on each other so we can all do our best."

Defenses are starting to zero in on Harris, with BYU sending a safety at Harris at the start of each snap for most of the game.

Miller is hoping to use that to his advantage.

"They just want to double Reed," Miller said. "They forgot I'm still on the team."

It's been a quarterback carousel for ASU this season, with freshman Jake Fette now helming the offense after sophomore Cutter Boley began the year as the starter and then was replaced by senior Mikey Keene. A knee injury to Keene paved the path for Fette.

While it's early on, Miller can feel the connection between him and Fette growing.

"It's coming along really good," Miller said. "I feel like Jake knows the skill set of our receivers. I always tell him, 'Throw it in the air, and we gonna make you look good.'"

Miller's on the cusp of showing why the Sun Devils targeted him so quickly in the transfer portal, and it may poetically come against the team he left for ASU.

AI memory management: from 30 KB to 11 KB without losing facts

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at wallabytoken.com

The fact was on disk. Twice. Our first winback draft included a customer segment that our current records did not support. That was the moment we stopped treating this as a copy problem and started treating it as a memory architecture problem.

We found 30,195 bytes in our central L2 file: 28 completed items, 28 migration pointers, and a legacy error log. We rebuilt it into an 11,228-byte view with 33 facts intact. This is what changed, what we measured, and why deleting was not the point.

Disclosure: Wallaby provides inference-as-a-service for curated large language models. This is a field note from the memory system we use for our own work, not a claim about customer outcomes.

The relevant customer records were explicit: the proposed segment was not eligible for this campaign. Yet the first draft still had a "previously topped up, now inactive" segment. That segment did not match the people the message was meant to reach.

The correct state existed in two domain records. The draft had not used it. This was not merely stale copy. It was a retrieval and lifecycle problem: the right state was present, but the task that needed it had no reliable way to see it.

A better search engine might have found the right line. It would not have told us which line should still be visible, which domain owned it, or when it had expired.

The central file had grown to 179 lines and 30,195 bytes. The raw counts were useful because they showed that this was not simply a file with too many words.

The file was trying to be five things at once: a cross-domain baseline, a current work board, a domain-state copy, a history archive, and a routing index. It also held a sixth role, the old error register, which had its own proper home elsewhere.

This explained the bloat, but not the missed fact. For that, we had to look at the lifecycle.

Completed work was marked with a strike-through, not moved out of the current view. Migration to five domain READMEs had left behind repeated pointers. Our status checker still searched the old locations rather than all five domains. Dates told us when something had been written, not whether it remained true. And the old error summaries had never been formally retired.

The issue was not a lack of rules. The rules were spread across the system, but they did not form a usable page.

Our first temptation was to make retrieval smarter. That would have addressed the visible symptom and hidden the architecture problem.

We already had event logs, domain projections, an append-only write path, and a query command that returned JSON or one-line Markdown. We did not need another memory store, a vector index, or a new semantic query layer.

The central L2 became a shared current page for humans and AI, not a second task database and not a smaller copy of everything that had ever happened.

The implementation reused domain_state.py, domains.json, the existing event writer, and the existing tests. The only new pieces were a composition entry point and a curated list of sections.

That preview command assembled the anchors into readable Markdown. It did not write L2 or create a second source of truth. Missing anchors failed closed. valid_until exposed stale states. Closed items left the preview after one shift.

The first preview had three anchors: the revenue baseline, current pricing, and the current product homepage. That was enough to prove the mechanism. It was not enough to replace the page.

Next, we migrated the current focus, in-flight work, recently completed items, and account states. The expanded list covered 33 display anchors. The candidate and production files then matched byte for byte, which let us check the replacement instead of assuming it was faithful.

A few implementation details mattered more than the framework:

We wrote the acceptance criteria before calling the work complete. They were intentionally unglamorous.

The projection and freshness tests passed 35 of 35. The wider domain-state regression passed 51 of 51. The index tests passed 10 of 10, and the unified-write integration tests passed 5 of 5. After we fixed the false positive described below, the domain-state suite was 54 of 54.

These are not claims about memory accuracy or business results. They are claims about what the implementation and fixtures demonstrated.

One remaining drift warning would have been easy to misread as a migration failure. It pointed at or-application in the growth README.

The current state was fine. The warning came from an older activation log containing or-application as its anchor. Our legacy-anchor scanner was treating an audit trail as if it were a duplicate current state.

We changed the scanner to exclude proj:activation audit views from the legacy-anchor check. The check still fails on a genuine old anchor outside the projection. It just no longer mistakes an audit log for one.

That small fix produced drift=[] and brought the domain-state suite to 54 passing tests. More importantly, it kept the distinction between a current-state projection and an append-only audit record. Confusing those two would have recreated the original retrieval problem in a new form.

We have not measured a change in customer conversion, memory quality, or long-term maintenance cost. A smaller file is not automatically a better memory system. A passing test suite is not proof that the next real task will retrieve the right context every time.

The current evidence supports narrower conclusions. The central page is smaller. The facts we required are present. The preview updates from events. Closed items leave on schedule. Stale states fail visibly. The old write path still works, and the rollback file exists.

The next checks are equally concrete: whether new events update the central page without hand copies, whether closed items keep leaving on schedule, and whether a real task can answer the current baseline without opening unrelated domain files. If those checks fail, the response should be to inspect the curated list and read path, not to restore the old pile of narrative.

For us, the useful change is not less memory. It is less ambiguity about what is current, who owns it, and when it should stop being shown as current.

Our earlier posts cover the adjacent layers: why memory needs an audit between its log and current state, how we grouped 31 control points into 9 and compared shadow projections, and why a saved status is not evidence of a durable write. The broader upgrade and verification flow is in AI agent memory governance.
