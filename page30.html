I can’t remember the last time an SNL sketch went as viral as “Sister” did a week ago. While impressions, “Weekend Update” and host and musical guest moments are topics of conversation at Studio 8h every week, everyone and their mother saw the “Sister” sketch, and now all those people are going to be in a confined space with one another over Thanksgiving weekend. The comment section is all over this awkwardness.

I’d already been thinking about “Sister” ahead of my own Thanksgiving celebration. Caretaking has hit my family hard while my mom’s battled cancer twice, and while I have another sister who has helped me with those dynamics, SNL’s John Mulaney sketch really spoke to me, and I just already know it’s going to come up at Thanksgiving for many families dealing with caring for an older family member. For us, it should be a humorous conversation. For many? Not so much.

Written by Chase Rosenberg, Mikey Day, Streeter Seidell, and Simon Rich, the sketch follows brothers who have implemented their “sister” for elder care, and have then stepped back to let her handle everything that comes with that label. It’s really funny, but there are also many rings of truth to it, and it has sparked a lot of conversation from angry siblings who feel burnt out and tasked with doing too much. It's led to so much discourse in the days after its release, I'd imagine it will land on the list of Saturday Night Live sketches we'll talk about for years.

Now, one Internet user thinks it’s going to be a big topic of conversation over the holidays, and many SNL viewers agree the timing was probably intentional for a sketch that was this biting and led to so many personal feelings.

Streeter Seidell dedicated the sketch to his own sister Heidi! I wonder how many awkward moments this will inspire next Thursday. 😂

In fact, lots of people think the timing of the SNL sketch was perfect or awful, depending on what you think about it. I don't want to harp too hard

One viral tweet also noted the release of the sketch just before family get togethers had to be “deliberate." (Again, it's definitely interesting.)

SNL gets a bad rap, often deservedly so. But this is a sketch we'll be talking about for decades. Look at the replies and quote-tweets: person after person after person saying "this is my life", or "I'm Sister." Dropping this right before Thanksgiving had to be deliberate, right?

To note, I don't personally agree with the bent that SNL deserves a bad rap. The show has had ups and downs as long as I've watched it, and silly bits can sometimes drive home harder than some of the political or Update stuff. While not on topic to Thanksgiving like the Adele-themed "Thanksgiving Miracle" sketch with Aidy Bryant, I do think this is one we will continue to watch and discuss this week.

I'd just recommend people do so before people are too hyped up on tryptophan, alcohol and sugar.

From Hines v. Stamos, decided yesterday by Judges W. Eugene Davis, Leslie Southwick, and Stuart Kyle Duncan:

Jill Hines sued Stanford University, Alex Stamos, and others, alleging they conspired with the government to censor her online speech about COVID-19. The district court dismissed her suit without prejudice for lack of subject-matter and personal jurisdiction. Because Hines has not shown that her injury is fairly traceable to Stanford or Stamos, she lacks standing. We therefore AFFIRM without reaching personal jurisdiction….

This case is closely related to Murthy v. Missouri, which guides our resolution of this appeal. In Murthy, Hines (along with others) sued federal officials and agencies, alleging the government censored her online speech against COVID-19 restrictions in violation of the First Amendment. Here, Hines challenges Defendants' participation in an academic project named the Election Integrity Partnership (EIP). She alleges the EIP and its participants aided the government's scheme to have Facebook suppress her online speech, also in violation of the First Amendment. {Our review is limited to Hines's claims against Stanford and Alex Stamos, the former director of the Stanford Internet Observatory, which was an EIP member.}

Facebook began moderating COVID-19 content early in the pandemic, deleting or demoting posts it deemed false. In April 2020 alone, it labeled roughly 50 million pieces of content. That same month, Hines launched a grassroots campaign to challenge COVID-19 government restrictions and used Facebook to communicate with her supporters. She was aware of Facebook's moderation and took deliberate measures to circumvent it.

The EIP, a consortium of researchers and institutions, was formed in July 2020. It sought to identify misinformation about elections and voting. For about three months in 2020, it monitored elections-related content on social media. EIP participants sometimes recommended actions to social-media platforms and logged their communications when they did. In most instances, the platforms took no detectable action on those recommendations….

To have standing, a "plaintiff must have (1) suffered an injury in fact, (2) that is fairly traceable to the challenged conduct of the defendant, and (3) that is likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision." For the claims before us, the second inquiry—traceability of Hines's past injuries—is "the whole ball game." …

There is one injury that Hines claims is traceable to Defendants: on September 9, 2020, Facebook removed a post from Hines's page that attached an article entitled Masks Don't Work. At the time, Facebook explained the post went "against our Community Standards." It also reduced distribution of Hines's page on COVID-19 restrictions for "repeated" violations of its standards on "Inauthentic Behavior" and for "repeated sharing of false news." Hines does not dispute that these restrictions fit Facebook's own content moderation…

Hines's traceability theory rests on an inference drawn from timing. On September 8, 2020, the EIP received a massive submission of over 200 webpages and social-media posts from the Democratic National Committee. Among those 200 webpages was a single post by Hines where she encouraged her followers to lobby their representatives to end the COVID-19 public-health emergency. In Hines's view, the one-day timing between the DNC's submission and Facebook's moderation of her page is evidence that EIP participants called Facebook's attention to her single post in the 200-webpage DNC submission.

We are not persuaded by her argument. Discovery revealed no communication between the EIP and Facebook about Hines, and Facebook did not remove or moderate the post that the EIP received. {Hines argues there may be evidence of Defendants' communications with Facebook in undiscovered Slack or Zoom messages. But Stanford produced "communications and other documents" mentioning Hines or her account, and Hines neither challenged that production nor moved to compel additional discovery, although the scheduling order allowed for that. The mere possibility of undiscovered communications that were not sought in the district court is not evidence.}

A notation in the EIP's records even says Hines's COVID-19 posts were beyond the scope of the EIP's elections-oriented work. We conclude as a matter of law that timing alone is too attenuated to trace Hines's injury to Defendants.

Murthy also requires us to consider Facebook's own incentives to moderate content. Facebook gave its own reasons for acting when it removed Hines's post and demoted her page, and Hines does not dispute that that moderation aligned with Facebook's COVID-19 policies. We agree with the district court that there's no indication that Facebook, in moderating Hines's post, did anything other than follow its own moderation policy….

I am not at all involved with Stanford's defense of this lawsuit.

Recent research is raising questions about the commercial value of AI visibility. It also exposes a problem with what we have chosen to measure.

Rand Fishkin recently asked whether AI visibility is becoming a marketing boondoggle. In a Whiteboard video shared on LinkedIn, he discussed several studies questioning the commercial returns from visibility in standalone AI assistants, while distinguishing those platforms from Google’s AI-powered search experiences.

I understand the skepticism. We have seen an extraordinary amount of enthusiasm for GEO measurement, accompanied by new tools, new metrics, and a growing expectation that brands should monitor how often they appear in AI-generated answers. Much of that work is useful. I want to know whether a company appears when prospective customers search for its category, how its capabilities are presented, and which competitors are recommended.

For some time, I have been concerned that we are investing heavily in tracking mentions, citations, and share of voice without giving enough attention to the decisions customers are asking AI systems to help them make. We seem to have accepted that being mentioned is inherently valuable, even when we cannot demonstrate that the mention helped a customer evaluate the company or move toward a purchase.

I’m also concerned about this given the executive attention SEO, GEO, and AEO are receiving. Search professionals have spent years trying to secure the resources and cross-functional cooperation needed to make meaningful changes. Now, in some organizations, we have senior leadership listening, budgets for tools and content, and an unusual willingness to bring marketing, product, sales, and technical teams together.

We risk squandering that opportunity if the principal outcome we report is more AI visibility. Executives are investing because they believe AI is changing how customers discover and choose vendors. We need to show that our work is addressing that change, not simply producing another set of exposure metrics.

The starting point is the customer’s motivation. People generally aren’t going to ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google’s AI-powered search experiences for another page of links. They want help understanding something, comparing alternatives, or reaching a decision. Traditional search left much of that work to the user, who had to visit multiple sites and reconcile the information. AI systems are attractive partly because they reduce that effort.

Yet we still talk about GEO as though the expected journey is a brand mention, followed by a click, followed by a conversion. That sequence may describe some purchases, but it is a poor model for many complex B2B decisions.

Fishkin’s argument drew on research from Marie Haynes, The SEO Works, and Kevin Indig. Although they approached the issue differently, their findings make it difficult to assume that increasing visibility in standalone AI assistants will necessarily produce meaningful traffic or conversions.

In GEO Is a Distraction, Haynes examined analytics data from 100 websites representing more than 145 million sessions. ChatGPT referrals accounted for approximately 0.62% of Google organic sessions, rising to 0.73% when other tracked language models were included. Even when ChatGPT visitors converted at higher rates, the difference in traffic volume meant that Google organic traffic remained far more important to measured conversions.

Her concern is partly one of priorities. Given the changes taking place within Google Search, how much effort should businesses devote to improving visibility in standalone assistants that currently contribute relatively little measurable traffic?

The SEO Works’ AI Referral Traffic Benchmark examined 95 websites and found that organic search generated 39 times as many sessions as AI assistants. For every 100 conversions attributed to organic search, AI referrals generated 3.2. The study also found considerable variation in conversion performance. Although AI referrals converted approximately 18% better overall, the rate for B2B product companies was only 0.62 times that of organic traffic.

That last finding caught my attention because B2B buying behavior is often discussed as though AI-assisted discovery will naturally produce exceptionally valuable visits. I agree it may, but only if the information is available, and I note that it might not be universally applicable.

Kevin Indig explored another possibility in Does AI Drive More Direct Traffic?. Perhaps AI influences buyers who subsequently visit a website directly or search for a brand, leaving the original influence unattributed. In an analysis of 12 analytics properties, he found that some apparent direct-traffic growth was due to bot activity. Additional testing across four brands and 632 tests did not establish a reliable relationship between AI visibility and subsequent direct or branded search activity.

Indig did not argue that AI has no influence on purchasing behavior. Rather, his work demonstrated how difficult it is to establish that influence using the signals available in conventional analytics.

I think these findings deserve more than a debate about whether GEO is worth the investment. They should prompt us to reconsider what we are asking visibility metrics to prove.

If a customer uses AI to compare vendors, rule out unsuitable products, or validate a shortlist, there may be no referral click. The customer could make substantial progress toward a decision without visiting any of the cited sources. That does not mean the AI interaction had no value, but it also does not mean every brand mentioned in the response benefited commercially.

The question I want to answer is what happened during the evaluation, particularly whether the company had enough supporting evidence to be considered a suitable choice.

A recent conversation with an executive brought this into focus for me. He was evaluating outside consulting support and explained that his organization had been disappointed by consultants and vendors three times in the previous year. He was not interested in another polished sales pitch or a broad claim of expertise. Having been burned repeatedly, he intended to make the next selection process considerably more demanding.

What stood out was his sense of responsibility for the decision. He needed to be confident that the next consultant could deliver, but he also needed to justify that confidence to the organization.

We sometimes underestimate how much of a complex purchasing decision is about avoiding risk. Buyers are trying to establish that a product will work in their environment, meet the requirements of different stakeholders, and avoid creating problems they will later have to explain. When budgets are tight, the consequences of getting that decision wrong become even more significant.

A company can be well-known, highly visible, and frequently mentioned without addressing those concerns.

Consider a buyer evaluating specialized software. Knowing that a vendor is a recognized provider may be useful at the beginning of the process. Once the buyer introduces requirements involving integrations, compliance, implementation capacity, or operational constraints, general category visibility becomes much less informative. The buyer needs evidence that the vendor can satisfy those requirements.

This is where I believe the distinction between appearing in an answer and earning a recommendation becomes commercially important.

We saw this problem in an engagement with a specialized B2B software company. Across the AI responses we observed, the brand was cited 98% of the time. By most visibility measures, that would be considered an excellent result. The company was clearly being recognized within its category.

At the same time, it was receiving little observable AI referral traffic, and its lead volume was declining. We could not establish that AI had caused the decline, but the situation prompted us to look more closely at what happened when buyers moved beyond broad-category questions.

We began testing more specific decision scenarios, introducing the requirements that a prospective customer might use to determine which vendors to include on a shortlist. Under those conditions, the company’s recommendation rate was 17%. In a separate testing condition where supporting evidence was absent, the rate was 8%.

As we examined the responses, we found that important capabilities were not always supported by information the AI systems could retrieve. In a workshop reviewing those gaps, someone on the client team said, “We have all of that. We just don’t have it on the website.”

I have heard variations of that statement throughout my career, but it takes on a different significance when customers can use AI to evaluate vendors before speaking with sales.

There are often legitimate reasons information is not publicly available. Some details are commercially sensitive. Some capabilities are complex enough that sales or technical teams prefer to explain them directly. Other information simply never makes it onto the website because nobody has recognized its importance to the buying decision.

Historically, a website could generate interest and leave the lead nurturing to sales. As research has shown, especially in B2B, AI-assisted evaluation often takes place before the buyer makes contact, creating a significant disadvantage. The company may satisfy the requirement, yet the available evidence may not be sufficient to establish that suitability.

The answer is not to just publish everything but to clearly identify the requirements customers are likely to evaluate and determine what credible, appropriately disclosed evidence can support those assessments.

See also: B2B Buyers Choose A Vendor Before They Reach Out – 3 Ways To Be Visible When It Counts

This is the thinking behind the Decision Coverage framework I introduced in Decision Coverage: Why AI Recommends Some Brands And Not Others.

In an earlier article, When AI Takes The Click, Click Worthiness Should Guide Your Strategy, I argued that marketers should focus more attention on interactions where direct engagement still adds meaningful value after AI has answered an informational question. Decision Coverage approaches the problem from the other side: before a customer engages directly, have we made enough evidence available for our company to be evaluated accurately?

One useful concept is the eligibility gate, a requirement that a solution must meet to be considered suitable for a particular buyer. This is a way to model purchasing decisions, not a claim that language models use a fixed internal checklist.

A buyer might regard some capabilities as desirable and others as mandatory. If a vendor cannot satisfy a critical requirement, it may not belong on the shortlist, regardless of how visible it is. But there is another possibility that marketers need to recognize: the vendor meets the requirement, yet the supporting evidence is missing or difficult to retrieve.

Traditional content-gap analysis does not always uncover this problem. We may find that a competitor has written about a topic we have not covered, or that our pages need stronger explanations of a product category. Those findings can improve discoverability, but they may have little bearing on whether a buyer can establish that we meet a mandatory requirement.

Decision Coverage starts with the decision itself. What is the customer trying to accomplish? Who is involved? Which requirements are essential, which are negotiable, and what trade-offs are likely to influence the choice? We can then examine whether the information available about the company is sufficient to support an assessment against those criteria.

This requires significantly more cooperation across the business than many traditional SEO activities. Marketing may understand the buyer persona, but product teams often hold the evidence needed to answer them, and most importantly, sales understands the prospects’ objections and evaluation criteria.

That is another reason I am concerned about wasting the current level of executive attention. We finally have an opportunity to bring these groups together around a problem that matters to customers. I would rather use that access to close important evidence gaps than simply produce more category content.

Our research into AI fan-out questions gave us another reason to focus on decision criteria. In one study beginning with a prompt asking for the best solution in a software category, 62% of the observed questions concerned understanding the category, developing evaluation criteria, or comparing vendors.

Those observations do not reveal a model’s complete internal reasoning, and I would not suggest that every AI system follows the same process. What they show is that the observable research supporting a recommendation can involve considerably more than retrieving a list of vendors.

We also found that recommendations sometimes changed when we altered the buyer’s perspective. A prompt framed around an individual’s professional responsibilities did not always produce the same recommendations as one written from the perspective of a department or organization.

In another test, a vendor appeared as the leading recommendation until we changed the emphasis and ordering of the requirements. That vendor then disappeared from the recommended set, replaced by a company that had not previously appeared but was particularly strong on the newly emphasized criterion.

I cannot attribute that change solely to the order of the requirements, as we saw other cases where retests days later prompted different interpretations, putting focus on different criteria. Nevertheless, the results made it difficult to defend the idea that recommendation performance could be represented adequately by a single broad prompt or a static collection of category queries.

That is why understanding decision context matters. A company may be the strongest option for one combination of requirements and less suitable when another constraint takes priority. Unfortunately, we don’t have unlimited budgets for every conceivable prompt, but we can maybe use larger diagnostic prompts that capture multiple prompt turns.

Accordingly, for measurement and alignment purposes, I would rather test a representative set of buyer decisions than simply increase the number of prompts being tracked. The objective is to understand when the company qualifies, where evidence is missing, and which competing vendors are better supported for particular requirements. Those are the critical insights we need to understand to truly close gaps.

Mike King also raises a related measurement issue in How Do We Move Forward if the SERP Is Generative?. As search results become dynamically generated, he argues that conventional assumptions about stable rankings are increasingly difficult to maintain. Measuring visibility requires repeated observations and a probabilistic approach rather than relying on a single result.

I agree, and I think recommendation measurement needs to account for another source of variation: the customer’s requirements.

A company might appear in nearly every broad category response but be recommended infrequently for the decision scenarios that matter most to its business. Another company might have lower overall visibility but be consistently recommended when its particular strengths align with the buyer’s needs. Aggregated mention counts cannot adequately explain either situation.

For our B2B software client, the evidence gaps suggested a testable hypothesis. If the company genuinely satisfies important buyer requirements but has not made that information retrievable, improving the available evidence should increase its recommendation frequency under comparable decision scenarios.

We can establish a baseline, address specific gaps, and repeat the testing. Because generated responses vary, that work needs multiple observations and consistent scenarios rather than a few favorable examples.

I want to be careful about what an improvement would mean, however. A higher recommendation rate would suggest that the available evidence is better supporting the company’s suitability. It would not prove that buyers are choosing the company more often or that the work has increased revenue.

That distinction matters especially for this client, whose sales cycle typically lasts 9 to 14 months. Even if better evidence starts influencing evaluations now, we may not see the corresponding effect on qualified opportunities or pipeline for several quarters. Some buyers may already have made their decisions while important information was missing.

We also have to recognize that the decline in leads could have several causes. Market demand, product competitiveness, sales execution, and other marketing activity may all be influencing performance. The combination of declining leads and weak recommendation performance is a reason to investigate, not evidence of causation.

I would therefore measure progress in stages. First, determine whether the gaps in decision-support evidence have actually been closed. Then test whether recommendation performance changes in the scenarios where those gaps mattered. Over time, examine whether qualified opportunities, pipeline, and eventually revenue show corresponding improvements, while considering other factors that could explain the results.

Customer interviews and self-reported discovery information may help fill some of the attribution gaps. They will not provide perfect answers, but they can help us understand whether AI-assisted research played a role in the buying process. This is consistent with Indig’s broader finding that neither referral traffic nor changes in direct traffic can explain AI’s commercial influence on their own.

I would not make AI referral traffic the primary KPI for this work. If the customer can reach an evaluation or shortlist decision without clicking, referral counts may tell us relatively little about whether our information helped or hindered that decision.

I think Fishkin is right to challenge the industry on business value. The findings from Haynes, The SEO Works, and Indig should make marketers cautious about presenting increased AI visibility as evidence of commercial success. The question is not whether AI visibility has value. It is whether we are confusing being present in an answer with being considered in a decision.

At first the kids jostle to get to the games that they know. "Welcome to the Retro Reset Bus," says Luke Stoner as the students push to get to the screen that’s caught their eye. Sonic on the Sega Mega Drive is the first they see, and the quickest student to get on board the bus looks triumphant as he nabs the stool in front of the console. His retort to his mate appears on the 1981 BBC Micro Model B‘s screen as the other students crane to have a look. Luke’s knowledge is beautiful but the students also find him approachable. One student asks where Minecraft is and Luke explains that it doesn’t fit the parameter of being more than twenty years old yet, sharing details of when it was published and by who. Roblox could be included, but Luke isn’t keen because of recent controversies around safety. When this group are reluctantly herded off the bus by even more reluctant teachers, another heads in with just a little gap for resetting the machines and cramming a biscuit.

When Jason asks if anyone would like to break an iPhone and have a look inside, all of the hands shoot up. He then talks about memory, RAM and chips, brings out a floppy disk; and tells the tale of where the expression "computer bug" came from. Still, it's the game on a tape - Jungle Story for the Commodore 64 - which they all turn away from screens to see. Perhaps we have Stranger Things to thank, but the tape has retro currency and they all think it’s sick.

The event is being run by East Sussex Careers Hub and is specifically aimed at students with SEND (Special Educational Needs and Disabilities). More than twenty-five schools turn up and most teachers leave with a flyer, chatting to colleagues about where the bus could park onsite. For now, he is juggling it with work commitments.

The bus was Luke’s idea and inspired by a visit to The Centre for Computing History in Cambridge, where visitors can get their hands on home computers and consoles from decades gone by. The family started thinking about what was possible and finally, after Luke finished college, began kitting out the bus: buying or accepting donations of hardware, researching which monitors would work with the ancient systems, and installing it all within the cabin.

On a basic level, the charity is telling the story of how technology has iterated. But by allowing visitors to take apart their machines, and giving brief lessons in coding with Basic, it also pulls the veil back on the work that goes into making a game - while opening students' eyes to what they could create themselves. "I guess it’s bridging the gap between the polished and finished latest game," Jason says, "which could be perceived as not achievable for them, to seeing how all that coding came about. Other than making sure they are really accurate with their typing; they realise that coding can be quite simple and something that they can actually do. I would go further and say that when they have written some code, nearly all of them have a big smile on their face." This, he hopes, might be the spark for some students to get into coding, especially for PC games, where under-18s are an underrepresented demographic.

This is reflected in the staggering stat that the retro gaming market is estimated to be worth $3.8 billion globally, with a projected rise to $8 billion by 2033. Retro-focused videos on YouTube and TikTok rack up millions of views of views, and communities formed on reddit or Discord - some with hundreds of thousands of members - discuss nothing else.

Nostalgia is playing a huge part. Atari’s Implicit Conversations is built on just this, creating retro games for modern consoles like PlayStation 5 or the Nintendo Switch. For Luke, though, this misses the point. He likes the click when you put a cartridge in a machine; and he likes that he is just him and the game. The parents of visiting students appreciate this too, and often share how overwhelming it feels to keep their kids safe in the online gaming environment. "Parents of younger children [tell us that] they hate that Minecraft and Roblox can allow their kids to play against perceived strangers. Even the more technical parents say they still miss safety measures and would prefer the games not be online at all," Jason explains.

Last month, Ofcom published its Growing Up Gaming summary report, which found that almost two-thirds (64%) of parents of three to 17 year olds who game online are concerned about their child talking to strangers through games or their chat functions. And, while the vast majority of young players go online without incident, it is true that those who use chat functions will likely interact with strangers. The same research found that more than half (55%) of children who game online say they play with people they have never met offline at least sometimes, while one in five (20%) do so all or most of the time they play.

By contrast, Jason claims that not one student has asked if they can get online, and he’s also never seen one get out their phone when they’re on the bus. Perhaps it is a welcomed break. "We offer a completely safe environment to come and experience these games and machines. That's really what we're here for," he states.

Luke shares too that he became nervous of the data he was sharing with gaming companies, and conscious that this was all being stored, thus making it vulnerable to breaches. He also baulked at what he felt was a constant money grab from gaming companies, including moves that render games unsupported or completely obsolete. Retro gaming is cheap in comparison and therefore more accessible, especially for younger players. "When you tell them that they can get an old console for less than £100 and often games come free when you buy them second hand, their ears really prick up," Jason says. "For a lot of the youngsters, the cost of modern gaming equipment is unattainable. And of course, we explain that they could take old computers and laptops and then load free operating software onto them. This gets them online, often for free. Once they are there, they can game."

John W. Champeau is founder of Champ Games, a Connecticut-based venture, which develops retro games system primarily for the Atari 2600. "Atari has released a line of "+" systems that are modern reinterpretations of the class Atari 2600 and 7800 hardware, but at a lower price point but with modern conveniences such as HDMI support, firmware updates, and new controllers," he notes. "This means that the games themselves can be acquired at a low cost as well, sometimes even free if someone is lucky enough to know someone with a box of old Atari carts in their basement or attic."

But there may also be something intrinsically different about how these games are designed to be played that is driving their renaissance. In December 2025, Newsweek shared the views of two mental health professionals that "90s Video Games Were Better for Kids’ Brains." In what was sure to be a contentious piece (and perhaps that was the point), mental health counsellor Veronica Lichtenstein, and Melissa Gallagher, a licensed clinical social worker, argued that the very nature of gaming has changed and is now damaging. Retro games had a start and an end – and with the latter came a big hit of dopamine for achieving victory. Modern games are designed as "loops for addiction" with no real end; and constant prompts to buy. Satisfaction is constantly deferred. Lichtenstein also says that modern games leave little room for critical thinking, as a quick search online will delivers solutions if a player ever gets stuck.

Champeau agrees. "Our games are not designed to be addictive 'time stealers' like some modern video games that encourage players to 'stay online' for hours on end to acquire additional resources needed to complete a game or advance to a new level, etc," he says, adding: "All of our games are designed to be short burst distractions that don't rely on long gaming sessions to feel a sense of accomplishment.” He says that the lower graphics resolution and primitive sounds are actually a relief for many after years of hyper-realistic gaming. "Retro games distance the gap between game and reality, which was the intention of video games in the first place," he argues.

This is play for play’s sake, with no distractions, and could be why the games of the past might be just the future these kids need.

After months of long runs and tough workouts, you’ve finally crossed the finish line of your goal race. Now, it’s time to recover—and figure out what’s next.

While you can scale back your running in the offseason, it’s worth making room for strength training. Even a short time in the weight room can help you build a stronger foundation for your next training block.

The good news? You don’t need to spend all your newfound free time in the gym to make progress. By hitting the following minimum amount of strength work each week, you set yourself up to handle more mileage and run more efficiently when it’s time to chase a new goal.

In the first two weeks after your race, you should focus mainly on rest and getting back into easy jogging. After that, aim to add at least two strength sessions per week to your routine, exercise physiologist Heather Milton, C.S.C.S., previously told Runner’s World. Milton says these offseason strength sessions should focus on heavier weights (just make sure you work up in weight!) and a low-to-moderate number of reps.

During these heavy lifting workouts, athletes should aim to complete four to six sets of one to five reps of each exercise, pausing for two to four minutes in between each set, strength coach and physical therapist Lisbeth Hoyt, D.P.T., C.S.C.S., previously told Runner’s World.

While running is your main focus during marathon training (or any race distance, for that matter), strength sessions often help you maintain the strength you already have, Milton explains. But between training blocks, you can put more emphasis on building strength.

“Plus, when you start training for your next marathon, you’ll be in a stronger position to increase your running volume again because you’ve strengthened all of the areas of your body that were impacted by your previous race, which can help prevent injuries,” Milton adds.

You might associate heavy lifting with short sprinters or football players—athletes who focus on quick, explosive movement, rather than steady endurance. But building stronger muscles through heavy lifting supports long-distance running, too.

For starters, lifting heavier weights helps you build muscular strength, which translates to improved running economy, New York City-based strength and run coach Yusuf Jeffers previously told Runner’s World. Meaning, heavy lifting can help you use less energy to run at a given pace.

Research backs this up. A review published in Sports Medicine in 2023 examined several types of strength training in middle- and long-distance runners, including lifting heavy, submaximal lifting, isometric exercises, and plyometrics. The researchers found that lifting high loads—at least 80 percent of a person’s one-rep max—had the greatest effect on improving running economy at faster speeds. (Plyometrics worked particularly well for improving economy at slower speeds.)

The benefits extend beyond your muscles, too. Hoyt explains that strength training can support connective tissues and bone health.

It’s important to remember lifting “heavy” looks different for everyone. If you’re new to strength training or you’re unfamiliar with a particular movement, start by learning proper form and gradually work up in weight, Hoyt says.

Hoyt suggests focusing on movements such as squats, deadlifts, lunges, and rows, encouraging athletes to move slowly and with control.

I knew from that moment forward what my path was,” she says.

It didn’t take long for Prager to start getting noticed in art circles. And this winter will be one of the most significant seasons of her career, with both a major solo museum show at the Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art in Bentonville, Arkansas, and an exhibition at the New York gallery Lehmann Maupin. DreamQuil, her first feature film, was released in theaters this fall and will be available to stream in December.

Prager’s color-saturated works capture cinematic scenes that bring to mind classic Hitchcock films. She creates full worlds for her photos, with sets, casts, and impeccable period costumes. Her pieces gained notice with her 2007 show “Polyester” at L.A.’s Robert Berman Gallery. The series showed women, perfectly attired in ’60s-era looks, posed in evocative ways. A heavily made-up girl clutches a Big Gulp, a flaxen braid thrown over one shoulder; four young women huddle together in swimsuits, a plane flying across the perfect blue sky above. An ominous feeling pervades.

Her work sparkles on the surface but explores something darker underneath. “The first color photograph I took was staged and a play on The Wizard of Oz and death, but it was very colorful and beautiful. I was interested in the way to explore something that felt layered. Underneath it, you find there are very uncomfortable things or darker stories and emotions,” Prager explains.

Prager steadily built up a following through a series of gallery shows, but things changed markedly in 2010 when her work was selected to be shown in the elite “New Photography” exhibit at MoMA. It’s still about the questions she needs answers to.

The idea for DreamQuil came to Prager during the pandemic (she was inspired by a meme that advertised YearQuil, a fictitious product that would allow people to sleep through the misery of 2020), and she wrote the script with her sister, Vanessa Prager. Elizabeth Banks stars as a woman who’s living in a depressing future world where people rarely leave their homes and finds herself dealing with her robot clone.

Opening two major shows within months of the release of her film wasn’t something Prager anticipated—each alone would have been a high point in her career—but she often does her best work amid a frenzy of activity. “I thrive in a bit of chaos,” she says. “I like to try to mold it and wrestle with it and attempt to control it and bend it to my will. It doesn’t always happen exactly the way I want, but it infuses me with inspiration and energy.”

The Maya People Loved the Color Red. Scientists Say It May Have Helped Bring Them Down.

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The reddish pigment cinnabar was so popular that it seeped into the water supply around Maya settlements, setting off a disastrous downstream effect.

The Maya people had a toxic relationship with the color red—quite literally, according to fresh research. The new analysis of the Yucatán Peninsula during the Maya era identified astronomically spiked levels of mercury, which might have contributed to the collapse of Maya culture.

“The mercury data was crazy,” Matt Waters, an environmental sciences professor and paleolimnologist at Auburn University, said in a university statement about the unexpected discovery on the peninsula. “Off the charts. These mercury levels aren’t normal.”

Matt Waters led a study published in Science Advances that explored sediment samples collected at two lakes—Lago Coba and Cenote Xcach—showing the intense spike in mercury (Hg) levels ranging from around 40 C.E. to 800 C.E.

“We show that Classic Period Hg concentrations in sediment cores from two lakes on the Yucatán Peninsula exceeded modern sediment Hg toxicity thresholds by orders of magnitude for centuries during ancient Maya occupation,” the authors wrote, adding that the heightened pollution coincides with aquatic food collapse, “confirming that Hg toxicity was an additional environmental stressor for Classic Maya populations.”

While Waters isn’t saying mercury caused the collapse of the Maya civilization, he also doesn’t want to ignore its impact. “It’s been confirmed that drought was an issue, deforestation was an issue, erosion was an issue, overpopulation was an issue, and feeding the population was probably an issue,” he said. “This study adds the impact of water quality. Let’s put that in the list. That’s what our study does. It establishes that list and moves it forward.”

The study actually began as part of a National Science Foundation project focused on harmful algal blooms, but a then-graduate student and study co-author, Savanna Wooten, suggested also testing for heavy metals. Sediment coring from the lake bottoms pulled up mud from thousands of years ago that contained the intense mercury spikes during the Classic Maya Period, which were then followed by an abrupt decline.

The powerfully high mercury concentration lasted for hundreds of years, and Waters believes the sudden change at the base level likely moved up the food web, impacting fish and other larger creatures. Not only would eating fish or drinking water have exposed humans to the toxic levels, but there may have even been fewer fish to catch. “They would have seen a strange color change in the water,” Waters told Live Science, “and a drop in its productivity, such as fish for protein.”

Waters linked to a study he’d worked on with a team from the University of Florida in the late 1990s that found a gap in ostracod shells during a specific period from Coba, correlating the missing shell timing to the rise in mercury. Further investigation into the cores found that declines in biological markers occurred throughout. “The algae was killed,” he said. “The cyanobacteria, they tanked. Purple sulfur bacteria, vanished. The biological community completely changed.”

The people “experienced this for decades and, in one case, for centuries.”

The source of that mercury was cinnabar, they believe, a vibrant red mineral made up of 86 percent mercury that Maya culture viewed as a sacred pigment. “This was not like mercury raining down from the sky,” Waters said. “This was not a regional thing. It was concentrated.”

Maya loved cinnabar, using the bright red in pottery, murals, temple walls, and burials. To keep up with demand, they heavily imported it, especially around Coba and Xcach. “It’s common knowledge that there was trade in obsidian, in jade, and beautiful stones, and weaponry,” Waters said. “I think this could start a conversation that cinnabar could have been a major trade commodity.”

It may also show a highly toxic love affair that contributed to Maya struggles.

The 2027 Toyota GRMN Corolla Is Almost Worth the Squeeze

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Revised suspension, new gear ratios, and more torque make the GRMN Corolla more of a blast on track.

Toyota’s GR Corolla has valiantly filled the gap left by the deceased Subaru WRX STI and Mitsubishi Lancer Evo as a rally-inspired compact that combines everyday usability with performance. Combining a torque-rich turbocharged three-cylinder with a manual transmission and all-wheel drive in a practical four-door shell still sounds like a winning formula to us.

But how does it fare if practicality is reduced to sharpen the driving experience? When Toyota announced a limited run of track-focused GR Corollas with no back seat and a $65,655 price tag, alongside some choice mechanical upgrades, the first reaction was skepticism. Why dilute the approachable, jack-of-all-trades appeal of the existing GR Corolla with a more expensive version? To answer that, Toyota took Road & Track to Eagles Canyon Raceway on the outskirts of Dallas, Texas, for the chance to give the GRMN Corolla a proper romp around the track. I’m happy to report that the special edition offered a compelling defense.

Limited to 730 units, the 2027 Toyota GRMN Corolla’s upgrades are primarily focused on improved dynamic response. The suspension, tuned at the Nürburgring and Fuji Speedway, gets shorter, stiffer bump stops up front and longer bump stops in the rear to better deal with circuit curbing and midcorner bumps. Toyota has also firmed up the steering weight, especially at high speeds, and revised front-end aerodynamics to better vent turbulent air through new flaps in the wheel wells and carbon-fiber hood. Wider Michelin Cup 2 tires are also standard, replacing the GR’s Pilot Sport 4s. Driven hard on the elevation-heavy road course, the GRMN felt much more confident and able.

Part of the GR Corolla’s handling charm has historically been its rally-car rowdiness—a tendency to roll and then set into a corner, plus some degree of rear-end playfulness under power and braking—and the revised suspension setup manages to maintain this character. Approaching Turn 12, a descending, off-camber sweeper where the correct line takes the front end over an elevated curb, the GRMN Corolla refused to be unsettled. Similarly, the tendency to swing out the lightened rear end under braking I remember well from the GR Corolla seemed largely negated and easier to correct when it did happen.

The front end of the GRMN Corolla was also sharper than ever, with a rapid turn-in and heavier, confidence-inspiring steering weight. Leaning into Turn 6 at wide-open throttle, the front end didn’t waver as I asked for more steering angle at nearly 100 mph. There was even steering feel, which is a treat on any modern performance car. However, rapid steering changes were sometimes accompanied by a binding feeling, a trait we’ve experienced in previous GR Corolla variants. It was hard to say whether the aerodynamic upgrades contributed to the solid front end, but the addition of the grippier tires no doubt played a significant part in the improved confidence. Back-to-back laps in a standard GR Corolla on Pilot Sport 4s tires proved the GRMN was more planted and offered much better communication of its position and limitations.

Toyota has only made small changes to the 1.6-liter turbocharged inline-three in the GRMN, adding just 7 lb-ft of torque to take the total to 302 lb-ft, accompanied by an unchanged 300 hp. Response felt improved off the line, and there was more top-end grunt too, although the biggest reason for the perception of additional surge was likely the new revised-ratio six-speed manual transmission, which is exclusive to the GRMN.

With a longer first gear and shorter third gear, the car found a good middle ground between the slightly-too-shortness of the STI and the excessive length of something like a Porsche 718. For reference, fourth gear runs out at 105 mph, a good compromise between the thrill of changing gears and having to do so too often. The new, slightly smaller shift knob also felt more ergonomic in my hand, and the updated shifter-cable mount offered more satisfying action.

Of course, a core part of the appeal of the regular GR Corolla is its tunable differential, and this is carried over unchanged on the GRMN. Track mode offered the most rear-biased power split, allowing 70 percent to be sent to the back axle, while Gravel mode split power evenly between both axles. Even in the rear-biased setting, the GRMN Corolla was more liable to push the front end on power than rotate the rear end around.

I actually preferred the GRMN Corolla in Gravel mode around Eagles Canyon Raceway, as the front-wheel-biased performance best practice of trail braking and on-throttle patience more easily led to a clean lap. Brake feel was also improved on the GRMN Corolla, with the GRMN exhibiting no fade over more than half a dozen laps, and there was always more power available through the pedal when asked.

Ergonomically, the GRMN Corolla remains a sneakily competent sports car trapped in a four-door hatchback shape. With new bucket-style seats in red and black upholstery, the GRMN Corolla offered more lateral support at the expense of comfort. For me, the new seats felt thinner and decidedly less comfortable than the set in the standard GR Corolla. Even so, the driving position is low enough and well placed for pedal and shifter access. The pedal box was also spaced for easy heel-toe downshifts, though the intelligent throttle-blipping downshift assistance system means a driver doesn’t need to do this. Similarly, the lack of rear seats didn’t add any obvious level of noise intrusion, although the 66-pound omission came at obvious cost to carrying capacity. All told, the GRMN Corolla improves on the standard GR Corolla formula in nearly every dynamic way, but does that make it worth the premium?

At $65,655, the GRMN Corolla is a hard sell for the average enthusiast buyer. Sure, it is the best-driving GR Corolla yet—they should all come from the factory in this configuration—but it doesn’t have particularly inspiring acceleration nor mind-numbing on-track performance. Its best feature is pure agility, but even rear-driven sports machines like the venerable $65,395 Cadillac CT4-V Blackwing or the $69,550 BMW M2 offer plenty of hustle these days. Sure, there are few modern performance road cars that could outrun the GRMN Corolla on a gravel track, but that’s not a strong enough use case to sway the typical enthusiast buyer away from more horsepower and bigger engines.

Regardless, the GRMN has already proved itself well priced considering the warmth of the response it has so far received from enthusiasts, many seeking to upgrade from the GR Corolla. They will likely snap up every unit, regardless of markup. And they will be happy.
