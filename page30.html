Broncos, TV legend Tom Jackson dies at 75

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tom Jackson, a 14-year veteran with the Broncos and a longtime fixture on ESPN’s NFL programming, has died. He was 75.

Jackson was a fourth-round pick of the Broncos in 1973. He became a full-time starter in his second season. He started a run of three straight Pro Bowl berths in 1977, the same year the Broncos made their first Super Bowl appearance.

Jackson played until 1986. The next year, he debuted on ESPN. He worked on the network’s Sunday NFL Countdown show and the must-see NFL Primetime, which featured extended highlights packages years before the best plays could be instantly seen on social media.

“All of us are stunned,” Jackson’s long-time on-air partner, Chris Berman, said in a statement. “I’m stunned, we’re stunned and you’re stunned. We’ve lost one of the best human beings ever to walk the planet, Tom Jackson. I was fortunate to work side by side with Tom for 30 years, primarily on NFL Primetime and Sunday NFL Countdown. It was a labor of love for our whole team, both in front of the camera and behind it. We always hoped our viewers enjoyed the shows as much as we did bringing it to them every week.”

Jackson is a member of the Broncos’ Ring of Fame, the franchise’s 50th anniversary team, and the Louisville Ring of Honor. He received the Pete Rozelle Radio-Television Award from the Hall of Fame in 2015.

The recent changes to the Pro Football Hall of Fame’s rules could make Jackson a candidate for the expanded “contributor” category, given that it allows former players to enhance their standing with other contributions to the game. Few have combined an impactful and prolonged playing career with an impactful and prolonged broadcasting career.

We extend our condolences to Jackson’s friends, family, teammates, and colleagues.

The 8 Grittiest Hard Sci-Fi Movies, Ranked

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

What, exactly, makes a movie "gritty" is a relatively subjective matter. For the most part, though, these are films defined by a harsh, unpolished, and realistic tone. They prioritize raw emotional potency over any sort of elegant escapism, and are often visually desaturated, grounded in their storytelling, and full of flawed characters and sharp social commentary. One genre that gets along particularly well with gritty filmmaking is science fiction, as has been demonstrated by many an incredible masterpiece over the years.

But where "soft" sci-fi (a branch of the genre that focuses on human emotions and soft sciences like sociology and political science) is typically more polished, "hard" sci-fi films (which adhere as strictly as possible to real-world physics and natural laws) are often far better-suited for gritty tones. From underappreciated modern gems like Sunshine to foundational classics like THX 1138, plenty of the greatest hard science fiction films ever made can very reasonably be described as gritty in their stories, tone, or aesthetics.

Danny Boyle has time and again proven himself to be one of the most versatile filmmakers currently working in Hollywood and in the UK, and some of his most unique works come from his collaborations with screenwriter Alex Garland. Case in point: Sunshine, which Garland was inspired to write after learning of scientific ideas regarding the heat death of the universe. Boyle and Garland worked closely with scientific advisers like particle physicist Brian Cox in order to ensure the film was as scientifically plausible as possible.

Whenever the creatives behind a hard sci-fi movie hire scientific advisers, that's how you know that you're in for a hell of a ride—and that is, indeed, the best way to describe Sunshine. But even though the movie pivots softly into the realm of far softer science fiction in its third act, it's still one of the most perfect R-rated hard sci-fi movies ever, with a uniquely gritty atmosphere coming largely from the pure, almost surreal existential dread generated by its premise.

While it's almost inarguably among the most underappreciated sci-fi movies of the 21st century so far, Duncan Jones' Moon (his directorial debut) has nevertheless only continued to grow into more and more of a cult classic as the years have passed. It was made on a modest budget and made an equally modest amount of money at the box office, but with the brilliance of Nathan Parker's script and of Sam Rockwell's lead performance, it may well be one of the best hard sci-fi films of the last 26 years.

Moon is not gritty in that it's action-packed or filled with complicated conflicts, but rather in how visceral its story about corporate exploitation and how corporations see humans as disposable really is. Add to that a grimy, desperately industrial aesthetic and the strong sense of psychological realism of Rockwell's performance, and you get one of the most psychologically intense science fiction thrillers that the big screen has ever seen.

It's not perfect, but Sebastián Cordero's Europa Report is nonetheless one of the most intense hard sci-fi movies ever made, as well as one of the most unique found footage movies of all time. Grounded in scientific realism and made to feel all the more matter-of-fact by its found-footage approach to the story, it's a low-budget masterpiece whose non-linear style truly creates a sense of immersion much akin to watching a documentary about space exploration.

To ensure realistic depictions of gravity, radiation physics, and the surface geography of the eponymous Jupiter moon, the people behind the film actually consulted NASA scientists, and it paid off in spades. But on top of feeling scientifically plausible from beginning to end, Europa Report feels unique thanks to how much of an anti-Hollywood depiction of survival in space it presents. Stripping away any traditional form of sci-fi heroism, the movie instead depicts space as a completely indifferent vacuum that kills indiscriminately and punishes any kind of error. It's a cold, unceremonious movie, and hard sci-fi fans wouldn't have it any other way.

Many of history's greatest science fiction movies—hard sci-fi certainly included—have been substantially divisive, and Claire Denis' High Life is no exception. Starring Robert Pattinson at the top of his game, it's one of the most perfect hard sci-fi movies of the past 10 years, and it was developed with the help of physicist and black hole expert Aurélien Barrau as a consultant. Visually stunning and intellectually challenging in ways that not just everyone might appreciate, it's nevertheless one of the most potently thought-provoking sci-fi films in recent memory.

It's a boldly arthouse-coded approach to the genre, one that subverts traditional science fiction tropes by turning its focus inward to the decaying bodies and psyches of its crew, rather than the cold void of space. This makes it a masterpiece that feels far more psychologically gritty than it does physically or aesthetically gritty, and that works surprisingly well under Denis' magnificent direction. It's a movie full of blood, sweat, tears, and all other sorts of bodily fluids, making it irresistibly yet unceremoniously realistic.

Before he revolutionized not only the science fiction genre, but cinema as a whole, with 1977's Star Wars, George Lucas made his feature directorial debut with the social science fiction masterpiece THX 1138. Based on Lucas' 1967 student film Electronic Labyrinth: THX 1138 4EB, the movie received mixed reviews from critics and underperformed at the box office upon release, but in the years since, it has become one of the biggest hard sci-fi cult classics of the entire 20th century.

THX 1138 crafts a totalitarian dystopian society which feels so real that it's nothing short of terrifying.

It's an alienating, deeply alienating kind of sci-fi movie, with a gritty tone that feels as soul-crushingly visceral as it does terrifyingly sterile. Without any horror elements of any sort, THX 1138 crafts a totalitarian dystopian society which feels so real that it's nothing short of terrifying. Many dystopian movies that are described as "gritty" use rusty metal, rain-slicked urban hellscapes, and literal grit in order to achieve that kind of tone, but not THX. Instead, Lucas achieves that feeling of detachment from blindingly white and overwhelmingly empty sterility.

With its having made nearly $800 million dollars at the box office worldwide, Gravity is Alfonso Cuarón's second-highest-grossing movie to date by a pretty decent margin. It's science fiction that leans far more toward the "science" part than the "fiction" part, and that's exactly what makes it one of the greatest hard science fiction movies that the world has seen in years. Led by Sandra Bullock at her very best, it's a must-see for fans of the genre.

There's a wide variety of reasons why Gravity is one of the most masterfully gritty films of its kind, not least of which is the fact that it's among the most perfectly-written hard sci-fi movies ever made. Add to that some of the most groundbreaking CGI that the film industry has ever seen, and it should be perfectly understandable how Cuarón managed to make a film that's so exhausting, so powerfully claustrophobic, and so sensorially punishing.

Shane Carruth's Primer is a cult classic that's absolutely legendary among fans of sci-fi movies made on shoestring budgets, and for good reason. Made for less than $10,000 dollars, it's nevertheless one of the best—and most fascinatingly realistic—time travel movies ever made. Carruth studied physics in order to help him make the plot plausible and the characters' technical dialogue sound as authentic as possible, and the research definitely paid off.

Indeed, Primer is one of those hard sci-fi movies that are perfect from the first scene to the last, a love letter to science fiction filmmaking that's as dedicated as possible to scientific and technological realism. That commitment to realism inevitably ends up translating in the form of pure grittiness as well, which is further boosted by the visceral aesthetics of the movie, taking full advantage of its admirably low budget.

Right after he established himself as a true Hollywood titan with 2004's Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban, Alfonso Cuarón was given a creative blank check to adapt P. D. James' 1992 novel The Children of Men. The result? 2006's Children of Men, which bombed at the box office at the time of its release, but soon started gaining more and more of a cult following. Today, it's so universally acclaimed that it's just about as mainstream of a modern sci-fi classic as they come.

Calling Children of Men the single grittiest hard sci-fi movie of all time is a take as cold as Antarctica. Delta Weight-And-Balance Outage Triggers Atlanta Ground Stop Days Before Thanksgiving

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Delta Air Lines flights bound for Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL) were held at their departure airports on Sunday morning after an outage affected the carrier's weight-and-balance calculations. The issue led the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) to issue a ground stop at Delta's request, affecting hundreds of flights and causing mounting delays.

Delta said it was using "manual workarounds" while its teams worked to restore the automated system. While the ground stop was later downgraded to a ground delay program, the disruption came on the Sunday before Thanksgiving, with the holiday travel rush already underway.

According to the FAA's National Airspace System (NAS) status page, the ground stop on Sunday morning was imposed on Delta flights bound for Atlanta. It was initially scheduled to remain in place until at least 9:00 AM ET, but was downgraded to a ground delay program shortly after 8:40 AM. The change allowed flights to resume operating under restrictions, although delays are expected to continue as the carrier worked through its backlog.

FAA data showed inbound delays averaging 57 minutes, while flight tracking data shows that over 400 flights have been delayed at ATL, accounting for almost 20% of all inbound and outbound flights at the country's busiest airport. According to Atlanta News First, Delta passengers have also reported significant delays at airports including Newark, New York LaGuardia, Reagan National, Washington Dulles, Boston, Baltimore, Miami and Minneapolis.

The issue affected Delta's Automated Load Instruction System (ALIS), which handles loading information used in aircraft weight-and-balance calculations. At the time of writing, Delta had not disclosed what caused Sunday's outage, or whether any flights had been canceled as a result. Simple Flying contacted the airline for comment and received the following statement:

"Delta teams are using manual workarounds this morning to safely get flights departed while work continues to restore automation. We thank our customers for their patience and our people for their professionalism."

In its statement, Delta said its teams were working to restore automated weight-and-balance calculations for departing flights and that it was handling calculations manually. The airline added that it was working with air traffic control to "protect airport gates and tarmac areas from congestion" at ATL.

Weight-and-balance calculations are a critical part of airline operations and are typically handled by automated systems. Every flight needs a final load sheet before departure to confirm total weight and center of gravity after passengers, bags, cargo and fuel have been loaded. These figures are then used to calculate takeoff performance, including the speeds pilots need and the engine thrust required for departure.

When the automated system fails, airlines must complete the calculations manually, adding time to the process for every flight. At a hub as large as Atlanta, slower departures mean aircraft remain at their gates longer, leaving fewer spaces available for arriving flights. Holding inbound Delta flights at their departure airports helps prevent aircraft from arriving in Atlanta without an available gate.

This can help limit congestion on the ground, but it also means delays can build up at airports where Delta aircraft are waiting to depart for Atlanta. Only last week, United Airlines suffered a similar weight-and-balance system outage that forced it to hold departures across its network. The disruption lasted more than 90 minutes and demonstrated how heavily airlines rely on automated systems to keep flights moving.

While there are still some delays and cancelations due to aircraft and crews being out of place, the figures are far better than over the weekend.

The disruption occurred during the opening weekend of the travel rush before Thanksgiving, which falls on Thursday, November 26. US carriers are projected to fly nearly 29 million passengers between November 20 and November 30, representing around 2.7 million per day. Airlines are also stepping up their Thanksgiving capacity year-on-year, offering 38,000 more daily seats than during the same period in 2025.

Like all US carriers, Delta was significantly impacted by the recent nationwide ground stop on November 13 that lasted more than four hours. The stoppage followed a telecommunications failure within the FAA's system and affected flights nationwide. Delta struggled to recover its operations over the following days after aircraft and crews became displaced across its network, resulting in further delays and cancellations.
