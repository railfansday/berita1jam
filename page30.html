How President Trump And The Blue Wave Could Factor In MLB’s Labor Battle

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In less than a month, Major League Baseball is expected to impose a lockout on players as it pushes to implement a salary cap. If the lockout drags into Spring, political forces could push on either side of the labor battle to break an impasse.

On December 1st, just before midnight, it's expected that the owners will lockout the players. In the offseason, the pressure to reach a new labor deal is limited, and not until spring training is threatened will there be urgency for the sides to reach a deal.

Currently, all management proposals have been tied to a salary cap, a red line for players who have consistently opposed one in Major League Baseball. If the game of chicken pushes into missing regular-season games, politicians on both sides of the aisle are likely to jump into the conversation.

We have to go back to the 1994-95 strike to see the last example of the most drastic moves both the league and MLB Players Association took around the salary cap dispute.

After the league canceled the World Series in September of '94, President Clinton announced in October that he was appointing a mediator in an attempt to resolve the conflict. The efforts went nowhere, with each side moving incrementally but coming nowhere near an agreement. By the end of 1994, the league declared an impasse in negotiations and declared that they were implementing a cap system against the players' will. The union immediately filed an unfair labor practice charge with the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB). Frustrated by the lack of progress through mediation, Clinton ordered the sides to the White House and declared that if the sides didn't reach a deal by February 7, 1995, he would take the recommendations of mediator William J. Usery, Jr. on a settlement. The problem was, Clinton's pressure had no teeth as any recommendation would not be binding.

Ultimately, on March 26, 1995, the NLRB voted 3-2 to seek a court injunction forcing the owners to reinstate the provisions of the old collective bargaining agreement. That ultimately landed the case in a Manhattan courtroom in front of U.S. District Court Judge Sonia Sotomayor (yes, the same Sonia Sotomayor who now sits on the Supreme Court of the United States). On March 31, 1995, Sotomayor granted the NLRB's request for the preliminary injunction against the owners, saying, "When a contract ends, the parties must not alter mandatory subjects until a new agreement is reached or a good-faith impasse is reached."

Fast forward to a potential scenario in 2027 with regular-season games lost, the NLRB and the president could, once again, play a role. Trump has already commented on the matter.

"If you don't have a salary cap, you don't have a sport, because they can't help themselves," Trump told reporters on June 5 aboard Air Force One. "Football has a salary cap. They should have done it a long time ago.

"It's shocking, frankly, that they didn't put a cap on many years ago," Trump added, seemingly referring to the MLB strike in 1994 and 1995. "They had a chance to do a cap, but they blew it."

Trump has not been shy about using the bully pulpit in the past and could do so again, applying pressure on the NLRB if the union claims an unfair labor practice against the league. And in Trump's second term, he's aggressively reshaped the NLRB, making it more employer-focused through board filings and appointments.

Remember, the ruling in 1995 by the NLRB board was as a razor thin 3-2 ruling in favor of the players. Now, a ruling for the owners could be more favorable.

Back to the 1994-95 strike, Congress was a passive player. At the time, House Speaker Newt Gingrich stated, "I'm not sure Congress is the right place to try to organize the national pastime." Senate Majority Leader Robert Dole added, "We're very, very reluctant to have Congress get involved."

Note that at the time, both the House and Senate were controlled by the Republicans. By next year, Democrats will have a sizable majority in both as the midterm blue wave will dramatically alter Congress's dynamic.

It's unclear how much stomach the new coalition has for inserting itself into MLB's labor battle, but it is decidedly more union-friendly than it has been in the past.

As a means of applying pressure, one or more bills could be introduced to remove MLB's antitrust exemption, something the league has continually fought to maintain.

Threats of repealing MLB's antitrust exemption have come from both sides of the aisle in the past. In April 2021, several Republicans signed on to the Competition in Professional Baseball Act in an effort to strip MLB of its antitrust exemption. U.S. Senator Cory Booker (D-NJ) also signed on via amicus brief, saying, "For over a century, professional baseball has enjoyed a free pass to break the rules of fair competition because of a flawed Supreme Court ruling—one that the Court itself has acknowledged was a mistake," said Senator Booker. "The one-of-a-kind antitrust exemption gives leagues the power to artificially control salaries, ticket prices, team relocations, and more, harming players and baseball fans alike. It's time the Court fix this error and ensure that baseball, like every other sport, plays by the same rules of fair competition."

8 facts about seasonal affective disorder, from symptoms to treatment, per the NIMH

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

SAD is clinical depression that follows the seasons. See 8 facts from the NIMH on its symptoms, causes, treatments and research.

Plenty of people feel more tired and less cheerful once the days get short, and most of them never think of it as a health problem. The real question is when a low season stops being a passing mood and becomes an illness that needs care. NIH News in Health reports that SAD, short for seasonal affective disorder, is a form of clinical depression tied to the time of year. It belongs with the depression doctors treat at any time of year and deserves the same seriousness, not the shrug people give a gloomy week. Telling a seasonal slump apart from a seasonal illness decides which kind of help fits.

Doctors know far more about how to treat SAD than about what causes it. Researchers point to a mix of influences acting together and have not identified any one exact cause, according to HealthDay. Much of the practical advice about SAD therefore rests on what has been seen to work, not on a settled account of why it works. Researchers can still name the conditions that tend to set off each form of the disorder. Kelly Rohan, a psychologist at the University of Vermont, tells NIH News in Health that shorter days set off the winter pattern, while excessive heat and humidity can prompt the summer one. Anyone deciding what to do about SAD is working from partial knowledge, and that makes a clinician's judgment more useful than a plan a person builds alone.

The National Institute of Mental Health set out to explain seasonal affective disorder to the public, from what the condition is and who tends to develop it to what may be driving it in the brain, how it is treated and how people can take part in research. Millions of Americans are estimated to have SAD. Doctors have treated its winter pattern with light since the 1980s, yet the summer pattern has no treatments designed specifically for it. The useful lesson for a reader is to recognize SAD as a medical condition and raise it early with a professional, not to work through its symptoms as a checklist for diagnosing oneself.

Most people picture seasonal affective disorder as a problem of short, dark winter afternoons, but the condition is defined by the season it keeps returning with, and that season can fall at either end of the year. The familiar form matches the popular image so closely that its other name is simply winter depression, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. The second form takes its name from its own season in the same way and is called summer depression. A person whose depression returns every summer has the same disorder as a person whose depression returns every winter. In each case the name tells a person when the episodes come, and that timing is what makes the illness seasonal.

The summer pattern is the form people are least likely to recognize, because it runs against the winter image and is also the less common of the two. That makes it easy for someone to explain away a low summer as something other than SAD. A mood that sinks every summer deserves the same attention as one that sinks every winter, because the season doesn't change what the condition is. On the question of treatment, "Talk to your doctor," Kelly Rohan, a psychologist at the University of Vermont, told NIH News in Health. People whose low mood arrives with summer have as much reason to follow that advice as people whose low mood arrives with winter.

Knowing the definition turns a low that returns on schedule, in either season, into something to raise with a doctor rather than something to wait out.

The clearest sign of SAD is how long a change in mood and behavior lasts, not any one feeling. People with SAD have symptoms for about 4−5 months each year, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. That stretches past a single season and lasts far longer than a rough week. Anyone wondering about SAD has to look at the full stretch of months, and one gloomy morning tells them almost nothing.

Many signs of winter-pattern SAD are changes in the body and in daily habits rather than feelings of sadness. NCCIH reports that people with this form have low energy and pull away from others. Many of them also oversleep, overeat, crave carbohydrates and gain weight. People can easily write these changes off as ordinary winter habits, and any one of them alone proves little. A reader who recognizes the whole set in themselves should take it to a clinician and not treat it as a self-diagnosis.

Anyone who checks only for the winter signs can miss SAD that comes with warm weather. People with the summer pattern have a different set of signs, including a poor appetite and weight loss, and they often feel restless, agitated and anxious. Friends and doctors can easily mistake that restlessness and anxiety for stress rather than depression.

The sign that matters is a change in how a person sleeps, eats and copes that returns with the same season each year, whether that season is winter or summer.

Doctors diagnose seasonal affective disorder by confirming a depression first, and the season only describes when it occurs. In the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual, published in 2022, SAD appears as a seasonal pattern within a mood disorder and not as a separate disorder, according to StatPearls. The National Institute of Mental Health lists three criteria for the diagnosis, making it a multi-part test of an existing illness rather than a verdict on the winter blues. The test turns on timing: depressive episodes must come more often in one particular season than at other times of year. A pattern like that can only be shown across more than one year, and one rough winter, however bad, isn't yet something a doctor can confirm. Diagnosis is a conversation about past seasons more than a single test, and it helps to remember how earlier winters went.

A questionnaire can be part of the process, but it narrows the possibilities rather than naming the illness. Seasonal timing doesn't even tell a doctor which mood disorder is at work. StatPearls reports that between 15% and 25% of people with bipolar disorder have a seasonal pattern, so a low that comes every winter can belong to bipolar disorder as well as to depression. Before settling on SAD, a clinician also has to set aside other explanations, among them generalized anxiety disorder, dysthymia, adjustment disorder, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder. A seasonal pattern on its own is a clue for a clinician to follow, not a diagnosis.

Putting a name to the pattern matters because the depression usually comes back. Between 50% and 70% of people see it return the next winter, according to StatPearls, so a diagnosis gives a reasonable warning about the coming season. The same range also means a real share of people get through the next winter without another episode, and the pattern is not fixed for life.

A SAD diagnosis describes how someone's illness has behaved so far, and it is worth checking against each new winter rather than treating it as permanent.

The risk of seasonal affective disorder is higher in the north than in the south. People who live in places such as Alaska and New England face a higher risk, while people in places such as Texas and Florida face a lower one, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. These states mark the two ends of a broad regional difference, not a measured rate for any one place. For someone living in the north, a low mood that returns winter after winter deserves a closer look rather than a shrug about the cold.

Age also shapes when SAD tends to appear, and the disorder usually begins in young adulthood. A seasonal pattern that first shows up in the early adult years is worth raising with a doctor while it is new, before a person spends years treating the same slump as a normal part of the calendar. The typical age describes most cases, not all of them, so a first episode later in life doesn't rule SAD out.

A person with SAD can have another condition at the same time, such as an anxiety disorder, panic disorder, an eating disorder, or ADHD. Anyone already living with one of these conditions has good reason to tell a clinician about mood changes that follow the seasons. A clinician is better placed than the patient to sort out which symptoms belong to which problem.

Place, age, and other conditions show when to look harder at a seasonal low, but none of them decides who is safe from it.

Winter depression is often blamed simply on too little sun, but the leading explanation for it is more specific and involves brain chemistry. People with SAD have less serotonin, and those with the winter pattern most of all, the National Institute of Mental Health reports, and people with the winter form also make more melatonin than normal. That points to an imbalance running in two directions at once rather than a shortage of a single chemical. As their serotonin and melatonin levels change, people with SAD lose their normal daily rhythm, according to HealthDay. In that account, winter SAD is as much a disorder of the body's daily clock as of mood. The account gives the winter form a coherent explanation, but it is a theory that researchers are still working to confirm.

The explanation for the summer form reverses the winter one and carries less confidence. In summer-pattern SAD, melatonin levels may run low instead of high, and so far that idea is only a possibility. Researchers have not yet tested these theories in a systematic way, so for people whose depression arrives in warm weather, the cause remains an untested idea. Anyone with the summer form should not assume that what is understood about winter SAD applies to their own case.

Negative thoughts about the season itself may also play a part in SAD. "It is unclear, however, whether these thoughts are causes or effects of the mood disorder, but they can be a useful focus of treatment," the National Institute of Mental Health said. Clinicians can work on that pattern of thinking without first deciding whether it caused the depression or grew out of it.

Treatment does not have to wait for the science of causes to be settled.

The best-known treatment for winter SAD sounds gentle, but it is a strong dose of light taken on a strict schedule. Light therapy is the standard treatment, according to PBS NewsHour. The National Institute of Mental Health reports that the box used for it gives off about 20 times as much light as an ordinary room. A brighter bulb on the desk can't stand in for it. Patients sit in front of the box for about 30−45 minutes daily. The hour of the session and its regularity also count as part of the dose. "The research best supports a half an hour of 10,000 lux of light seven days a week before 8 a.m.," said Dr. Paul Desan, director of the Winter Depression Research Clinic at the Yale School of Medicine. Following that schedule means building an early session into nearly every winter morning, which is a real commitment for anyone who chooses light therapy.

Anyone can buy a light box without a prescription, but some people shouldn't start using one on their own. People with retinal disease or bipolar disorder face particular risks from light therapy, according to PBS NewsHour, and the treatment should begin under a medical professional's supervision. The right first step is a visit to a doctor, not a trip to the store.

People who can't keep a daily light routine still have a proven option. In a direct comparison, a form of cognitive behavioral therapy designed for SAD matched light therapy in how well it worked, according to the National Institute of Mental Health. Desan tells PBS NewsHour that the therapist questions a patient's negative beliefs and urges the patient to stay active, rather than let fatigue lead to a retreat from the activities that help. A reader can weigh whether that kind of work suits them better than a lamp at dawn.

Many people assume that the "sunshine vitamin" can fix a condition tied to dark winters, but it hasn't earned that trust. Research on whether vitamin D eases SAD has not come out consistently, so a pill can't take the place of a light box or therapy.

Take the light routine and the therapy option to a clinician, and treat a vitamin as an extra at most.

People who have had SAD can plan for their next episode before it starts, because the episodes tend to return with the same season. The National Institute of Mental Health says that someone whose past episodes came in winter might gain from beginning treatment ahead of the fall. For them, the decision comes in late summer, while they still feel well and before the days grow shorter. People whose episodes come in summer follow the reverse calendar, and their time to start treatment falls before spring. In both patterns, the signal to act is the timing of last year's episode, not how a person feels today. The practical step is to raise treatment with a clinician before the season arrives rather than in the middle of it.

Starting treatment early is a reasonable step to discuss with a doctor, but it does not guarantee that an episode won't come. Only a few studies have looked at preventing SAD, which is why the advice stays cautious. With so little research done, early treatment counts as a possible help rather than a proven defense. An extended-release form of the medication bupropion has approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. A reader therefore has at least one specific medication to ask a doctor about when planning ahead, even though research on prevention as a whole remains thin. SAD is predictable enough to plan for, but only more studies can show whether that planning keeps it away.

SAD remains a condition that doctors treat with partial knowledge. Research is how medicine will answer the open questions about what causes it, how to treat its summer form and whether it can be prevented. A trial exists mainly to produce knowledge for the patients who come later, not to treat the person who enrolls. "The main purpose of a clinical trial is to gain new scientific knowledge so that others may be better helped in the future," the National Institute of Mental Health said. A person thinking about joining should weigh the decision mainly as a contribution to that knowledge.

Signing up for a study is a different decision from seeking care. A person with active winter depression needs treatment from a clinician first. Joining a trial is a separate choice that someone makes for the sake of others, ideally once their own care is in place.

People run into the unanswered questions about SAD in ordinary decisions, such as whether to buy a vitamin D supplement at the pharmacy. Research on whether vitamin D eases SAD has come back mixed, so a buyer has no firm reason to expect the pill to work. Researchers turn mixed results into firm answers only by gathering more evidence, and clinical studies with enough volunteers are how they gather it. Every question about SAD that remains open today stays open until people agree to take part in that work.

People who volunteer now give the next winter's patients what earlier volunteers gave today's: treatment advice backed by evidence rather than by guesswork.

That is reflected in the data, where YouTube is now king of the spenders, with an estimated $28 billion annually. Thanksgiving’s 7 record-breaking TV moments, and what really drove each one

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Thanksgiving 2025 set NFL, streaming and parade viewing records. See which gains reflect real demand and which reflect Nielsen's new counting.

A growing share of American TV time goes to viewing on each person's own schedule, with people picking shows from apps and libraries instead of tuning in when a network airs them. As that habit spreads, a broadcast that brings tens of millions of people to the same screen at the same moment has become rare. Thanksgiving is the exception, and its audience is getting bigger. Streaming took 46.7% of all TV watch-time in November 2025, according to Nielsen. Even so, the holiday's football audience set a record for the fourth straight year, according to The Associated Press. Networks and the NFL prize the day because viewers keep gathering for it while they drift apart for nearly everything else.

The more people split up across screens, the more a live event they all watch together is worth to the companies that sell it. "What really drives this business is the unique ability of sports to bring people together and create shared experiences in real time, and those shared experiences become more rare and more valuable when there's so much media content out there that is driving people into more isolated experiences," said Mike Mulvihill, president of insights and analytics at Fox Sports. This year's numbers come with a caveat, though. The Associated Press reports that Nielsen switched to a new way of measuring audiences for every event starting Sept. 1, 2025. NFL Commissioner Roger Goodell says the holiday audience probably came in above the league's projections and calls it helpful that the league can now count viewers who went uncounted in the past, according to The Associated Press. For every record, the real question is how much came from more people watching and how much came from better counting.

Axios looked at which 2025 Thanksgiving broadcasts drew record audiences and what drove them, from the Cowboys-Chiefs game and the holiday's night game to streaming and the Macy's $M Thanksgiving Day Parade. The records don't share a single cause. Some depend on a marquee matchup, some on the ways people now reach a broadcast, and one has nothing to do with football at all. The counting question matters to the league as much as to anyone, since in September the NFL had accused Nielsen of undercounting its viewers. What follows weighs each record against the other explanations for it, so readers can tell a lasting appetite from a one-year bump.

Thanksgiving's football audience set a record this year by a margin wide enough to raise questions about what drove it. The holiday's three NFL games averaged nearly 45 million viewers, according to Axios, and they beat last year's record of 34.5 million by a wide margin. A jump that large in a single year needs an explanation before anyone reads it as a surge of new interest. Two explanations besides more fans also fit this number: the way the audience was counted and the games the league placed on the holiday.

The Associated Press reports that Nielsen's count now includes what people watch on smart TVs. The gap between the two records likely combines people who started watching with people who were already watching but weren't counted before. No available figure separates those two groups. The record holds under this season's count, but the size of the jump likely overstates how much interest actually grew.

The matchups on the holiday also shaped the number, and networks compete for a limited supply of the strongest games. Mike Mulvihill, Fox Sports president, insight and analytics, told The Associated Press that "at some level it is a little bit zero sum." Fox has next year's Dallas Thanksgiving game, so next year's audience will depend on a different network's choices and a different set of matchups. A repeat of this year's average would take more than steady demand.

The record proves that Americans still gather in front of one broadcast on Thanksgiving. Whether more of them gather than a year ago is still an open question.

The NFL's regular-season audience record has become a Dallas Thanksgiving tradition. The Cowboys' holiday game against the Kansas City Chiefs had 57.2 million viewers on CBS, the biggest regular-season audience in NFL history, according to Axios. NFL.com reports that the previous record came from the Giants-Cowboys game on Thanksgiving 2022, and this year's audience was 36% larger. A gap that wide is too large to put down to ordinary change from one year to the next. The credit belongs first to the date and the team. Much of the country already spends Thanksgiving afternoon watching the Cowboys, and a record in that slot says more about that habit than about a sudden new appetite for football.

This year's matchup added stakes that the holiday slot doesn't guarantee. "It so happens they were both coming off last-second, come-from-behind victories and they're fighting for their playoff lives," David Berson, CBS Sports president and CEO, told The Associated Press. Those stakes grew out of how both teams' seasons unfolded in the weeks before the holiday. The Cowboys won 31-28, according to NFL.com, in a game that stayed in doubt to the end. The audience reached its peak of 61.3 million in the final moments, well above the average for the whole broadcast. Nobody can schedule a three-point finish, or two teams with their playoff hopes at risk on the same afternoon.

The record shows what the holiday can do when everything lines up, not what it will do every year.

Thanksgiving afternoon fills living rooms even when the Dallas Cowboys aren't playing. Some 47.7 million people watched the Green Bay Packers and the Detroit Lions on Fox, according to Axios. That crowd beat Fox's regular-season record of 42.06 million, set by a Giants-Cowboys game in 2022, according to The Associated Press. For a short time, the early game also held the record for the biggest regular-season audience in NFL history, until the later Cowboys-Chiefs game on CBS topped it. The holiday's largest audience grew so much in one year that a record set in the first game fell before the day's football was over. The fair reading is that the whole afternoon drew a deeper audience this year, not that Green Bay and Detroit held some special appeal.

The average also understates how many people watched at the same moment. At the game's busiest stretch, 57.96 million viewers were tuned in, according to The Associated Press. That peak is the truer measure of how many households the early game gathered at once, and a crowd of that size is what makes the holiday so valuable to the networks that sell it.

The Fox audience is not only people watching Fox. Awful Announcing reports that the total combines viewers on Fox and on Tubi, and that the early game's audience rose 28% from the same game last year. Nielsen changed how it counts this season, though, so this year can't be lined up cleanly against last year. Some of the gain is likely real appetite, given how far the game passed the old Giants-Cowboys mark. Some of it comes from wider counting, and nobody can yet say how much of each.

The record also rested on a choice Fox made. "I think it was entirely strategically correct to really go for it this year and try to get the biggest number," Mike Mulvihill, Fox Sports president, insight and analytics, told The Associated Press. He says Fox will have to weigh how aggressive to be next year, when the network has the Dallas Thanksgiving game. The executive in charge of Fox's analytics won't promise the same all-out push, so the 2025 number came from a deliberate bet as much as from demand.

Next year's early game will show whether the appetite lasts without Fox pushing as hard for the biggest number.

The Thanksgiving night game drew the smallest crowd of the holiday's three games, and it was still a huge audience. It came at the end of a long day, and it matched the Cincinnati Bengals and the Baltimore Ravens rather than the holiday's best-known teams. Even so, 28.4 million people watched, according to Axios, and few broadcasts ever see a crowd that size. That total combines NBC, Peacock and Telemundo, Awful Announcing reports, so it includes English-language TV, Spanish-language TV and streaming in one count. Fans followed the holiday's last game on whatever screen they use, and together they still formed a mass audience.

This number also includes some viewers who were simply counted for the first time. Nielsen's new way of measuring live sports is estimated to add 5-15% to audiences, according to Awful Announcing, and part of the 28.4 million likely watched in past years without showing up in the count. That cuts both ways. NBC did not need a marquee pairing to post a big figure, but nobody can read that figure as proof that interest in the holiday is growing.

With no afternoon slot and no headline teams, the night game is the closest measure of the day's own appeal. That measure supports a modest claim. Tens of millions of Americans watch football on Thanksgiving night even without the league's biggest names on the field, so the holiday's audience has a high floor. This year's figure cannot separate a lasting rise from a counting bump, though.

A fairer test of whether the Thanksgiving night habit is growing will come next year, when both night games are counted the same way.

Thanksgiving viewers turned to apps to watch football in far larger numbers this year. The holiday games averaged 2.2 million streaming viewers, a 58% jump from last year and streaming's best Thanksgiving yet, according to NFL figures reported by Axios. A rise that steep in one year reflects a sudden shift in how people watched rather than a slow drift onto apps. At first glance, that shift suggests the holiday audience is leaving traditional TV for streaming services.

On the same holiday, people also used apps in large numbers to watch live TV channels, the same kind of broadcast that streaming is supposed to replace. Nielsen reports that viewing live channels through pay-TV apps reached its second-highest daily level ever on Thanksgiving and made up 10.1% of all TV use, well above its share across November. Only on Super Bowl Sunday in February 2025 did more people watch live channels this way. Nielsen tracks this channel viewing separately from streaming services, so the two figures measure different habits. Both, however, point to people on Thanksgiving using apps to reach TV that was airing live.

The rest of the day's viewing suggests that people may be adding apps as another way to watch broadcast TV rather than abandoning it. If so, the streaming record is partly a record about access, with interest in the holiday's games reaching viewers through a different kind of service. On that reading, the 58% jump says less about new viewers than it first appears and more about broadcasters' ability to reach their audience on whatever screen it uses.

The holiday's crowd didn't leave for streaming. Streaming became one more way that crowd gathers.

Thanksgiving's records came in a season when NFL audiences are larger across the board, not only on the holiday. More than 17 million people watched the average NFL game through week 12, according to Axios, which means an ordinary regular-season game now draws an audience the size of a major TV event. That average is also climbing, with per-game viewership up 6% from last year. Part of the holiday's gain is the same rise in football viewing that fans brought to every other week of the season. Thanksgiving set its records in a year when more people were watching NFL games on ordinary Sundays, too.

The season's increase is no cleaner a measure of fan demand than the holiday records are. The 6% gain sets this year's games against last year's, and Nielsen changed how it measures audiences in between. Nielsen's new method adds data from smart TVs to its traditional representative panel of viewers, so the company now counts viewing on sets that report what they play. People the panel alone would have missed can therefore show up this year as growth. None of this takes away from what Thanksgiving accomplished. It means the same caution applies to the whole year: on these numbers, nobody can yet say how much of the holiday's gain or the season's gain comes from more people watching.

Only comparisons counted the same way in both years will show whether fans found a new appetite for football or Nielsen found a new way to count them.

Americans gather around one broadcast on Thanksgiving even when there is no game to watch. Viewership for the Macy's $M Thanksgiving Day Parade grew 8% from last year, according to Axios, and its audience of 34.3 million was a record. Those viewers watched balloons and floats, with no score to follow and no final whistle to wait for. This year's gain was not the parade's first recent increase, since its audience also rose 6% in 2023, according to CNBC. No team, rivalry or pairing of opponents was part of the broadcast, so the parade's record cannot be credited to a matchup the way a football record can.

This year's record can't settle whether broadcast TV is holding its ground, because the 34.3 million total combines NBC viewers with Peacock streams. In 2023, about 22.3 million people watched the parade on traditional TV alone, according to Variety figures reported by CNBC. The gain since then can't be credited to broadcast on its own. That 2023 audience made the parade a strong argument for old-fashioned television. "When it gets the right content in the right context, it blows everything else away," Patrick Crakes, a sports media consultant and former Fox Sports executive, said of traditional TV at the time. This year's blended total can neither confirm nor refute his case.

Nobody can yet say whether Nielsen's new counting method, which applies to the NFL games, also changed the parade's figure. The parade's rise can't be traced in any part to that change, and it can't be cleared of it either.

People clearly still gather together on Thanksgiving morning, but this year's record can't show whether they do it on broadcast TV or on a streaming app.
