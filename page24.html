According to Rockstar, if you want a strong relationship in GTA 6, you have to play both sides of that relationship. That means not just playing as Jason and doing nice things for Lucia, but also playing as Lucia and treating Jason well.

GTA 6 review: "An open world with truly astonishing scale, scope, and quality awaits"

GTA 6 is the highest-fidelity sandbox of its generation. Rockstar has built an open world of truly astonishing scale, scope, and quality. There's a degree of sophistication to everything GTA 6 does, and there's no aspect of the series' gameplay that hasn't been improved over past instalments.

It was around the 80-hour mark that the panic started to set in. I thought I had done and seen it all. Ran narcotics across the Leonida Keys. Exiled myself from Port Gellhorn. Fought bikers in Ambrosia. Joined a cult. Robbed banks. Partied. I created a life worth living in the heart of Vice City.

Jason and Lucia's story may have been over, but an adventure of my own was just beginning. While I had accomplished a lot in GTA 6, I never did find the time to refuel my beloved '65 Vapid Dominator and drive the long, winding roads up to Lummox County. It's astonishing to think so much time could be spent exploring Leonida, only for its northern border to remain something of a mystery.

Going from a fan at GTA 4's midnight launch to professionally covering the series has been a career highlight, including reviewing GTA 5 in X360 magazine. I'm never more than a few moments from diving back into Rockstar's worlds. After finishing GTA 6's story, I'm now well past 100 hours still exploring Leonida, using a PS5 Pro code provided by the publisher. – Reviewer Josh West

Then again, there are still so many mysteries left for me to solve. So many incidental interactions that have the capacity to spiral into pure carnage. Diversions to discover that may lead to utter disarray. There are streets I've yet to set foot on, and shops I still haven't visited in my hours-long search for a quality leather jacket. There's at least one financial institution that I haven't yet figured out how to escape from without five-stars' worth of VCPD officers shooting me to smithereens.

I fear I may never see or experience all that this world has to offer. And yet, after 13 years of waiting for a new Grand Theft Auto game, perhaps that's the way it should be.

There are precious few development studios who are willing to play in these sorts of contemporary urban environments. Such is the inherent complexity of regulating an underlying simulation where even the simplest act creates such a wide array of possible consequences. Rockstar Games hasn't just set a new benchmark; it has delivered what is undoubtedly the highest-fidelity sandbox in existence.

How Rockstar has been able to build something of this scale, scope, and quality will be dissected for years to come. It's easy to marvel at the near total lack of loading screens, the speed of character switching, and an exceedingly stable performance with minimal pop-in – despite absurd population density and the speeds at which you may traverse such a topographically diverse playspace.

These aspects are certainly impressive, but they aren't what ultimately elevates GTA 6 into being such a singular experience. Rather, there's a maturity behind the ethos of its core mechanical design, a cohesion in the way that its systems combine, which helps separate it from what the studio has accomplished in the past. In GTA 6, you can feel Rockstar's relentless pursuit of iteration, and the studio's ambition to create the most grounded instalment of the Grand Theft Auto franchise to date.

GTA 6 has the potential to be exceedingly divisive, particularly for those whose only point of interaction with the series thus far has been GTA 5. The life of crime you build for yourself in Leonida is far less chaotic and caustic than the adventures splattered throughout the streets of San Andreas. If the 2013 instalment was a faint echo of a Michael Bay blockbuster, then its successor is more Michael Mann – no less entertaining, but more considerate in how action feeds carnage and frames characters.

Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos – partners in crime, ensnared in a criminal conspiracy that quickly outstrips their understanding of an underworld that they once played in for idle thrills. The dual-protagonist foundation works exceedingly well for GTA 6, allowing you the space to grapple with two distinct personalities who are, in a sense, eternally bound to one another. You get to decide why that may be on a daily basis – for family, for honor, or for the simple promise of a bigger payday.

The interplay between the two characters – born through a combination of nuanced writing, strong performances, and intricate character animations – feeds what is perhaps the most realistic and adult portrayal of a romantic relationship in video game history. Everyday acts of kindness bring Jason and Lucia together, indifference drives them apart. This isn't captured as part of some tawdry in-game checklist, but through deliberate, empathetic actions.

In GTA 5, all that L2 was ever good for was aiming a weapon – in GTA 6, it also lets you hold hands with your partner. Interactions only get more detailed from there. Check in with each other over the phone, hit the gym together, hustle drunks at bars, or raid banks with his-and-hers matching tactical rifles. The connection you organically foster between the two characters (or allow to decay) only fuels a more considerate narrative that acts as a catalyst for endless free-flowing excursions across Leonida.

The thematic coherence necessitated by the narrative arc acts as a foundation, feeding other critical changes to the way Grand Theft Auto has traditionally played. If Jason and Lucia's relationship feels real, then so too should the world they express themselves within.

You can only carry two handguns and two longarms at a time, for example, and brandishing a weapon will cause the public to react in different, volatile ways. Police presence isn't some glorified cone of vision, but rather it works as a communicative force to be feared – able to track your appearance, vehicles, and criminal activities between city limits. All of this (and more) contributes to a feeling of true, immersive role-play within Leonida.

With Red Dead Redemption 2, Rockstar Games sought to create a reactive virtual world. The studio aspired to near-total immersion – action, interactions, and character development feeding off the sandbox's simulation of an everyday reality. In that sense, GTA 6 is a true sequel to RDR2. Rockstar has pursued relentless iteration of those Old West foundations here, leaning into those immersive qualities where it makes sense for the criminal-fantasy GTA fosters, and pulling back when they don't.

Unlike RDR2's Arthur Morgan, your hair and beard won't naturally grow over time. Weight and health fluctuations are less severe. Eating and exercising will impact the definition of Jason and Lucia's bodies, and resting helps to ease the exhaustive toll of constant criminality, but you'll never find yourself entirely at the mercy of these systems. Engagement with the Vice City lifestyle simply helps further the feeling that you are living life on your own terms.

Rockstar has gone to great lengths to inject more "freedom and flexibility" into its main mission design, all in an effort to reduce the number of fail states you encounter for daring to be experimental.

GTA 6 also features a more nuanced version of the Red Dead Redemption 2 honor system, with your reputation fed throughout the world more organically. It's one of the more fascinating new systems, and a sharp diversion from prior games in the series that have never really commented on your criminality. Criminal Profiles are built gradually over time, ranging from Professional to Irredeemable, based on the way you commit crimes and handle pressure.

The improvements to graphical fidelity and animation quality, and the lengths Rockstar has gone to create a virtual world with a functioning ecology, have meant that there are more human reactions from the populace to your presence than ever before. This intersects well with the Criminal Profile system, which (while never overly judgemental) does gradually convince you to define a moral compass and follow your own value system.

There was a point where I realized that I had inadvertently sunk the entirety of my BuckMe bank account into an arsenal dedicated entirely to stealth action. I was raiding banks, drug labs, and gang compounds like GTA 6 was some sort of perverse Florida-set Metal Gear Solid. Sneaking my way into cover and onto rooftops, tracing enemy patrol patterns with a cigarette in one hand and silenced pistol in the other. I'd like to tell you that smoking before every firefight was because it temporarily boosts the Focus bar, but at some point I had become addicted to the aesthetic of it all.

A stealth-action approach to encounters in GTA 6 worked in my favor because I was pursuing a Professional criminal profile – assigned by the worldwide reputation system for those who lean towards non-lethal incapacitations and clean getaways. I gravitated towards this style of play because of the Focus system, which iterates smartly on aspects of RDR's Eagle Eye and Max Payne's Bullet Time. Skillfully shooting weapons out of hands, or capping enemies in the knee before watching them tumble down staircases in slow motion, is just as satisfying as striding out of cover with a shotgun.

Combat is one of many aspects where Rockstar has built precipitously beyond GTA 5. Placing bullets requires real aiming and precision. Weapon feedback is weighty, with each weapon type and manufacturer variant carrying its own unique handling profile. Survival requires movement and cover. Improvements have been made to the driving models too, with narrower streets necessitating more dexterity behind the wheel – not to mention countless more variables at play, from the threat of running out of fuel to the scores of modifications that can be applied to your favorite cars.

There are degrees of sophistication to everything that GTA 6 does. There isn't an aspect of the series' gameplay that hasn't been improved over past instalments. It's a little slower and certainly more balanced, but no less entertaining. It's immediately noticeable that the artificial intelligence guiding the actions of both your partner and the NPCs who inhabit Vice City is greatly improved too, with some of GTA 6's best moments coming as you ask Jason to take the wheel while you lean out of a window as Lucia, a chattering assault rifle chewing through the tyres and rotary blades of a pursuing police force.

If more nuanced storytelling has the potential to be divisive, then the revisions to the Wanted and Heat systems could be downright controversial. The police are more attuned to your actions than ever, actively tracking a number of variables at any one time in an effort to curtail crimes both big and small.

The return of a six-star wanted level is great for those looking for more of a challenge, although the Heat system – which can identify you after a crime based on factors such as your appearance, vehicle, outfit, visible weapon, partner's appearance, and whether you were recorded by CCTV – introduces a whole new dynamic to Grand Theft Auto. Some will love it, others will hate it.

This system can fluctuate between being a little frustrating (particularly at lower Wanted levels, for more incidental infractions) and utterly thrilling once the pressure is on, particularly if you're holding hundreds of thousands of dollars in stolen jewellery, market bills, and narcotics between Jason, Lucia, and the storage of their personal vehicle – loot is lost if not properly funnelled through fences. And when the Heat and Wanted system are on your back, it evokes this almost desperate pressure to survive a city that is collapsing in on you.

There's a sort of useful friction introduced through the revised Wanted and Heat system, overlapping with more freeform opportunities presented throughout the world itself. While GTA 5 is best remembered for its cinematic, structured heists, its successor finds its best moments as you decide on a whim to stick up a gas station or start smashing up the display cabinets in a jewelry store whilst you're between objectives. Bank robberies present massive opportunity and carry enormous risk, necessitating careful, tactile preparation before you burst through doors with weapons raised.

The high-speed pursuits which can unfurl out of these encounters get gradually more out of control until they don't – Busted, Wanted, or free to fight and love for another day. Your Heat level and the world's awareness of your crimes is tracked intuitively on a more dynamic HUD, and I appreciate just how many opportunities there are to escape the police depending on any number of external factors.

One of my favorite escapes saw me fleeing a large bank in Downtown Vice City – a densely populated area with streets packed full of police, pedestrians, and other vehicles. Throwing stash bags to my partner, spilling out of the vault and onto the streets, I was able to gain just a little distance from the cops and pull into a multistory carpark. Precious seconds to hide Jason's weapons in the trunk of his car, swap out Lucia's outfit, and hotwire a new vehicle. Clambering into the car, I manually shut off the headlights and engine, and ducked beneath the steering wheel – the patrolling police passed none the wiser, affording me the space to slip out of the garage slowly.

The sheer scale of Leonida is something to behold. It's a playspace which rewards curiosity and creativity. It's impressive, especially when you remember it has all been "curated, considered, and created by a human hand."

It's these sorts of unscripted sequences where GTA 6 shines brightest, although that isn't to say that the main story isn't packed with its fair share of memorable moments. There will be some debate as to whether the standout GTA 6 heists rise to the levels of GTA 5's 'The Paleto Score' and GTA 4's 'Three Leaf Clover', but they certainly come close.

Core mission design is still cinematic and largely linear by design, although you do have wider space to experiment in approach and escape than in previous Grand Theft Auto games – mission parameters can be broad, and it's up to you to decide how to achieve them. Rockstar has introduced the ability to manually retry a mission from its last checkpoint, further exorcising some of the series' more frustrating demons.

GTA 6 also happens to be exceedingly well-written. Missions may be grounded in the virtual reality of Leonida, but they still have scope to be ridiculous. Mysteries unfurl from QR codes embedded in the world. Snippets from a radio news broadcast can make you laugh in one moment and pay off as a chance encounter in the world some 40 hours later. Random citizens will make you laugh, moments between Jason and Lucia will make you cry. It's a true joy to spend time in this world.

I have Jason and Lucia start their road trip up to Lummox County in Key Lento, just a short drive to the federal penitentiary where they had once served time. Now, it's just the open road. Their favorite outfits packed into the trunk of an over-torqued Vapid Dominator, along with a small arsenal of weapons, ammo crates, body armor, and parachutes.

The sun shines brightly; the radio tuned to V-Rock as hits from the '80s soundtrack a transition from lush ocean views to the quiet character of Hamlet. A quick stop at Sara's Unisex Salon for a fresh cut for Jason, and makeover for Lucia. Later, an unintended diversion into Southside after a small collision on Interstate 97 led an aggravated driver to jackknife my whip and feed bullets into the windshield – a local Pay 'n' Spray draining what little Pocket Cash I had on me for repairs. The sun started to set.

I stop to refuel somewhere around Vice City's Little Cuba district. An eruption of rain washing Lucia's makeup down her face, her gaze fixed entirely on another bay at the garage – a drunk driver pours gasoline into the flatbed of his truck. On the road and out of the city, the subtle changes in landscape reveal a world of contrast and contradictions. The changing colors of police vehicles, the different accents and demeanours of everyday citizens at roadside restaurants, indicate a crossing of boundaries.

Day transitions to night, night back to day. The weather shifting constantly. The vehicle splutters as roads become more uneven the further we cruise away from the big city. I switch from driver to passenger, smoking cigarettes as Jason and Lucia chatter about their lives. Eventually, vast farmlands settle into dense forest canopies beneath the shadow of Mt. Kalaga National Park. It took me 80 hours to see the dense foliage and lush, leafy green hues of Lummox County. And there's no telling how many more hours await me there.

In Grand Theft Auto 6, Rockstar Games has created the ultimate time vampire. This is an open-world that is driven entirely by freedom and consequence, where every one of your actions ultimately impacts the lives of Jason and Lucia. GTA 6 invites you to live a life in Leonida. A world that is as beautiful and it is deadly; as full of heart as it is heartbreak. GTA 6 is an essential play for PS5 and Xbox Series X owners.

My GTA 6 review was written based on a PS5 Pro playthrough, with the game code provided to me by Rockstar Games. And it must be said, the improved ray tracing to help bolster lighting and shadow on PS5 Pro is pretty damned delightful. Vice City looks gorgeous at night.

So long as we're clear that I reviewed GTA 6 on PS5 Pro. I was very impressed with the performance. Rockstar targeted 30fps on all platforms, but I didn't encounter any fluctuations. There was infrequent pop-in, but that was only during play sessions which stretched beyond eight hours.

I spent over 100 hours playing GTA 6 for review. The Jason and Lucia story is all wrapped up, all optional missions are done, and I've made some pretty good headway on getting through a lot of the activities. Time to hunt for some collectibles!

I have completed every Grand Theft Auto game. Yes, even the ones released for PS1. I have very fond memories of attending the midnight launch for GTA 4, and even fonder memories of reviewing GTA 5 way back in 2013 for X360 Magazine.

This will be a contentious debate, but there's a very good argument to be made that Grand Theft Auto 6 is the best Rockstar game. It's certainly a better overall experience than GTA 5, and more entertaining than Red Dead Redemption 2.

More than a car: in GTA VI, your vehicle is a mobile base of operations to stash your guns, fits, money and drugs.

Should you get into a bust-up with the law after a jewelry store smash-and-grab gone wrong, the game’s action is frenetic. Jason and Lucia each have a “criminal profile”—be unnecessarily violent and you’ll get a “psycho” rating—that adds replayability and roleplay. It’s the closest GTA has honed in on the art of crime. The feds will surround the store you’re robbing. “Come out right now with your hands above your head!” Do things right, and you’ll have prepped your partner with a getaway car out back. Vice City alone could be an entire game in its own right, complete with distinct districts, an explorable international airport, and more. Yet, Rockstar spoils us with a massive expanse of varied towns, high-class beach resorts, lush green national parks, murky swamplands and sun-kissed island keys. All packed with people that range from useful, to funny, to completely unsavory (shout-out to the old woman who took one look at my Jason and said “I’mma take my teeth out and suck the skin off that dick”). Frame rate dips don’t spoil gameplay, but the slowdown is noticeable when you’ve punted a couple of grenades into passing cars to set off multiple explosions at once.

NPCs don’t just feel like backdrop mannequins anymore, but real people with an incredible range of faces, body types and outfits.

Every frame a photograph. Stop anywhere in GTA 6, and you’ll likely find something enticing to see, hear or interact with.

All it took to make this world was Rockstar founding an entire studio to work solely on Leonida’s population. A quarter of a century later, VI shows Rockstar at the peak of entertainment in its very own right.

Caramelized Cabbage Pasta

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This pasta is the easiest way to cram an entire head of cabbage into one comforting dinner. The secret? Cooking it in butter over a long, relaxed period of time until it softens into a decadent, caramelized tangle. Paired with spaghetti, heavy cream, and some Dijon mustard and lemon zest to brighten everything up, this dish is a celebration of how versatile cabbage can be. Feel free to top your bowl with lots of fresh dill, or leave it unadorned with nothing more than an extra sprinkle of cheese.

Cook 12 oz. spaghetti in a large pot of boiling generously salted water, stirring occasionally, until very al dente, about 1 minute less than package directions. Drain, reserving 2 cups pasta cooking liquid. Set aside.

Melt 4 Tbsp. unsalted butter in same pot over high heat. Add 1 large head of green cabbage (about 3 lb.), cored, thinly sliced, and 6 garlic cloves, thinly sliced, and cook, stirring often, until cabbage is very tender and browned in spots, 18–25 minutes.

Add zest of 1 lemon, 2 oz. Parmesan, finely grated (about 1 cup), 1½ cups heavy cream, 2 Tbsp. Dijon mustard, and reserved pasta to cabbage and toss to coat. If sauce is too thick, add pasta cooking liquid a splash at a time, tossing constantly, until glossy. Taste pasta and season with kosher salt and freshly ground pepper.

Divide pasta among shallow bowls and top with finely chopped dill if desired.

It’s easier to take than the sociological theme in Green Book, the egregious previous film of director Peter Farrelly. I Play Rocky regains the genial humanism of such Farrelly brothers’ movies as Me, Myself and Irene, Stuck on You, and The Ringer, and especially the Farrelly brothers’ script for Outside Providence.

Kiki Seto (Talia Shire) and Anthony Ippolito (Sylvester Stallone) in I Play Rocky

Stallone’s ambition is epitomized by his insistence that the Rocky screenplay he wrote in four days and sold to producers Irwin Winkler and Robert Chartoff can be filmed only starring himself. He demands, “I play Rocky.” That’s not a playground taunt in the milieu of financial bargaining and egotism that’s gleefully depicted by a series of Hollywood types — P. J. Byrne and Toby Kebbell as Winkler and Chartoff, Rob Demery as Joe Frazier, and a standout cameo by Trevor St. John as a self-serious fight coordinator.

In Hollywood’s gladiatorial circus, Stallone’s striving and eventual success become a credible moral victory. He must claim his own identity and career path separately from his worldly father Frank (Matt Dillon gives a moving characterization of a parent’s cynicism, affection, and personal regret). His wife Sasha (AnnaSophie Robb) encourages him: “I’d rather be optimistic and be wrong than pessimistic and be right.” These backstage details (including a hostile producer who dismisses Stallone as “a droopy-eyed hack actor”) make I Play Rocky a credible and appealing saga of Stallone’s indefatigable confidence. The movie rejects the sort of dark, objectionable narcissism that we saw in the Springsteen hagiography Deliver Me from Nowhere.

It is important that the filmmakers accurately measure Rocky’s significance. Farrelly and Gamble mix film history with good sense and humor when portraying wild-eyed director John Avildsen (Jay Duplass) deep in his own world of pragmatic professionalism (“Great art never comes through compromise”). They also capture Stallone’s own, uncanny sense of achieving the proper effect — not so much when Rocky emotes to his manager Mickey as when, in a startling shot, he points to details in a big-screen image of Kirk Douglas in Champion, from 1948.

Putting Rocky in proper perspective is a curatorial feat. It recognizes Stallone’s film as a work of pop lore. Its popularity gave Hollywood the opportunity to ignore scary powerful art like Scorsese’s Taxi Driver in favor of the folk and commercial elements that are easily enjoyed in American culture — although missing from Millennial film culture. Remember: In 1976, Rocky seemed an artificial retread of old boxing films, pilfering the ethnic sentimentality of Marty, On the Waterfront, and Requiem for a Heavyweight as if Muhammad Ali had never happened.

But I Play Rocky works differently. It recalls Stallone’s response to Wepner (“This guy won’t go down!”) to achieve the authentic Millennial pop lore of Better Man, Song Sung Blue, Back 2 America, Power Ballad, and the upcoming Madden. (These restorative myths correct the disappointing Creed sequels that failed to live up to Rocky.) I Play Rocky channels Stallone’s personal perseverance to enlighten our current demoralized sensibilities.

Exclusive: Germ DM Is a Signal Alternative That’s Now on Android

Tessa Brown is visibly excited to talk to me about Germ DM, her secure messaging platform. It’s now available on Android, a major expansion for the small startup. Her eyes light up as she explains how her ambitious, three-person team—including an ex-Apple engineer who worked on iMessage—could pull off this platform expansion.

“It's essential to be able to talk to everybody, and that's really what this Android launch unlocks for us,” she says. Brown was previously a lecturer at Stanford University, and her cofounder, Mark Xue, worked on iMessage’s privacy provisions. The duo first launched the iOS app for Germ in 2023.

The platform expansion makes it easier to recommend Germ as an interesting alternative to Signal, especially for early adopters curious about trying new ways to message others and connect with the world.

My favorite aspect about Germ is that the platform doesn’t nudge you to attach a phone number. “Germ is a multi-identity messenger,” Brown says. “The way walled gardens are constructed means that every time you log into an app you get one identity that the app owns.” On Germ, it’s not about creating a permanent handle that you give to everyone; multiplicity is central to this messenger’s pitch.

Tessa Brown and Mark Xue, cofounders of Germ Network.

The app revolves around “burner cards.” Each card works like a separate phone line built into the app. Instead of a dedicated number or a public handle, you can share these Germ cards in two ways: a QR code or a direct link. You can scan QR codes inside the app if meeting someone in person. For digital connections, you can generate a custom link tied to the card, from just one to 100.

After downloading the app, you create these cards by tapping on the green plus button under the Me tab. Choose the name, pronouns, and photo you want to use for that card. Cards associated with your Germ account are not mutually discoverable, and who you connect with on each card is private. As the name suggests, if you delete these “burners” at any time, the connections associated with that card will no longer be able to contact you, and those chats will be removed from your device.

The Germ network runs on Messaging Layer Security, built by the Internet Engineering Task Force as an end-to-end encryption standard. When you are exchanging those “burner cards,” you are actually exchanging cryptographic keys with your new contacts. However, Germ has not undergone a third-party audit, though Brown is open to doing so in the future.

“People want to share different parts of themselves with different people,” Brown says. “Queer folks get Germ immediately; it's not just queer people. It's also people who are gig workers and creatives that have multiple jobs.” Germ is built to share with people in different parts of your life and let them contact you in a central hub through distinct messaging channels.

Xue is serious about the work Germ put into building a secure platform, including addressing the looming issue of quantum computing. Essentially, security experts worry that super-powerful computers could break encryption protections and reveal previously private messages. Developers at large messaging platforms, like iMessage and Signal, have built protections against these quantum concerns. “What we've accomplished as a small team is we've reached the same level of security,” he says. Germ publicly laid out how the platform claims to be “quantum secure.”

Germ’s most recent success came from its integration with Bluesky. Since that social media platform is built on the Authenticated Transfer Protocol, Bluesky users can connect their existing handles to Germ and display it as a messaging button in their profile. This lets friends on the platform easily send you encrypted texts. While many users enjoy that platform-specific integration, Germ’s expansion to Android is a fresh opportunity to meet a new user base.

Brown already has her sights set on Germ’s next move: group chats.

“We’ve worked together this whole time over the belief that the future of communication is in private chats,” she says. For now, the platform supports one-on-one conversations, but the team is ready to build support for larger, group threads.

It might be hard to convince your group chat to switch to a new app, but flashing a QR code to a stranger you just met to continue a conversation could actually work.

Grand Theft Auto 6 review

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

My best attempt at summing up Grand Theft Auto 6 came from a conversation with a friend about something else entirely. This friend was telling me about a theory they'd recently heard, and half agreed with, that just so happened to layer over this game perfectly. Americans, they said, paraphrasing, have found a new 'third place': the car.

I'm slightly skeptical of the obsession with third places these days - love the concept; less convinced that societal bonds are collapsing because today's men aren't spending enough time at their barber's. But here it felt just right: GTA 6 is, of course, a game both about America and about the personal vehicle. The clue's in the name. And as you play GTA 6, you realise, over and over, that the car is where so much of this game happens. It's where you take calls that lead to missions or story developments; where you pick up, hang out with, and chat to your partner; where you race away from police, gangs, wild animals and weirdos. Where you do much of your discovery of this properly, disorientatingly, astoundingly big and detailed world. Where you'll be when you find the most 'emergent events'. And, more than it's ever been before, the car is now an essential partner in itself; a place to stow weapons, disguises, and stolen goods - a companion it feels almost impossible to do without.

I could go on about this. GTA 6 is a game of perpetual motion, as protagonists Jason and Lucia do their best to outrun a world that so often feels a step ahead - and which often culminates in some of the most exhilarating, astonishing chase sequences ever built in a video game. GTA 5, its Los Santos-set predecessor, was aware enough of the importance of the car to name an in-game canyon after Reyner Banham, the British architectural critic who described Los Angeles' freeway system, one of the city's "four ecologies", as "Autopia". So yes: the car is the third place, in modern America and in GTA 6. The place of self-actualisation between work and home - two points that, for GTA 6's protagonists in particular, seem to always be in a state of flux. Maybe the car's their true home. For the longest time I really thought that idea summed up GTA 6 quite nicely. Until I didn't.

Reducing GTA 6 to just the car, I realised, looking at it through only that one lens, forces you to be too narrow. You miss too much of it. What about the romance? The sensuality? The people and the natural world? And so what I've come to settle on instead - and honestly I expect this to change many more times over the coming days and weeks too, as even more of this horizonless game rolls back to reveal itself - is that GTA 6 just doesn't have a single thread to pull on. No one thing that, when properly grasped, suddenly draws it into a manageable shape.

I've no doubt we'll all try. People have, for whatever reason, always reached for the most reductive ideas when they talk about Grand Theft Auto in particular. Maybe it's a natural response to something too big and unwieldy to instantly get your head around: to seize the first acceptable idea and declare it just 'x'. To admit something is too complex for you to immediately summarise is far less comfortable than simply dismissing it on the spot.

Whatever it is, GTA 6 isn't just anything. For one, every thing it is also seems to have an opposite. It's vast - incomprehensibly vast - but also overloaded with miniscule detail. It's about big ideas, themes, places - America, society, love - and also about individuals. It's ridiculous and puerile, and also sometimes mature. Tender and violent. Deep and shallow. Novel and, in places, surprisingly familiar. It's both a big adventure playground of video game activities and a focused story of suffocating intensity. Whatever foible might arise, the game presents you with a ready-made answer - often quite literally: you can turn off notifications about your relationship status in the menu if you just want to ignore that part. You can switch to the old controls (GTA 6 has seen a bit of a control overhaul), from the first screen you're shown. Or simply tear off in the opposite direction to whatever it is that dissatisfies you and do something else. If there's an idea to reduce this game to, then, the best I can manage is this: maybe Grand Theft Auto 6 is a game designed to be irreducible.

None of that is to say it's perfect. It's easy to stand in front of something like this and just bask in the awe of it, forgetting to stop a moment and step back. The awe is all part of the point, of course, and GTA 6 is nothing if not truly awesome, but there are also nits to pick. But then you step back again and they tend to fade away. GTA 6 will not be for everyone. But no game has made a better attempt at being everything.

GTA 6's story begins with Jason and Lucia. Discussing it beyond that is, frankly, almost impossible to do with any kind of substance without also spoiling it. For all the precipitous leaks, Rockstar has kept its story's two most essential moments - its beginning and its end - entirely secret, and without those two moments we're only really left with euphemisms.

Jason and Lucia are on the run, after a seemingly simple fetch quest goes awry. Who they're running from, or why they're being chased quite so hard, remains agonisingly beyond their field of view. But what is clear, right from the off, is exactly why they're so determined to escape. And amongst all this Rockstar has achieved something kind of momentous, when it comes to Grand Theft Auto: we've got real and very human stakes.

What else can be said? Well, let's go back to Rockstar's decision to begin so explicitly with Jason and Lucia. This is a story about a relationship. You can nurture or neglect it, but fundamentally that relationship - however strong - is at the heart of things.

The fact that Grand Theft Auto 6, of all games, is in many ways a game about love, and arguably the first blockbuster video game to be quite so explicitly a love story, is notable enough on its own. These are the games of rampages, wanted levels, hookers and hot coffee - the series which, in its most recent entry until now, featured effectively zero women of note or meaningful substance. Immediately there's a kind of tension forming as you play: I've sat down with this thing, fully prepared to steal a tank and squish beachloads of ultra-realistic civilians like playdough. But should I pick up some flowers first? The secret is that tension, like a lot of GTA 6's many opposites, is kind of the point.

Buying flowers, for instance, is one of the game's early, albeit optional missions. You begin GTA 6 proper playing solely as Jason for a short while, as he waits in his hazy, barefoot, margarita-soaked island hometown for Lucia to be released from prison. The two have been separated, their relationship effectively and conveniently reset - what kind of impression do you want to make when you finally see her after all this time? While the urge might be to test out those new wanted mechanics or - as recent memes would have it - race across the map to the first stripclub you can find, suddenly you have an altogether different to-do list.

Pick up some groceries. Get those flowers. Grab some clothes for Lucia to change into. Maybe finish those odd DIY jobs you've left half-started around the place so, y'know, your bedroom actually has a wall. Ever since GTA 4, and Roman Bellic's slightly desperate calls to go play darts and shoot some pool, Rockstar has leant further and further into the mundane. But here the studio has gone many steps further, past the Sopranos of GTA 5 and poker of Red Dead Redemption 2, full-tilt into slice-of-life. The result, mercifully, is anything but dull - all these situations, familiar as they may be, are really moments for irony, humour, character, just as they are in life itself. That, and a welcome chance to catch your breath amidst the bombast, which accelerates from these early missions exponentially.

That early, to-do list mission is like a lot of other moments in GTA, in that it's a case of Rockstar not-so-subtly dropping hints. This time, it's "we are going to play with your priorities". If there's a constant in GTA 6, you realise, it's that there often seems to be a nagging thought in the back of your mind as you progress. Should I be prioritising this right now? Is that optional thing worth the effort? Will this choice matter? How? It's such a prominent thought as you play, the actual act of devising what systems this game is using as you progress is as interesting in itself as how those systems end up working. Or to put it more simply: figuring this game out for yourself is really part of the fun.

Back to Jason and Lucia. The other thought I kept returning to is how profound a bond I started to form with them. I have to admit: I've tended not to care much about video game characters, frankly. Even in the obvious games, where voice actors have started to attract almost cult-like followings, I've never really got it. The closest any game has got to making me cry is in Football Manager 23, when I lost the Champions League final in the dying minutes to PSG. But with Jason and Lucia, suddenly I think I get it. I put much of this down to the performances - they are spellbinding - but really it's all interconnected, a factor of the extreme levels of development craft, from animation to cutscene direction, and of several relatively simple, but smartly executed systems layered on top (more on those later).

Lucia's performer - unveiled at last as Manni L. Perez - is astoundingly natural, charismatic, authentic in her delivery. The range of this character takes her from fierce to afraid, giddy and giggly to imperiously strong, or petal soft. And despite those extremes of emotion, and GTA characters' historic knack of scenery-chewing, she is never a caricature; between those two far ends lies the entire spectrum, too. Jason, prosaic and inarticulate - arguably even shy - feels destined to be underappreciated by comparison, but there's something to his performance also. Next to the sheer dazzle of Lucia, you need a straight-man to play off. And so where she might shine with sass, or protectiveness, or playful faux annoyance for a laugh, Jason might offer an eye roll, a sigh, or some other exasperation under his breath. He's also - at least with the relationship at its strongest - a surprisingly fine example of the strong, yet thoroughly non-toxic man; gentle and considerate, reassuring and attentive. Occasionally I have to catch myself, before I start believing GTA 6 - again, of all games - might genuinely be the game to offer the most persuasive model of a positive relationship to the kids who play it. (Again, aside from all the killing and stuff).

And those quieter aspects are a necessary foil - in fact, they're really a place where Rockstar exercises some rare subtlety. Some of GTA 6's funniest moments - between the big, obvious, admittedly still very funny gags for this childish mind (be sure to look out for all the game's many WAiNK locations!) - come from almost imperceptible glances and asides amongst the chaos. Together, both protagonists feel somewhat aware of the ludicrousness of the world around them, even more than they are simply a part of it.The two stars' relationship - and I chose to lean into this all the way, full 'wife guy' Jason and guard-down Lucia - is developed through minutiae. In a way, it comes just as a real relationship does. Little touches, side eyes, in jokes. After a mission together with some classic, larger-than-life GTA character or other, they'll occasionally have a little debrief, in the way a real couple might the morning after a particularly lurid double-date. They'll check-in, chit-chat, hang out, discuss hopes for the future, fears about what might happen, skepticism that they'll ever escape this constant whirlpool. And how you've behaved so far will dictate how the other might respond, with reassurance and commitment or something else.

Small gestures will add up, and both parties have to contribute. You can hold hands whenever you're together, open car doors for each other, send check-in texts or invitations to hang out. But it gets even more menial than that. Sometimes it's just sitting on the sofa together, watching TV or talking about nothing in particular (on the topic of TV: GTA 6 has a number of fully fledged TV shows, from Weasel News to actual, fully realised cartoon animations with multiple episodes; it also has four fully playable video games, including a pastiche arthouse walking simulator and twin-stick extraction shooter). Other times, sure, it's a particular kind of passion ignited by the unique thrill of a violent, climactic mission. This couple can be a burning cocktail, if you want them to be, born of extreme external danger and the safety of each other's embrace. And in their most tender moments, handled with genuine attentiveness and sincerity, it's intoxicating.

None of this, crucially, feels like a chore. Instead it feels almost irresistible, the easiest and most natural role play, created in part by Rockstar smartly prompting you with certain icons in moments, incentivising you with the tease of branching narrative - the idea in the back of your mind that there's some kind of outcome, somewhere in here, if things go a certain way. In others, they leave it to you: if I hadn't approached Lucia in the kitchen one early-game morning and pressed L2 to see what interactions came up, I wouldn't have noticed I had the option to offer her a lift to work, another little moment to bond.

All together, it's a picture of what many great relationships - maybe minus the rampages - become as a whole. A series of grand memories made together, sure - across rooftop bars, helicopter rides, and sunsets at the beach, maybe a couple of bank robberies and killing sprees - but also hours upon hours of touches, jokes, hugs, nicknames, shared friends, cups of coffee, dancing, laughter, or long, quiet journeys in the car. It is without doubt the most believable, captivating relationship I've ever played.

I could talk forever about this pair, not just in their relationship but as characters, extensions of GTA 6's thematic world. At various moments in my time with the game, for instance, I found myself wondering about Lucia's experience of the world, and how Rockstar uses its famously detestable NPCs - individually written and constructed in the thousands, another quietly stunning factor in this game - to occasionally play the role of, for instance, leering older men. How villains love to taunt her as "mamacita", while Jason affectionately nicknames her "mami". Or through a bizarrely affecting, bittersweet friendship she develops with a gentle(-ish) giant - and also another, not-so-gentle giant for that matter, in a mission that takes a turn. And in the casual racism she simply rolls with, just an everyday annoyance. And Jason too, in turn, in how he's sexualised and objectified in his own way, reduced in various moments to a weapon, a meathead, a walking gun, the staff. How the two of them wrestle with their position of desperation, poverty and precariousness in the world of Leonida, a microcosm of Rockstar's take on the United States.

I should probably start talking about that world. Where to begin? Let's start with the obvious stuff: GTA 6's map is absolutely massive. I haven't tested this directly, but the leaked maps are more or less accurate, and the comparisons in terms of size - multiple GTA 5's within GTA 6's map of Leonida - are about right. After dozens and dozens of hours with it there are still little areas, islands, and archipelagos I haven't explored, and many more spots so rarely visited they still feel entirely new when I drive through them again.

At one point, amongst the forest of the national park up in Leonida's northern third - trundling down one winding dirt track among many, trees in all directions - I actually found myself slightly lost. At least for a moment, anyway, until I plonked down a map marker and the GPS sorted me out. GTA 6 asks more of you than any previous GTA game, but it's also a retraction from the intentional clunk of Red Dead Redemption 2. You'll never be truly stuck or stumped; there's no sense of toil.

A quick example: the Scooter Bros (remember those?!) return here in a delightful reference, now acting as an official in-game phone app that lets you summon an owned vehicle for a fee, or up to three times a day if you unlock the app's "unlimited" version for a one-off cost. They can also repair your car or recover it if, like me, you have a habit of landing it on its roof. You can also fix it up at the returning Pay 'n' Spray's and all-new gas stations - which, thankfully, you really don't need to visit for refuelling all that often. (When you do, it's often worth the time: this is a great window for an emergent event to pop up - more on those, too, later.)

Soon after that little diversion in the woods, up on the scraggy peak I'd originally set out to reach, a wave of fog rolled in, bringing visibility eerily close amongst colonial-era ruins, crumbling log cabins, and the rusted-out tracks of disused, Old West mine carts. Hikers strolled by with a nod. Then, a firewatch tower I'd sought to climb for a proper view offered me the start of one of several mysteries GTA 6 has laid out here for players, continuing the series' long-running tradition. Later that evening, after finding and raiding an encampment of drug runners, being chased, found, and chased again by police, nosing around a nudist colony, then an eerily romantic lover's lookout (plus another bizarre encounter I won't spoil), I drove my couple home, to the city in the south, country radio giving way to electronica, and thunderclouds rolled in, soaking the hills and fields and miles upon miles of tarmac as much with atmosphere as with the rain itself.

Time and time again you'll find yourself in moments like this, not really doing anything or going anywhere - or suddenly abandoning plans for something stranger, more curious. But crucially still passing through the world, typically from the seat of a car. Right near the beginning of the game, in the first miniature moment of free roam with Jason - a short car journey home from wherever it was I was doing some fresh-from-prologue activity - I hit a perfect example of this. Rockstar clearly knows the value of the first drive: night, rain on the asphalt, Vice City FM on the radio, red tail lights flaring the lens. That's one I've never grown tired of, and probably never will: the tail lights. A kind of universal symbol of America's bigness, your tiny position in the queue of thousands, amongst this sea of snaking red stars against the darkness. All this together an absolutely superlative vibe.

I expect everyone will find their own little fascination like this. All of these things come as a result of Rockstar's ludicrous budget and infinite runway, but also an obvious determination to use that almighty privilege to its fullest. This studio has always been emphatically, maybe fanatically focused on detail, and now it's had the greatest opportunity to exploit that obsession that might ever be afforded to a developer. And so detail is everywhere; from the way Jason's t-shirt creases as he walks or rumples in the wind, to the noise and gust kicked up by helicopters; hair that flows impossibly like water, water that actually looks like water, sweat on skin, and sweat on bottles, and dusted condensation on cars. One of the very first things I wrote down in my notes on this game, as Lucia paced the hallway of her prison, was about a patch of mismatched paint on the wall.

This is not a patch of paint designed to be noticed. It's in the foreground for a fraction of a second as Lucia heads into another room, but it stuck for precisely that reason. This is the product of someone looking intensely at the environment as they attempt to recreate it and realising how important imperfections are to simulating reality. Clearly someone's scuffed, scratched, or graffitied something outside of this office, and the penitentiary system has felt the need to paint over it in some vain declaration of order. And then tight budgets, like a crumbling English council once again repainting a playground fence, have dictated the colour of paint that'll be used - likely whatever was to hand - to cover up these little marks of rebellion.

I looked at this and wrote it down. And then a few hours later, as I was whizzing around this world evading cop cars, hopping out to make a mad dash for cover, I realised how pointless that was. I'd already been darting by piles and piles of this stuff. Back alleys filled with rubbish - glorious rubbish, the most decadent scrap metal, buckets of mulch, and sacks of rotting food waste you've ever seen - and tables covered in piles of tat. Back offices stacked with papers, posters, family photos, files, mugs, notices, trophies, pens, and all kinds of amalgamated bric-a-brac that forms a picture of a real office in the mind.

The point is: at first, as Rockstar gently guides you from place to place, gradually layering the systems onto one another, easing you into this ocean of water by toe, ankle, knee, waist, you think it's doing that thing where video games front load fidelity to set the tone, maybe flex a little, before easing up after hitting you with some big and glorious view. Then you realise this is just what this game is like. It's everywhere. A huge amount of this map is, in the nicest possible way, kind of pointless. It's things - a disused motel, a lumber mill - which have no mission or character attached, and are instead there predominantly because in a real world they would be there, and therefore, to Rockstar, they must be there.

In some moments, if you're like me, it might genuinely create a kind of sadness: the sheer, incomprehensible amount of things that have evidently been slaved over by literally thousands of humans that will almost certainly be missed. Things you'll fly by in an instant as you whip the camera round the room in search of valuables or escape routes, never to return, worker hours piling up like grains of sand on Vice City's heavenly beach. It's so easy to focus on breadth and scale when talking about GTA 6's world. The volume. The "content". But what matters more I think is this miniscule detail. There's a point where detail, in a simulated world, tips over into effective infinity, where your brain just learns to let it glide on by. A point I never thought an open world game could ever truly reach - and yet here we are. In turn it creates almost a kind of obligation to linger - linger, that is, in a game built to be experienced from behind the wheel of a supercar. Another point of brilliant tension.

Speaking of, let's get into some mechanics, where all these tension points are at their strongest. GTA 6 works like this: Jason and Lucia's story is spread over a prologue and then several chapters. The world is open more or less from the off, not dissimilar to GTA 5, and yet the game, to borrow a term from Rockstar, is "stratified" in how it gradually rolls out its systems and greatest rewards. In other words, there's a little bit of progression to get through before what feels like the true GTA 6 reveals itself.

And the real drive here, insofar as there is one, is earning money. GTA 6 is easily the purest expression of life as an actual thief that Rockstar has attempted to make. Theft is at the heart and all along the spine of Jason and Lucia's story. From the setup of the game's prologue to the inciting incident its entire, lengthy first chapter builds to, and then well beyond it, all the way through to the finale. Over time, for instance, you'll have a perpetual optional objective: to top up your joint savings account to higher and higher targets, essentially building a flight fund or future life, whatever it may ultimately be. Likewise at various points you'll need to spend a certain amount of money with someone or on something to progress. And throughout, money is ultimately the gatekeeper to the game. Money is the "bridges" of previous GTAs, if you like: in the very early game you can, as far as I could tell, go more or less anywhere. But to actually do anything worthwhile and engage with Leonida's many, many activities, you'll need to both progress the story and get a bank account, the latter of which our dear off-the-grid, dishonourably-discharged minor criminal Jason has so far opted against.

Over and over then, you'll find yourself scraping together cash, and gradually GTA 6 offers newer, more rewarding and more complex means of doing so. At first it might be via minor drug deals, collected and delivered via your boss' bi-plane, or discovering a small-time criminal operation to rob out the back of a local liquor store. Then it might be stealing some low-tier cars to flip to a new-found fence, or busting bigger drug operations and gang hideouts. These are a rare example of Rockstar seemingly taking outside inspiration - criminal operations, marked on your map in yellow once discovered, are essentially Ubisoft-like outposts. At first I wondered if that might be a drawback, but then separate the idea and execution itself from the stigma and I don't think so. They're dense scenarios that essentially offer a more combat-focused route to money, and a texture-forward slice of the game's criminal world. In the simplest terms they're also quite fun, and a nice break from all the other forms of theft.

Theft continues beyond that still. You soon learn how to stick up minor stores and restaurants, and rob people at gunpoint on the street. Then it's pawn shops, jewellery stores, payday loan businesses. Then before you know it you're ripping ATMs out of walls with a tow truck, hurtling down the highway with this big metal cube still attached like a bouncing, sentient steel piñata on the run, until it cracks open for you to feast on its delicious insides. Then you're yoinking supercars or - and oh, this is the good stuff - robbing full-fledged banks at will.

This is where a lot of the various systems teased in previews - all of which sounded, on the surface, kind of awkward to me - weave into what the game is trying to do. There are several here, all overlapping in their own way and typically evolving - but not outright replicating - ideas first brought in via Read Dead Redemption 2. The combined impact is a sense of constant decision-making, tension once again between the immediacy of steaming in somewhere and tearing away from the cops, and the satisfaction of a slightly more restrained plan.

To stick with money, it operates in a kind of tiered system. At the bottom you have cash, which has a $20,000 limit per character and which can be lost in its entirety, particularly when you're wasted or busted. Once you've unlocked your BuckMe (yep) account - a kind of PayPal parody - cash can be deposited at ATMs into a much safer kind of basic shared current account. Even that is at risk of charges of some kind when you're killed or arrested - I've yet to reverse engineer what dictates these, but they're at worse in the low thousands, typically less. Then above that is your savings account, again shared across Jason and Lucia, where your money is at its safest but also, crucially, irretrievable once deposited.

The physical act of collecting money is now an action, too, as opposed to something you can simply run over and vacuum up. You need to manually collect it from characters' bodies, from the counter after being placed there by a panicked cashier, from inside safes you crack, and so on. Sometimes it might be in a locked container - such as when you bust open an ATM - which you'll need to shoot first to access. Sometimes it's stored behind a bank's vault door, which only opens in a very specific circumstance, meaning a narrow, heist-like window needs to be exploited in order for it to be reached. And when you're stealing anything other than clean cash - namely jewellery, marked bills, and drugs - you'll store it in a stash bag.

That feeds into two other systems here, which both operate around a sense of ownership, in vehicles and weapons. Stash bags contain all your loot until they're filled, and then they need to be sold to a fence - with different fences offering more or less for certain goods, or not taking some at all - before you can actually access the profit. You can drop these bags when you're hit by a car or knocked off a bike (or, ahem, fumbling with the controls for a second), and likewise hand them off to your partner to carry or even independently sell for you. You can also store them in your car's trunk, where they're slightly safer, but also then take slightly longer to access if, say, you're switching vehicles mid four-star cop chase along the freeway.

And cars and weapons also follow a similar system in turn: stolen cars are now only temporarily yours; safe when stored in a parking spot but at risk when out in the world, until you pay to register them with a fence (or, later in the game, at Pay 'n' Sprays). But owned cars are yours forever, even when destroyed, as long as you have enough garage space to store them - garage space that, you realise, costs more and more money to procure. The same goes for weapons: found weapons are essentially borrowed, and lost when you're killed or arrested. But owned weapons - ones you buy, or unique, custom ones you discover - are yours forever, recoverable even if lost or left behind.

This then, in turn, ties into the new police and 'wanted' system. Now it's much more realistic: police can spot or receive reports of you and take note of several factors, from facial appearance to clothing, whether you're with your partner, what weapon you have on you and what vehicle you're driving. And when it comes to things like robbing stores and banks, there's a system for whether you're spotted on locally stored CCTV, which can be destroyed, or fancier cloud-based footage which can't.

All kinds of things then play into this, from whether you walk into a place with a "longarm" gun - automatically putting guards on high alert - or a concealed pistol, to whether you're committing a crime somewhere fancy or rough. I got a strong sense that my crimes were being far less attentively dealt with in Vice City's poorer neighbourhoods. There are also two rings of attention drawn on the map when you're wanted, dictating how intensely a police search for you is concentrated, and different kinds of star ratings too, via a system called heat. These indicate whether police can see you, have a description of you, or are simply looking for a criminal for the crime you performed in that area. Even after you successfully escape, heat - dictated by whatever police might know - remains over the entire region of your crime, the size of that area and rate of its deterioration depending on just what exactly that crime was.

Finally, the criminal profile system is another tangent on its own, but once again it all folds together. It's a tiered honour system ranking you from "Professional" at one end to "Irredeemable" at the other, that last one the only point from which your reputation can never be recovered. Incapacitations instead of kills - which crucially are satisfying and easy to achieve with a bit of concentration - as well as completing objectives more subtly or achieving cleaner getaways from crimes, will tilt you towards Pro status. Driving that tank over those civilians or, heaven forbid, kicking a dog takes you the other way. If this sounds burdensome, again I'd say you really shouldn't worry: I naturally found myself with both Jason and Lucia as Professionals at the end, mostly thanks to shooting lots of cops in the groin. Your necessary actions during missions or when under attack don't impact it in the same way, I gather. It feels closer to a case of illustrating the role play on offer here, and ultimately committing to the consequences of that, than it does any kind of struggle to manage.

All in all, the effect with the overall game is similar: less that GTA 6 becomes hard work - as I'd feared myself, and as Rockstar intentionally made Red Dead Redemption 2, to its thematic benefit in many ways - and more that, now, a greater amount of what you're doing is gamified. A robbery is in itself a game: whether you go in loud or quiet, together or separate, or focus on cracking the safe or looting the registers. As is how you divide up the bags between you, or whether your getaway is done together or separately to split cops' attention; whether you use your own car or a stolen vehicle, and if you're smart enough to park another one somewhere out of sight nearby for a sneaky changeover. You can also pack changes of clothes in your car to help disguise your identity.

And all that's introduced, ingeniously, via the main missions of Jason and Lucia's story. If you were ever in doubt that GTA might have lost its edge, amongst all these soft and cuddly themes and grand ideas - that it might be sacrificed at the altar of realism and narrative heft - you can put that worry firmly aside. The low-brow humour, parody, larger-than-life characters, out of body experiences, 90s film references or cops-n-robbers bombast are all here, and all about as good as they've ever been.

In fact, really, in a lot of cases they're quite a bit better. The highlights are hard to pick out without spoiling again - many of the best arise from moments where you wonder if the game will go there, before it inevitably does. Others involve movie references that feel more overt than ever - Lucia openly quotes John McClane at one point, lighter in hand - but while some of this can feel a tad predictable, the sheer spectacle is often spellbinding.

The best moments, aptly, come in car chases, where tyres are eviscerated by gunfire and speeding 4x4s suddenly dip, flip, and crash into one another with a sensational boom. Clearly some magic has been worked under the hood here to ensure that when they crash out, they do so with a kind of cinematic, organic-looking stuntman style, swerving into other vehicles in the chasing pack, across lanes into trucks and lorries, over highway partitions, wrapping around trees or catapulting off bridges. The new focus system - bullet time, on a specific, buffable meter - highlights weak spots such as engines and tyres for you to aim at for generally non-lethal take-outs, helping you with yet another system I'll get to in a moment. But really that system is kind of secondary here: I started shooting at these spots because, more often than not, it rewarded me with something awesome.

Main missions and the bigger character-based, optional side missions work similarly to how they've done before in GTA games. A location is marked on a map and you head there, or call someone, or wait for a call, and once you arrive things kick off, elements of the open world closing somewhat as self-contained scenarios within the sandbox open up. There are few low points, as the first act glides through a wistfully carefree, low-key criminal life before the game shifts to the snowballing momentum of its crooks-on-the-run melodrama. There are multiple heists, which are naturally high watermarks - big moments of catharsis and chaos that propel the couple forward across some new Rubicon after hours and hours of sub-mission preparations. There are episodes with every vehicle, every ludicrous modern American scenario, every body part you could imagine. You'll be dragged through all kinds of Floridian subcultures, naturally, from sleepy key life and everglades hicks to rusted-over rural towns, biker gangs, strip clubs, record labels, towerblock drug kingpins and, erm, bodybuilders. And then plenty beyond that.

Rockstar is as ever ruthless with the skewer. The targets remain broad, from Miami's new wave of non-indigenous crypto bros to wellness Bali girlies aiming to be their very own #SheEO. But there's also a maturity here, believe it or not, even if it's slightly tucked away between the dangling wangs and murderous– actually I won't spoil that one. I've found no cases of Rockstar punching down - although rest assured no progressive, gentrifying hipster goes unscathed - and there is a subtle, but absolutely present awareness of the world in which Rockstar is now operating. In one moment the concept of using dirt on a senator is discussed, at which point Jason wisely remarks that dirt must be pretty spectacular, given what they've already gotten away with these days.

The changes here are small, ultimately, and while there's a whole essay of its own to be written about the concept of parody, my sense is Rockstar has played this just right. GTA 6 has essentially taken the 'mirror' approach, in simply concentrating the extremes of the current world - so often deemed impervious to satire - by putting them all together in a single place, then reflecting them back at the world they came from. Florida is often described as a kind of heightened America - America Plus, the country's Ultimate Edition - and Leonida is just a heightened Florida. With an awful lot of very silly puns.

Away from the main missions and primary sidequests, Rockstar's made another seemingly subtle, but kind of revolutionary tweak. Aside from more static activities - the races, sports, pastimes, and the rest - the other source of missions in GTA 6 is what the game calls emergent events. These are entirely missable - though I think they re-present themselves eventually - and occur essentially as overheard goings-on just out of shot. They could be anything from one-off opportunities or minor punchlines to entire quest chains and conspiracies.

For instance: driving around Vice City you might find a guy doing his best Florida Man impression by livestreaming himself to his Snapmatic following while standing on the highway overpass wall above a high drop. Then, inevitably, he slips and you might be able to pull him up or might - as I may or may not have done - fumble the controls and have him plummet to his death. You might watch a bunch of amateurs rob a payday loans store and get blasted by the marked bills' exploding dye. Or a group of masked, heavily-armed professionals rob a cash truck or minor bank. A weirdo might flag you down and trigger a whole load of nonsense happening next, or the cops might be arresting someone and leave a supercar and bag of coke happily unattended while they try to run off. Even beyond that, there's also just weird stuff lying around, without anything specific tied to it. I once spent a worrying amount of time in conversation with a pioneering member of the Hollow Earth Society. Then got a good laugh when I next paid him a visit.

It's a cracking system, in part because it reinforces a sense of continuous, organic weirdness around every corner, and in part because each of these feels like a kind of invitation. Once you do a couple and find yourself blackmailing a guy who in turn blackmailed GTA's in-game Amazon equivalent for a cut of his lawsuit winnings, or spraying manure on a spurned farmer's ex-lover's house, or running over some numpty protesters trying to set an actual bear loose on the freeway, it's hard not to stop at every distant argument or out-of-pocket comment that crosses your airwaves. Rest assured, if you think I've spoiled anything here: there are scores of these events, many of which I've yet to encounter or investigate, and many of which are far weirder and more curious than these.

All this feeds back into a sense of choice, which is a challenge to accurately describe, thanks to both how difficult it is to figure out when and how one might have mattered, and when a moment of the story might have diverged. But choices, combined with the strength of your relationship and your criminal profile, and other factors I'll intentionally decline to mention here, all have an impact. One particularly great achievement of Rockstar's, in fact, is in making you wrestle with those choices, within missions and without, in this state of blindness to their outcomes. Even whether they're significant at all is often unknown - in many cases the variables, permutations and forks are still unknown to me now, after all this time - and yet also, once again without spoiling any moment at any point, the studio has still made whatever outcomes you might encounter feel as resonant as any I've experienced. Forgive me for dodging any examples and insisting you take my word for it.

Away from missions themselves, of which there are of course many, and there are so many more things to do beyond them. You can dive for treasure across dozens of underwater sites. You can bird watch, animal watch, fish, and hunt for game. You can customise guns and cars to extreme ends - please do the street racing side missions for a, ahem, lovely example of custom wrapping - and customise your characters just as much. The fitness system is great, with a surprising number of superbly animated gym and bodyweight exercises and, naturally, walking BBLs who hit on Jason as he demonstrates perfect form.

It's also uninvasive, I found, thanks to clearly marked, and relatively long, windows around maintaining your current status, but there's still a level of detail here. Your body composition is dictated by both cardio and strength training, but also what you eat and how often. You can ignore it and just look like an average person, or pay attention for meaningful buffs to health, focus, stamina, and appearance - and naturally the world around you reacts to that in comments from passers-by and, I suspect, small details of certain missions changing too.

There's more! There's racing - at racetracks and mud tracks; in street races and off road courses; with bikes, cars, drones, and the rest. There are stunt jumps, naturally, and basejumps too. There are places to meditate. There's a system around not just fishing but using the right bait, multiple series of collectible trading cards to hoard or sell, various collectible books and magazines, meteorites, golden doubloons, and 'key valuables' found at gang hideouts including one-off guns, cars, and jewellery like fancy watches. There are clubs, bars, strippers, love hotels, diners, coffee houses, stores of clothing and shoes, hairdressers, and beyond. You can play an alarmingly realistic version of pool. Or listen to the radio of course - there are roughly double the stations you've seen revealed so far - or numerous talk shows and podcasts, including entire true crime dramas and series. You can ride jetskis, pose in rented supercars, order cocktails at swanky pools and go on dates to rooftop restaurants or crusty marina dive bars. There are DJs and live performances, including a cover band with the most accurately depicted, finger-specific fretting of a guitar solo I've ever seen in a game.

The overall effect - yet another overall effect! I'm aware I keep doing this but I just have simply no other way of summarising it all - is of a genuine open-world playground, but crucially rather than a somewhat directionless soup of content, there is thematic sense to all of it. One of GTA 6's many messages, as foreshadowed on one of its most potent trailers, is a sense of embracing the moment while it lasts - of presentness and gratitude for things that might be accessible even without the big financial grind. And, surely, of what a joy it is to experience them with someone else.

What you realise is you're pairing all this stuff to do - ultimately, variations on simply hanging out - with the fact that so much of Jason and Lucia's relationship is delivered in quiet, hang-out moments. And as so many of their other relationships - with parents, adopted parents, friends - come in the same way, this picture starts to form. From an apartment sofa to the Vice Beach waves, GTA 6 is ultimately just a place to be.

Three control schemes of default, classic, and left-handed with checkable inputs. Customisable controller sensitivity in various forms, adaptive trigger settings (PS5), option to lock-on aiming, aim assist strength, toggles for forms of aim assistance. Five levels of "tap assists" for QTEs, 11 settings for a hold delay to avoid accidental button presses, and hold assist for actions requiring button holds. Toggles for most button holds to switch to single presses. Simplified minigames toggle. Auto drive settings, accessible combat toggle for reducing enemy health and damage taken, and reduced vehicle damage toggle. Sliders for visual effects, focus trail colourblind mode, and brightness reduction. High contrast shaders for interactable elements. Text size, colour, and other UI customisation. Subtitles, closed captions, and size settings. Minimap and compass customisability, and in-world UI marker options. Various reticle options, help text and reminders. Reviewable mission objectives and recent dialogue text. Many camera customisability options. Audio options such as tinnitus reduction and separate volume sliders, screen narration and audio descriptions.

In fact, in many ways it's a kind of evolution of the hangout game itself. The gentle, wistful, nostalgic guitar strums from Justice's background score as you idle on the map or menu. The dedication, through all those years of what I imagine to be Rockstar's incessant tinkering with sounds and performances and tiny visual touches, to an authentic sense of place. I could melt into this world. I think about the amount of people about to play this game, and the way it invites you to submerge yourself in it like a hot bath, and wonder if the axis of the entire hangout genre - maybe the biggest paradigm shift in video games of the last two decades - might have suddenly been shifted again. The comfort of continuously mic'd up pal chatter replaced here with a kind of sublime isolation.

There are, as I mentioned, a few very little flaws I could pick at. Some of the missions for Dre-Quan, a record label-owning friend of Jason involving a newly invented rap group for the game, were fun in their own right, extraordinarily well produced and an essential part of the whole, but also just a little throwaway in a sense. There seemed to be little tie-in from those to the progress of the actual story, leaving them feeling a little more like lavish side missions than a part of the main event, contributing to a little mid-game narrative sag. The controls, while a marked improvement from Red Dead Redemption 2 - I fear I'll never make it to heaven after the amount of times I accidentally punched that horse - could still be tighter. Shooting is less wafty than it has been for some time, forgoing auto-aim for aim-assist (though settings can be heavily tinkered or restored to those of GTA 5), and GTA is now a proper third-person cover shooter in its action moments, with better snapping to cover and dashing between it, which suits it perfectly. But there were still moments where the controls wobbled. Walking three steps along a narrow beam to open a small container without falling off felt like the hardest thing the game asked me to do in its entire runtime.

What else? Not much, frankly. On the technical side, I had one hard crash in my entire time with the game, which did cost me about 25 minutes of a mission, and one moment where an NPC got stuck trying to enter my car and I had to reload to a checkpoint made seconds earlier.

I also wish there were more save slots - GTA 6 does away with mission replayability, which might prove controversial. As you play it you'll realise why that makes sense, but I also longed for more room than the 15 given to load back in at various moments and replay something specific - often for no other reason than that moment being an utter banger. Performance-wise, yes it plays at 30fps, even on a PS5 Pro, but Rockstar's frame pacing - I have to assume it's this; I'm no tech expert here - is so good that it feels resoundingly smoother than a typical game that runs at the same rate. Put aside the arbitrary number and ask me purely how it feels, and I'd tell you it feels smooth. How that fares on other consoles, particularly the Xbox Series S, will be worth considering.

A little more philosophically, it's also not a perfect narrative, arguably because it never really could be. The middle of the game is phenomenal in its mission variety and excitement, though perhaps where Jason and Lucia's characters are pushed slightly to the background, when I wish their relationship - or lack of it - had even more time in the narrative spotlight. For all its dramatic heft, GTA 6 also can't purely be a drama, and so there is inevitably some tonal whiplash between an intimate love scene, say, and a quest involving chasing a man with a fake English accent wearing nothing but a comedy man thong around the poshest part of town. The point, instead, comes back to how all those strange and implausible bedfellows combine.

And this is the magic. Every modern Grand Theft Auto is considered now to be a kind of product of its time. Nico Bellic's story is labelled a tale of the immigrant experience of the American Dream, amongst the post-9/11 world of New York and the war on terror. I always thought of GTA 5 as something prodding at a crisis of masculinity, after the financial crisis, and a kind of early 2010s complacency and drift. These aren't the only things they're about, obviously. GTA 5 is also The Sopranos and an ode to LA and the split of the id, ego, and superego, and of course a broad, hours-long, occasionally mis-targeted piss-take. But by virtue of being games depicting the world as it was in some way, they also become a reflection of it.

What is Grand Theft Auto 6, then? I know I said it's irreducible, but I can't help it. For now, after the car, I'm coming back to money. How Jason and Lucia keep attempting the famous bootstrapping of American meritocracy, keep picking themselves back up from zero, keep scrabbling and clawing their way out of the vortex, and how the so-called land of opportunity keeps telling them it's possible to climb up and out the top of it, away from worry and pain, up to the balcony and the penthouse and the helipad on the roof, all the while with its other hand constantly pushing them back down. At every tier of life savings, after every big score and narrow escape, the goal posts move back, and the enemy reorganises itself again. It might be trite, but it feels essential to point out nonetheless, that GTA 6 is a product of capitalism at its most extreme: the most extraordinary amount of wealth ever directed at an entertainment product and, surely, the one to achieve the most extraordinary number of sales. It is about the most capitalistic place on earth, and the place within it that features perhaps the most extreme disparity of wealth, as its hypercars scream past the homeless.

GTA 6 is a reflection of the outer world and a part of it. And at its heart, its fragile and desperate humans play a rigged game, while attempting to walk the narrow line between exhausted desperation and the empowering wish-fulfilment of crime. You might wonder, as Jason and Lucia often do, whether they're always going to be doomed. And then later, whether that matters, when the beauty of it all is in the struggle.

Grand Theft Auto VI isn’t just the biggest GTA game to date; it’s also the most romantic. Though the series is well known for its sordid assortment of criminal leads, GTA VI switches things up by starring a pair of thieves who also happen to fall in love after meeting while stealing a car. It’s still an open-world action game full of chaos and mayhem. But many players will also be attempting to nurture the budding relationship in between heists. The sprawling game is designed to support both audiences.

“It’s a risk,” explains Rob Nelson, producer on GTA VI and co-studio head at developer Rockstar North. “It’s a real change for Grand Theft Auto. You can still play it the old way, you can reject all of this stuff. But for people who do engage, it’s meaningful — you’re earning it, you’re working at it, just like in a normal relationship.”

Despite the couple — named Jason and Lucia — being so central to the GTA VI experience, the development of the game didn’t start with characters or story. It began with a place. Nelson says that “pretty quickly” the team settled on placing the game in Leonida, the GTA universe’s version of Florida and the setting of one of the series’ most iconic games, Vice City on the PS2. In 2015, a small group of developers from Rockstar headed to Florida to get a sense of the place and set the groundwork for what would go on to become GTA VI.

“We just went around to feel it out,” explains Nelson. “Go to the Everglades, go to the Keys, go to the city, as many places as we can, and start thinking about what aspects of the place we want to incorporate. And then the map team starts building our version of that and talking very loosely and very broadly about what types of people we want to include and what types of places.”

The team realized they wanted to tell a story that would pull players through the various locations they were creating. And the best way to do that was to have characters who were relatively small-time criminals. They would start near the bottom of the criminal underworld and work their way up, traversing Leonida’s various locations, culminating in the splashy Vice City.

At the same time, the developers also wanted to do something different with the character-switching mechanic from GTA V, which let players swap between three unique criminals, each with very different stories and motivations. For GTA VI, a couple working together to steal enough money to escape the outlaw life fit both of these ideas perfectly. But it also presented some new challenges.

“Could we make a relationship work?” Nelson remembers thinking. “It’d be very different from three characters living separate lives that come together for narrative purposes and then split. These two are going to need to be together all the time and that’s going to be a challenge. It felt really wrong to have these people that are supposedly together only be together when we’re giving you scripted content.”

While GTA V is the obvious reference point, the new GTA also builds off of Rockstar’s 2018 Western Red Dead Redemption 2. That game was notable in particular for giving players more ways to interact with the world: You could talk to any NPC, sit around a campfire while drinking coffee or playing cards, or grow out your beard for a new look.

GTA VI expands this in ways that are designed to reinforce the relationship aspect of the experience. For instance, not only can you drink coffee, but you can also make a cup and hand it to your partner. The two can spend time together sitting on the couch watching TV, or go out to a club and dance. There’s even a button dedicated exclusively to making Jason and Lucia hold hands when they’re together.

“If it’s additive, and it makes you laugh, we include it.”

Nelson notes that when the team implemented the hand-holding feature, it was a revelatory moment for showing how the story and gameplay could connect. “When we got that mechanic in — and it’s not just a toggle, it’s an active thing that you have to commit to and hold — it was a really profound thing,” he says.

But it’s also a careful balancing act. While RDR2 was a widely praised game, it also received criticism for its slow pace, which was due in large part to all of the small mechanical details. These added animations and button prompts that gave texture to the world but could also lead to a plodding pace. That would be a much bigger problem in a game like GTA, which is built around action. Nelson says that he can be “freakishly obsessive” about these kinds of details, but he and the team had to learn to let go when realism got in the way of the game itself.

“Maybe you don’t have to carry around antifreeze in your trunk so that if your engine starts smoking you can top it up,” he says. “Certain things fall by the wayside in developing the game, where it’s like, we don’t need this. But other things, like eating food, it was important for us. You want to feel like you are this person existing in this world to a degree. That felt like it was additive. I love walking down the street with a beer and a cigarette and switching between the two, feeling like a dude. If it’s additive, and it makes you laugh, we include it.”

This also led the team down some unexpected paths. For instance, GTA VI is the first game in the series with functioning bedsheets. In past titles, and in basically every other video game, characters simply lay on top of a bed to go to sleep. That’s because it’s a lot of effort to both animate a character going under the covers as well as rig the sheets so that characters can interact with them in a way that looks and feels natural. The payoff isn’t worth it. But in the context of GTA VI, where the characters hide out together in everything from dingy motel rooms to fancy hotels, it made sense to actually build it.

“Because you’re sharing a living space, and you come in and see your partner sleeping at 3AM, it’s weird to see them laying on the top of the bed all the time,” Nelson says. “Nobody does that. It’s a lot of work to rig the covers on all of the different beds and have them both be able to get in and out of it and be able to be under the covers together.”

Bedsheets, holding hands, cups of coffee — they’re seemingly small details in a world as large as Grand Theft Auto VI, one that encourages players to run wild. But they’re also indicative of the approach Rockstar took with this latest GTA and how the studio attempted to make every element work in concert, even if it required a lot of extra work, like the bed covers. “That’s an example of something that was a lot of work, but it was worth it.”

Grand Theft Auto VI does something impossible — it lives up to the hype

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posts from this topic will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Posts from this topic will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Thirteen years later, Rockstar has crafted a worthy successor to GTA V and the new standard-bearer for open-world games.

Posts from this author will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

Posts from this author will be added to your daily email digest and your homepage feed.

How do you follow a game like Grand Theft Auto V? Thirteen years ago, Rockstar’s crime-fueled epic went on to redefine what an open-world game could be and became an enormous success, selling more than 230 million copies across three generations of game consoles. It sits just behind Tetris and Minecraft on the list of the best-selling games of all time, and its lucrative online mode has helped it remain popular for well over a decade.

That level of success has allowed Rockstar to take its time with a sequel, which has further increased the hype around the next GTA. A series of major leaks and delays only exacerbated this. At a time when much of the video game industry is struggling, the next edition of GTA stands alone, a cultural moment so big (almost) everybody has been forced to get out of the way. The buildup to Grand Theft Auto VI’s launch is unlike anything the game industry has ever seen.

Even more remarkable? The game was actually worth the wait.

At this point I’ve spent nearly 80 hours in Leonida, GTA VI’s fictional take on Florida. And what I’ve experienced is a game that combines the wide-open freedom offered in GTA V with the remarkable level of immersion from the studio’s 2018 release Red Dead Redemption 2, while also improving on both qualities. At the same time, it’s also the most mature GTA game Rockstar has ever made, merging the criminal antics and absurdist humor the series is known for with a love story that gives you new ways to interact with the world and new reasons for wanting to push forward. This is the GTA game the studio has been building toward for its entire existence.

Let’s start with that new premise. GTA V introduced the idea of multiple protagonists to the series, and VI takes that concept in a different direction. It’s a Bonnie and Clyde-inspired story about Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos, two small-time criminals who meet while, of course, stealing a car. It’s the most GTA-possible meet-cute. The idea is that you can play as both characters individually and also build their relationship over the course of the game. Things start out relatively slow. At the outset, Lucia is in prison, and you play solely as Jason, getting things ready for when she gets out. You can fix up your ramshackle house — that involves actually nailing down new stairs and rolling out paint on the walls — and also start building up a nest egg through means that are not exactly legal.

Jason and Lucia’s relationship grows naturally over the course of the game, but it’s also a slow buildup to becoming the inseparable pair they were by the end of my playthrough. GTA VI approaches most of the experience with confident and deliberate pacing. Even when you control them individually, the characters are often together, either just hanging out or partnering for a crime. And being able to play as both is one of the game’s standout features. But I didn’t get to that point in the game until almost 17 hours in. Before that, you play as both leads mostly solo, learning who they are as people, before you’re given free rein to switch between the two.

What’s great about the premise is how seamlessly it fits with what you’re actually doing in the game. There’s some bigger-picture stuff that I won’t spoil, but generally speaking, the main goal in GTA VI is to make a lot of money so that Jason and Lucia can get out of the criminal life for good and retire somewhere comfortably. And outside of the big story missions, you’re largely free to choose how you go about that. You can rob jewelry stores, steal cars, raid drug dens, or even search for hidden treasure at the bottom of the sea. Over the course of the game you’ll also meet different criminals who serve as fences, so you can sell them whatever you’ve got to build up your savings account. Sometimes they’ll even text you targets that are particularly lucrative.

The structure feels natural and gives you a lot of freedom in how you play.

The structure feels natural and gives you a lot of freedom in how you play. I’m not a big fan of armed robbery, for instance. There are just so many things that can go wrong, and I almost always end up killing some innocent bystander when I try to escape. But I love getaway chases, and so stealing cars became my main form of income. Most importantly, though, this structure gives you a lot of reasons to go out in the world and explore. The missions take you all over the map — which is actually entirely accessible from the very beginning, if you choose to play that way — including not only the bright lights of the Miami-like Vice City, but also tourist beaches, swamps, trailer parks, and a wild variety of other locations, each with its own landscape to explore and characters to meet.

To be clear: Despite being larger, more mature, and more lifelike, this is still very much GTA. This is a game about chaos and crime. This comes across in a few ways. First, there are those smaller missions where you never really know how things will play out. The cashier at the pawn shop you’re robbing might have a shotgun, or you might accidentally smash into a big rig during a high-speed getaway. The world itself is full of unpredictable moments that force you to think on your feet. Several times I stopped my car to talk to an NPC whose vehicle broke down on the side of the road. In one instance the driver just swore at me to leave her alone. In another, I got pulled into an entire storyline involving a delivery driver who needed help to keep her AI chatbot manager off her back. I delivered her packages and ended up dealing with everything from porch pirates to tigers.

There are also the multistep heists, which return from GTA V more intricate and complex. These are the big scores and involve several different missions: You spend time getting the right gear and gathering all of the information you need to actually pull them off. Even some of the sub-missions can be gigantic, including one where you have to go undercover for weeks of in-game time to get everything you need in order. But the payoff is incredible. The heists play out like over-the-top action set-pieces, as if they were pulled out of a Christopher Nolan movie like Tenet or Inception (but without all of the sci-fi twists). Even better, all of the missions in the game feel streamlined with a new checkpoint system that allows you to spend less time replaying sequences after failing, and if you fail too many times, you can just skip that mission altogether. There are still a few too many prolonged shootouts, but the checkpoints mean they’re much less tedious than in past GTA games.

All of this action is crammed into a world that isn’t just huge, but also feels more alive than ever before. Like in Red Dead Redemption 2, you can talk with any NPC, but here you have more options, letting you either greet them or, if you feel like causing trouble, taunt them. Most of the time you’ll get a simple “hey” or someone begging for a few dollars. Other times you’ll get pulled into a fistfight or an unexpected storyline. Once, on my way to buy new clothes, I accidentally bumped into some gang members who followed me inside the store, and I had no choice but to fight them. The owner of the shop called the cops, and so I figured if I was in this much trouble, I might as well empty the register. What started out as a quest to find a cool T-shirt turned into a high-speed escape from the cops.

Another important thing to note: While the game retains the series’ satirical tone in many ways, the humor doesn’t punch down like in past GTAs, at least from what I’ve played. Instead it makes fun of stuff that deserves to be made fun of: men’s rights activists, NFT bros, conspiracy theorists, Bryan Johnson.

But what really makes the world so special are all the small, meaningful details. Changing your clothes is a lot of fun, but it also helps keep the cops off your back when you’re wanted. Eating and drinking helps improve your physical abilities. And, this being GTA, you can use just about every kind of vehicle imaginable. That includes taking the bus, which runs on a predetermined route, making stops along the way. You can rent electric scooters, and just like in real life, they’re a menace clogging up the streets of Vice City. Your cars even need to be gassed up from time to time.

It’s a careful balancing act. These details make the world feel more nuanced and textured, but they’re also minor enough that they don’t feel tedious. This isn’t The Sims where you’re constantly monitoring your characters’ bodily functions. Usually, I only remembered to eat when the fitness app on my phone told me I needed more protein, and I didn’t once have a car break down because I forgot to fill up the tank. These smaller mechanisms are just enough to add a sense of realism without getting in the way of the action. You can also ignore a lot of them; at no point do you need to shower, but you might hear some snide remarks from your partner if you don’t.

Perhaps the best example of this is the in-game smartphone, which is integrated into the lives of both characters in a thoughtful, plausible way. Crime bosses might text you a location to meet up, for instance, and you can add it to your GPS and head over. Or Lucia might text Jason a link to a funny video, which you can then open in a social networking app, and you can even read the comments. Smartphones have become a common user interface in games, but in GTA VI it’s a part of the world; the game doesn’t pause when you pull out your phone, meaning that if you get a text while you’re driving, you can either pull over and read it or try to text and drive.

I also found these details, like the phone calls and texts between the couple, pulled me further into the story of Jason and Lucia and made me feel closer to both of them. When the two are together, you can press one of the shoulder buttons to make them hold hands. As long as you keep pressing it, they stay together. At first I used this strategically, imagining that if a couple holding hands was casing a jewelry store, it would be much less conspicuous. But eventually, as I grew to know the couple, it just became a thing I did without thinking about it. These two are crazy in love; of course they’re going to hold hands all the time. You can even do little things like make a coffee and choose to hand it to your partner instead of drinking it yourself, and all of these acts improve your relationship, which in turn impacts how the characters relate to each other and how some of the narrative elements play out.

You don’t have to do any of that. There are just so many ways to interact with the world that different players will find their own things to fixate on. For me, it was a lot of holding hands and drinking coffee every morning. Someone else might get really into fashion or hunting or customizing cars. I barely touched on any of those elements and still felt like I had a substantial experience.

Jason and Lucia are two very different people, each with their own kinds of stories to explore within the game. The big missions are cinematic heists that usually involve some combination of the two. But there are large, branching narratives that are only playable as one or the other character. Jason, for instance, has an old friend who is trying to start a record label in the back room of a strip club. As with the best GTA stories, the tale steadily escalates until you’re feuding with a famous rapper and facing down a number of life-or-death situations. Lucia, meanwhile, has to deal with her mom’s quest to become a real estate agent that gets her caught up with a violent gang, and her best friend pulling her into a pyramid scheme that seems very much like a cult but turns out to be something even more ridiculous. These storylines are so big and absorbing that I would often forget about the main campaign for long periods of time.

It’s not that there are zero issues with the game. The combat is still clunky, and the weapon wheel is still frustrating; there were multiple times when I accidentally equipped a flashlight instead of a shotgun when in the heat of battle. I also ran into a handful of small bugs, like dead bodies comically flopping around or a coffee mug that was stuck floating in a hotel room. But given how ambitious GTA VI is, and how badly things can go with large open games, the relative lack of technical issues during my playthrough is impressive. Even better: I played the game on a launch-version PS5, and it ran just fine.

Back in 2018, I called Red Dead Redemption 2 “the most convincing open-world game ever made,” and over the last eight years that has remained true. There have been great examples of the form since then — Elden Ring, Tears of the Kingdom, Control Resonant, Cyberpunk 2077 (eventually) — but none that approach the scale and detail of Rockstar’s works. Certainly none that attempted to do it in anything like the contemporary, real-world setting that has made GTA so pervasive. Now that mantle has been passed to GTA VI.

To give you a sense of how big this game is, despite spending more than 70 hours in the world and completing the main story, I’ve only managed to get through less than 60 percent of the total experience. And I just spent 2,500 words explaining how good GTA VI is without even touching on some of the best parts: the incredible selection of music, the hilarious talk radio stations, the pitch-perfect casting, the lifelike graphics. There are full-on TV shows, books, and video games that you can experience inside of the game. The most pretentious indie game of 2026? It exists in GTA VI.

For the last decade the idea of GTA VI has been inseparable from the hype around it. All of the noise caused by leaks, delays, and the protracted development time has turned the game into something larger than life, an experience hailed as the biggest game of all time before it even launched. It really seemed like no project could live up to the level of scrutiny that GTA VI has been and will be subjected to. But now we can forget all of that, because GTA VI is actually here. And the game is so good that the outsize hype is the least interesting thing about it.

China dominates battery graphite, but this startup could break the monopoly

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Electric vehicle sales in the U.S. may have leveled off, but the market for batteries inside them isn’t slowing down, quintupling in the last five years. Many of those are made in the U.S., but they’re largely dependent on China for key ingredients, including graphite.

Graphite is a key ingredient in lithium-ion batteries, where it forms the anode, the part that stores the battery’s charge. Chinese companies control about three-quarters of the graphite anode supply chain and 97% of synthetic (human-made) graphite anode production, according to Benchmark Mineral Intelligence. One five-year-old startup, Molten Industries, is working to break that dominance.

Molten Industries has started operating a demonstration plant in Oakland meant to prove the technology works, the company exclusively told TechCrunch. The plant is will pave the way for U.S.-made graphite to win a share of the market.

“The new plant will be producing graphite that’s at cost parity with China plus tariffs,” Caleb Boyd, co-founder and CTO of Molten Industries, told TechCrunch. In other words, once U.S. import taxes are added to Chinese graphite, Molten’s price would match it. He said future plants should match China’s cost even without tariffs, which he called a built-in advantage of the technology.

Molten’s Oakland plant will make about 300 metric tons of graphite annually, which is enough for 300 megawatt-hours of batteries. That’s enough for about 4,300 EVs. The bulk of the output will go to undisclosed customers that are testing it in their battery cells.

The next facility, which Molten has yet to announce, will take the same template used in the Oakland plant and replicate it several times over, said Boyd.

The startup’s technology began in a Silicon Valley garage. It uses electrical resistive heating, which passes electricity through a material to heat it, to split methane (the main ingredient in natural gas) into pure carbon and hydrogen. “You can think of it almost like a massive toaster,” Boyd said. The company says the system uses three times less electricity than existing methods, and it runs on natural gas, which is cheap and plentiful in the U.S.

“We think it’s very hard to compete with China if you’re just copying their processes and trying to compete on cost,” said Kevin Bush, who co-founded Molten Industries with Boyd and is the company’s CEO. “So we are using a resource in the United States that’s abundant in natural gas, and we’ve brought some innovation to actually be able to drive down the cost of graphite production globally.”

Here’s how the chemistry works: When methane (CH4) is heated without oxygen, each molecule breaks into one carbon atom and four hydrogen atoms. The hydrogen atoms pair up into hydrogen gas (H2), which Molten sells at market prices to a customer next door. The carbon is then processed to meet battery makers’ requirements.

The basic idea of using heat to separate methane has been around for over a century, but early attempts to commercialize it ran into trouble. Carbon built up and clogged the reactor, and Boyd said no company before Molten had been able to produce high-quality graphite this way.

Solving that problem changed the company’s direction. Boyd and Bush — both materials science PhDs from Stanford — originally set out to make clean hydrogen from natural gas. Most hydrogen today comes from steam reforming, a cheap but polluting process. The two wanted to reap the benefits of a low-cost feedstock like natural gas without the pollution, and they expected to sell the leftover carbon as a byproduct.

But as they experimented with ways to keep the reactor clean, “we actually started making this really high quality graphite,” Boyd said, which “changed the entire economics of what we were doing.” Molten went from a hydrogen company that happened to sell graphite to a battery-grade graphite company that happens to sell hydrogen.

The company says both businesses could bring in billions of dollars. If Molten supplied the entire U.S. battery market, it could also provide about a third of the hydrogen used in the country, the company claims. Other uses for high-quality graphite, like thermal batteries that store energy as heat, could expand both markets further.

The new factory, Boyd said, “is really a milestone, not just for the company, we think, but globally.”
