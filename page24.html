Anthropic’s 7 founders cashed out nearly $2B in record-breaking IPO

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic became a public company on Thursday, raising a total of $81.3 billion including $1.95 billion for its seven founders. As we previously reported, each of them ponied up about 3.4 million shares apiece to sell in the IPO.

At the upgraded IPO price of $83 per share, that means they each pocketed about $280 million before underwriting fees and taxes. It looked like they had calculated the number of shares they planned to sell to give them each around $250 million if shares hit $74. That was the midpoint price from Anthropic’s original price range. So that’s a cool $30-ish million more for each of them than perhaps they were gunning for.

Anthropic implied in its IPO filings that the founders aren’t entirely in this for the money though. The company said that each of the founders has pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal equity in the company to philanthropy.

However, if any of them struggle to live on their quarter of a billion, they can scale back their charitable commitments. The company points out that these commitments are “voluntary, revocable expressions of each co-founder’s philanthropic intent and do not constitute legally binding obligations.”

With the IPO in the record books, there were a few other interesting numbers worth highlighting.

$104.8 billion. That’s really the amount Anthropic raised for its coffers (with a possibility of $117 billion, if the bankers buy all of their allotment of shares). In addition to selling its Class A common stock, it also sold a previously announced $26 billion worth of a special non-voting Class N stock to some of the biggest tech names in the industry.

Nvidia bought $10 billion of these Class N shares, Google bought $10 billion, and AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft, and TSMC divvied up the rest.

Just for comparison: When Facebook had its then record-breaking IPO in 2012, it raised $16 billion on IPO day.

28% stake to Amazon and Google combined. All told, at the higher IPO share price, Google wound up owning 13.4% of the company. Amazon also now has a big stake in Anthropic because when it previously invested in the AI lab as a startup, it was granted promissory notes that could be converted to equity. It converted a small percentage of them as part of the IPO, leaving it with a 14.6% stake. Had it converted all of them, Amazon could have owned nearly 24%.

The two large cloud providers now own 28% of Anthropic, the AI lab says.

More than $1 trillion. As mind-blowing as it is to raise nearly $105 billion, giving Anthropic nearly $230 billion of working capital available (along with capital it already had on hand), the company’s need for future capital is still far greater.

Anthropic needs about $70.7 billion just to cover its compute and infrastructure spending commitments in the next 12 months as of the quarter ended September 30, 2026. It needs $451.6 billion to cover these commitments before the end of 2030. And it has racked up hundreds of billions of other commitments that are either conditional (and may not happen) or haven’t been priced yet.

All told, the company has disclosed a ballpark of $1.05 trillion to $1.08 trillion in infrastructure commitments in its foreseeable future. This was a huge IPO. But it is a pricey business to run.

Michael Burry Says He Tried to Warn You About Nvidia, CoreWeave and Nebius. Here's What the 'Big Short' Investor Saw

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The famed investor took a victory lap as AI stocks slid again on Thursday.

By Jonathan Ponciano – Nov 19, 2026 at 7:43PM EST

Michael Burry, the investor made famous by "The Big Short," isn't shy about saying "I told you so."

"Coreweave, Nebius, NVIDIA, semis, I tried to warn you all," Burry posted on X on Thursday afternoon. "I didn't say short. But I provided the information one would need to sell."

He picked a good day for it. Nvidia (NVDA -3.30%) fell 3.3% on Thursday, CoreWeave (CRWV -6.11%) dropped 6.1%, and Nebius Group (NBIS -9.39%) sank 9.4%, as tech stocks gave up an early rally.

Here's what Burry saw, and what investors should do with it.

Michael Burry. Image source: Getty Images.

Burry's warnings came in pieces. In mid-October, he shared a Wall Street Journal story arguing that CoreWeave's sell-off showed the pressure from rising borrowing costs, adding his own commentary: "Dumb da dumb dumb da."

CoreWeave and Nebius are so-called neoclouds, companies that rent out Nvidia-powered computing to AI developers. Building their data centers takes enormous amounts of money, much of it borrowed, so higher interest rates hit them hard.

On Nvidia, his case is about the long run. He says he expects Nvidia's competitive edge to shrink over the next five years. In September, he also argued that its chips "may be economically depreciating waiting in warehouses while GAAP depreciation remains at zero," meaning the hardware could be losing value faster than the accounting shows.

The numbers have lent credence to his concern. CoreWeave closed Thursday at $44.91, down 76% from highs last year, and Nebius finished at $96.92, down 66% from its June peak. Nvidia has held up better but still sits 39% below its October high. The damage has spread across the chip industry, too: The VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH -2.62%), a basket of the biggest chip stocks, is down 33% from its June high.

As he laid out his long-term worries about Nvidia earlier this month, Burry said he imagined "the whole market is going to think this is a heck of a trade fairly soon." But since then, the stock has fallen another 16%.

Nvidia's business, meanwhile, hasn't cracked. Its revenue jumped 103% to $115.8 billion last quarter, and management raised its growth forecast for next year. The stock's slide this month coincided with Jensen Huang's sudden resignation as CEO, rather than a drop in sales.

If you own neocloud stocks like CoreWeave and Nebius, watch how much they borrow and what that debt costs them. If you own Nvidia, watch whether customers keep buying at today's pace, and whether the chips they buy hold their value.

The AI stocks that come out of this sell-off in good shape will be the ones with the best answers to those questions. For long-term investors, that's a better guide than any single post on X, even one from Michael Burry.

LSD changes the brain’s rhythm in a way earlier studies missed

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Brain recordings from 17 volunteers show that LSD speeds up two brain rhythms even as it weakens them.

Brain recordings of people on psychedelics have told the same story for years: the brain’s steady electrical rhythms get weaker, as if the drug were turning the volume down.

A new analysis of recordings from 17 people given LSD finds that the volume isn’t the whole story. The drug also turns up the tempo.

LSD pushes two of the brain’s main rhythms to faster beats, and it makes the brain’s electrical activity more detailed and less predictable.

Venkatesh Subramani, a doctoral student at the Université de Montréal, led the analysis with his supervisor Karim Jerbi. Jerbi is a psychology professor there and a researcher at Mila, the Quebec artificial intelligence institute.

Co-authors included Nicolas Farrugia and Giulia Lioi of IMT Atlantique in France and Robin Carhart-Harris of the University of California, San Francisco.

“Imagine a radio station switching its broadcast frequency from 99.5 to 100.5 FM,” Jerbi said.

“If your radio stays tuned to 99.5, the signal may seem distorted or lost. In the same way, overlooking the shift to faster brain rhythms can lead us to misinterpret LSD’s effects on the brain.”

Groups of neurons firing together produce rhythms that a sensor outside the head can pick up. Researchers call them brain waves.

Alpha waves dominate when you rest with your eyes closed. Beta waves are faster and show up during concentration and decision-making.

Under psychedelics those waves look weaker, and the standard reading is that neurons are firing less in unison. Subramani’s team pointed out a catch.

If a rhythm speeds up, its peak can shift outside the frequency range researchers are measuring. That shift can make the wave look weaker even if its strength hasn’t changed.

So the team pulled the two possibilities apart. They first stripped out the brain’s rhythm-free background activity.

Then they located each person’s actual alpha and beta peaks under LSD and under placebo, and measured the power at those peaks.

The recordings were made at Imperial College London by Carhart-Harris’s group. Seventeen healthy volunteers came in twice, two weeks apart.

In one session, they received an intravenous injection of 75 micrograms of LSD, roughly the weight of a grain of salt. In the other they received a saline placebo, without being told which was which.

About four hours after the injection, each person lay in a magnetoencephalography scanner. It records the faint magnetic fields that neurons produce, with timing down to the millisecond.

They rested with their eyes closed for seven minutes, and in a separate seven-minute block they listened to ambient music.

The Montreal team reanalyzed those recordings with newer signal-processing tools and a machine-learning model trained to tell the LSD sessions from the placebo sessions.

Under LSD, alpha waves ran about 2 cycles per second faster than on placebo, and beta waves about 5 cycles per second faster, across large areas of the cortex. The waves did also weaken.

Once the speed-up was accounted for, that weakening held up as real rather than an artifact of looking in the wrong place.

The two effects didn’t fully overlap. The weakening was strongest in regions that handle hearing, language, and emotion.

The speed-up followed its own map, and the regions that weakened most tended to speed up least.

Anesthetics do the opposite to the alpha rhythm, slowing it down as the cortex becomes less excitable. The authors read the faster alpha under LSD as a sign of a more excitable cortex.

They also noted that psilocybin and DMT haven’t been shown to shift the peaks this way, so the speed-up may be a signature specific to LSD.

A fractal is a shape that repeats itself in its own parts, the way each floret of a Romanesco broccoli is a smaller broccoli.

The team measured how fractal the brain signal was over time, and how varied its patterns were. Both rose under LSD.

The increases were largest in areas tied to vision, hearing, memory, and mental imagery, and smallest in the motor cortex.

That split fits a familiar feature of the drug. Perception and inner experience change a great deal, while people can still move normally.

People on LSD often report seeing repeating geometric patterns with their eyes closed. The paper links the two only loosely.

“Our findings lend support to this intriguing hypothesis, but they do not yet confirm it,” Jerbi said. “We measure the fractal structure of brain signals over time, not the fractal structure of the images people perceive.”

Given only the shift in peak frequencies, the machine-learning model identified the LSD session more than 82% of the time.

Models trained on the fractal and complexity measures did almost as well. The brain regions the models relied on matched the ones the conventional statistics had flagged.

“Our machine-learning algorithm can distinguish brain states under LSD from those under placebo using brain signals alone,” Subramani said.

“By examining the clues it relies on, we can identify LSD’s signatures in brain activity.”

Music, which is routinely played during psychedelic therapy, didn’t amplify any of these effects. If anything the trend ran the other way, with slightly smaller brain changes during the music block.

That difference didn’t survive the statistical correction, and the authors called it exploratory.

Seventeen people is a small group, and everyone received a single dose while healthy, so the study says nothing about whether any of this helps patients.

The volunteers rated their experience at the peak of the drug’s effect, while the scanner ran about two and a half hours later.

The authors wrote that this gap may be why brain changes and reported experiences lined up only loosely.

The recordings also included no direct measure of muscle activity, which can contaminate the fastest brain signals. So the authors treated their high-frequency results with extra caution.

The next question is whether the speed-up really belongs to LSD alone.

Subramani and his colleagues are now running the same analysis on recordings taken under psilocybin, the active ingredient in magic mushrooms, and under DMT.

The aim is to see whether the three drugs produce the same signatures in the brain or different ones.

The full study was published in the journal Communications Biology.

Thus, even if all of the 1.7% of voters who chose Dan J. actually intended to back Dan S., it made no difference. Alaska's system of ranked choice voting (under which, if no candidate gets a majority in the first round, votes get reallocated) might have further reduced any problem. But no reallocation took place here, because Peltola got an absolute majority to begin with. This is likely what led DOJ to finally thrown in the towel.

As noted in one of my earlier posts about the case, Democrats may well deserve blame for seeking to deceive voters in this way, even though it was never likely to change the result (evidence indicates Dan J. may have gotten on the ballot as part of a Democratic effort to siphon off voters from Dan S.). Te Araroa day 22: Auckland here we come!

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

27.5km, Mangawhai trail host to Pakiri Paradise

Most of us (me, Lindsay, Sinead, Annabel and Anica) got going at around 7am again, although this time with good reason as we were expecting a bit of a longer day and needed to make a tidal river crossing later. The place we were staying was run by people who had previously done TA and provided everything hikers could need, at the very reasonable price of $10 per night. It was a great place to stay, and we walked out of the gate practically right back onto trail. We had been talking about coffee, but didn’t think that anything would be open at that time. But luck was on our side! Literally 50 metres down the street there was a coffee and smoothie van. What a great start to the day. While I was at university I was very much fuelled by coffee, but since then I’ve mostly switched to decaf. But I have been enjoying drinking it again while I’ve been hiking – it gives me a great energy boost, which puts me in a good mood, and I think the negative side effects I’ve experienced previously are reduced by having physical activity to funnel that energy into.

I found a friend when I went to pack away my tent

Coffee obtained, it was going to be a good morning!

Unfortunately, the day started with a decent road walk of about 7km, which I don’t think any of us had quite registered was coming. Eventually we made it to the beach we would be walking along for the greater part of the day. The signage was familiar to me and I didn’t quite realise why until I registered that it was Auckland Council signage. We had made it to Auckland region! The Northland/Auckland boundary is just south of Mangawhai so that morning, we had completed the first region of Te Araroa. Northland is one of the longest (perhaps the longest?) regions on TA, owing to its long and thin geography. So this was a significant milestone. It was also probably the region I was least looking forward to, yet I loved it. Not every minute, of course, but distance had made my heart grow fonder and I was able to appreciate the beauty of the place with fresh eyes.

The beach looked pleasingly short when we got onto it. That is, until I realised that the headland I could see was not the end of the beach section, but just a blip a few kilometres in. Still, the first section went pretty quickly and soon we were approaching the headland. There were hoards of surfers and foil board surfers, which looked pretty cool! There was also an interesting set up on the beach. Evidently a photoshoot was going on – a bed was set up, and a model posed on it in pajamas while a drone hovered and various involved people fluttered around. I’m sure the six dirty hikers walking through the background didn’t improve their shots!

The first part of the beach, looking towards Te Arai point

Up on Te Arai Point, there were awesome views along the coast. The tracks weren’t super well-marked though, and the route was a bit un-intuitive. I was with Lindsay and Sinead at this point, and eventually we reached a wooded section that popped out on a road back to the beach. Just before the road we stumbled (almost literally) upon Zoe, who was taking a break in what she pointed out might be the last shade for a while. We plopped down to join her, and collectively decided that the rest of the beach necessitated podcasts. I realised I had forgotten to download more episodes when I last had wifi, and scrambled around for something to listen to. I tried an audiobook but I wasn’t really getting into it, and stopped to switch to music and try to find the specific song I had in my head that morning (u + me = <3 by Olivia Rodrigo, in case you were wondering). Tunes acquired, I set off again.

This beach was actually perhaps the most annoying we have had to walk down so far. The tide was fully in at this point and the available area to walk on was soft, slopey, and narrow in places. There was one spot where the waves had created a bit of a cliff about 75cm tall, with very little space between the top of the cliff and the dunes behind. My feet were already wet from a stream crossing earlier, so I just decided to commit and walk in the water below the cliff. Apparently some of the others had taken their chances with the top of the cliff, with fairly unsuccessful results. It was a shame they were behind me, as the descriptions they gave of their falls were pretty amusing!

Walking over Te Arai point, with the surfers in the background

The other notable thing about the beach was the number of dead birds we came across. I really haven’t seen that many before. I hope it isn’t bird flu, which has recently arrived in NZ – perhaps it’s just something to do with the ocean currents that lots of dead stuff washes up there?

At about 11:45 I was hungry. I was going to grab a snack and keep going, but realised I might as well stop for lunch. Who says lunch can’t happen before 12, anyway? I was hungry so I might as well eat properly. I enjoyed my wraps with fresh salad, although I kept dropping things that then got covered in sand. Annabel had told me about these sun-dried tomatoes that come without oil, and I had bought some in Mangawhai. They were great, 10/10 reccommend those. While I was lunching, the others all passed me. After a few more kilometres of walking, I came across everyone sat on the beach eating their (late) lunch. We were nearly at the tidal estuary, which the trail notes said could be crossed 90 minutes either side of low tide. It was 2pm, and low tide wasn’t until nearly 5. But the others reckoned someone else had crossed outside of the reccommended window, and we decided to go and have a look.

The butterflies were having a good dance in my stomach as we approached the estuary. I find rivers quite scary, especially when I don’t know how deep they’ll be. We went to the mouth, as that’s where the smaller streams on the beach had been shallowest. But the water here looked quite fast-flowing, so we went further up to a wider, slower bend. It was impossible to tell how deep it was, but at least we wouldn’t get swept away. Sinéad offered to be the guinea pig. She went up to her ankles, then her knees – and then she was basically across. No problem! We all followed without difficulty. Sometimes the trail notes really overstate the difficulty of a thing. I guess it can be deeper sometimes – but we crossed 3 hours before low tide with no bother, when the notes suggested 90 minutes. One of the most valuable pieces of advice I got about TA, which I tell to everyone, came from a couple of NOBO hikers that I picked up near Nelson Lakes last season. They said that there is a lot of scare-mongering from other hikers, but you have to see for yourself and not put too much stock in what everyone else is saying. And apparently the same goes for the trail notes, too!

The crossing was so fine! Zoe wading across with me, Lindsay, Annabel and Anica waiting. Photo by Sinéad

Putting shoes and socks back on after the beach

We walked up the beach towards a holiday park. We were planning to go a bit further for the day but hoped they would sell us some cold drinks, and maybe fill some water bottles. It was a bit unclear where to go, but there was a man mowing the grass. ‘You lot look like an invading army!’ he called down to us. I suppose the six of us did look like we were plotting how to storm the castle! He invited us to come through the campground and rinse out our shoes and socks. After my sandpaper socks the other day, this was a very welcome invitation. He also said that the notes don’t give good advice about where to cross the river we had just been over. It’s much shallower inland, but the area is home to rare nesting birds, so DOC won’t allow the TA trust to tell people to cross further inland. Personally, I’m happy to wait a bit for the tides if it means not disturbing the birds. But I can see that it’s frustrating for the locals to see people being given less-good safety advice.

After washing our feet, we all headed over to the shop where we bought ice creams and cold drinks, and checked out the next bit of the route. Everyone kept saying it was really steep, but for some reason I didn’t think it looked too bad on the map. Then I decided to look at a different map app, the one I normally use, and the contour lines looked way denser! I realised that the contours on the TA app are really light and narrow compared to the ones on the map I’m used to, so things don’t look as steep. I guess that could be helpful, in some ways.

After a while, we headed off down the road. It was a fairly quiet road, and it wasn’t too long before we reached some track. The hill began immediately, and it was steep but not impossible. I ended up passing the others at the first flattish bit and continued on up. The views were pretty good and I felt happy about my fitness. I had a full 4 day resupply on my back but I found the hill ok, and I was able to keep plugging on without having to stop too often. Towards the top, there was a large fallen tree that I had to scramble over. Then I realised I was at the turn off for where we would be staying, and stopped to wait for the others. I watched them all clamber over the tree with varying levels of elegance. I would have taken photos but I didn’t want to walk back down the hill.

You can just see the others lower down the hill

The house we were staying at was a couple of hundred metres along a side-track. Annabel and I got there first, and the couple came out and introduced themselves, and asked where we were from. Annabel is also from southern England, to which the guy responded that we were both ‘bloody Southerners’; he’s from Yorkshire! The rest of the group arrived and they showed us where we could pitch our tents. It wasn’t as luxurious as the previous night, but we had everything we needed. And, they showed us the view of Auckland city from their garden! This was the first time we had seen the city, and although it still looked very far away, it was cool to know we were getting closer. Everyone was planning to take some time off or visit friends in Auckland, and it’s also the first major city on the route. So it seems to have become a big milestone in everyone’s minds.

You can’t see it, but trust me when I say that the Auckland city skyline was visible in this view

The weather was threatening rain so we pitched our tents, which were soaked from the morning dew still. I managed to snag a decent spot, but some of the others were less lucky and Annabel realised she had pitched her tent in a bit of a bog. Our hosts came out with a packet of scorched almonds, which was very nice of them! A little while later, Xavier showed up. He was at the campsite the previous night, but I didn’t end up speaking to him. Some of the others knew him from before, but I hadn’t met him so I took the chance to introduce myself. Then we carefully prepared dinner on the tables and chairs that kept sinking into the mud, and sat around talking for a while before calling it a night.

SOCi's context layer is the unified source of truth for brand knowledge, guidelines and goals that guides SOCi's agentic workforce of more than 400,000 deployed Genius Agentsâ¢. With the MCP and more than 140 integrations, that intelligence now flows in from the systems enterprises already run on and out to the places their teams already work.

The announcement moves SOCi from a platform that agents work in, to an interconnected intelligence layer that speaks to, learns from and works with a brand's most important systems.

"Agents will only ever be as smart as the context they are allowed to see," said Afif Khoury, Founder and CEO of SOCi. "SOCi's context layer already knows the brand, every location and what earns visibility in local discovery. Our integrations bring in the rest of the stack, and through the MCP, our agents and tools are available wherever our customers' teams already work."

Context in: an agentic workforce that learns from the whole stack

SOCi's Genius Agents make better decisions when they can see more of the business. SOCi's integrations bring signals from the systems that run enterprise operations into the context layer, so agents act on what is actually happening at each location. Context out: SOCi's intelligence wherever work happens

SOCi's MCP server, now generally available to all SOCi customers, lets AI assistants and agent frameworks that support the Model Context Protocol call SOCi's standard platform tools, agentic tools, and reporting and insights directly, so the results become part of the context those assistants work from. A marketer can ask their AI assistant to "show me my SOCi locations" or "which locations need review responses," and the assistant calls SOCi, gets the answer and responds, without the marketer logging in to another interface. SOCi customers can also work with the same tools through Ask Genius, SOCi's agentic front door to the SOCi platform.

Access is governed by SOCi's enterprise permissions. SOCi Shield applies brand and compliance rules with approval paths for work that needs a person's judgment, and SOCi holds SOC 2 Type II and ISO/IEC 42001 credentials.

"Context flows in, so agents are grounded in how the business actually runs," said Alo Sarv, co-founder and CTO of SOCi. "And through the MCP, SOCi's tools, agents and insights flow out to wherever our customers' teams are already working. That is what makes the workforce compound."

From software you manage to work that gets done

Enterprise brands no longer have to choose between a platform that works and a stack that connects. SOCi's agents execute local search, social and reputation work at every location, and the context layer behind them is grounded in more of the business with every system it connects to.
