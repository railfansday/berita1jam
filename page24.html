Jensen Huang’s return is what Silicon Valley wanted—but things won’t necessarily be the same

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jensen Huang’s affair with an alleged Chinese spy led to his ouster from Nvidia. 11 days later, he’s back—the outcome Silicon Valley founders, watchers, and luminaries wanted all along.

Within hours of Huang’s original (and ignominious) exit from Nvidia earlier this month, a “Stand With Jensen” petition circulated online, demanding Huang’s return to the company he’d cofounded in 1993. Signatories included Elon Musk, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney, Shopify CEO Tobias Lütke, Atreides Management CIO Gavin Baker, and Databricks CEO Ali Ghodsi, who wrote in the comments “Nvidia runs on Jensen! Bring him back!”

Travis Kalanick, Uber’s pugnacious cofounder and former CEO, was both a signatory and especially vocal in his support.

“Jensen is one of the great builders of our time,” Kalanick—himself ousted in 2017 from Uber amid compounding scandals—wrote in a public post on the “Stand With Jensen” site. “Liberty and civilizational progress depend on Jensen,” Kalanick declared. “He has erred, yes—to err is to be human—but I do not believe anything he has done rises to the bar of separating him from his company. Jensen deserves fairness in adjudication and we must presume innocence until that adjudication proves otherwise beyond a reasonable doubt.”

Huang’s return is a Rorschach test of sorts. Some critics see a corporate governance nightmare, while much of the wider public has memed or expressed horror at the circumstances of Huang’s initial resignation (widely described as potentially raising national security concerns). In Silicon Valley though, Huang’s ouster was an injustice that trumped any other aspects of the story . To tech, Huang remains the most indispensable of founders, a nearly god-like figure in a culture that worships “founder mode.”

So, Huang’s return, however questionable it may be, is cause for celebration. Even the very circumspect Sam Altman, who’d previously expressed cautious sympathy for Huang (and who of course, went through a very similar ordeal), tweeted on X on Monday, “Jensen!” Musk, meanwhile, tweeted: “The mistake has been corrected.” Sarah Guo, the plugged-in founder of Conviction VC, welcomed Huang back. The chorus from tech was, in its way, relatively unanimous about just how positive this news was. (The markets agreed vigorously, Nvidia closed yesterday up about 31%, after last week’s massive tech rout.) Meanwhile, for Nvidia’s board, it’s a dicey U-turn.

“Six days after NVIDIA’s board said it had no intention of reinstating Jensen Huang as CEO, he’s back—before the board’s own independent investigation has reported anything, before he answers questions from Congress next week, and with no conditions attached (or at least none that anyone outside the boardroom can see),” said Nicole Bestard, founder of strategic communications firm Quarter Horse Strategies, via email. “From a reputation standpoint, this is a huge mistake. It asks investors, employees, and the public to take the board’s judgment on faith.”

The move from here, in part, is likely to attempt to bury this, David Tepper, CEO and cofounder of Ascerta, predicts.

“It very likely means that Nvidia will be putting out their next GPU cycle (the 6000 series) much sooner than otherwise expected,” he said via email. “They will be eager to bury the news cycle with Nvidia’s continued dominance in the space versus keeping the attention on his infidelity.”

Nevertheless, things are not likely to be the same from here on out, said Umesh Padval, managing partner at Seligman Ventures.

“No one can replace the vision he has,” said Padval. “With him back, he may not go back to 100% immediately, he’ll likely be more cautious. It will still be significantly better than a few weeks back.”

Translation: We still can’t assume Huang will continue to be as aggressive an investor, operator, and guarantor as he may have been before, even with a supposed return to normalcy.

The private markets ecosystem has to stand by itself,” said Padval. “This was driven by Nvidia a lot in the past, the whole AI ecosystem. If participation is less, the venture community needs to step it up with conviction. We all need to believe in the companies we’re backing. And if we believe in the companies, [we] don’t have to wait for the signal from Nvidia.”

Allie Garfinkle is a senior writer and editor at Fortune, where she runs Term Sheet; leads coverage of private capital, investors, and startups; and co-chairs the Brainstorm conference series.

In This Seaside Rhode Island Family Home, the Interiors Have an Ever-Changing Color Palette Inspired by the Ocean

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“It was like Frankenstein,” says the husband of the shingled postmodern house on Rhode Island’s rocky coast that he shares with his wife and their three young-adult children. Built by his father in the 1980s, the house occupies an extraordinary site overlooking the Atlantic ocean and an expansive marsh. But successive additions had left it architecturally disjointed. The Manhattan-based couple, fresh from updating a Litchfield County weekend retreat with AD PRO Directory designer James “Ford” Huniford, asked him to take another look. “We were thrilled to have him try to rethink this house for us,” says the wife, “which may have been easier in retrospect to just knock down.”

In a favorite sitting room, which Huniford reconfigured from a triangle into a rectangle, rattan Audoux-Minet armchairs and a custom sofa flanked by 1930s Kittinger pedestal tables wear cushions covered in a Colefax and Fowler chintz. The walls, clad in Scalamandré wallpaper, display 1950s ceramic plates from Italy’s Amalfi Coast, while an antique French chandelier hangs overhead. The coffee table is custom and the carpet is from Sacco. “I was impressed with [Ford’s] ability to take risks and do things that were different than he had done,” says the wife, pointing to the chintz fabric, “and we were very happy with the outcome.”

The French wicker barrel chair with a Jasper-covered cushion and a custom sofa, upholstered in Pierre Frey, complete the seating arrangement around a vintage coffee table. Layered beneath are rugs from Doris Leslie Blau and Sacco, the former a midcentury Scandinavian design. Rococo side tables each support a 1930s Camille Tharaud vase lamp, while the 1961 patinated-brass ceiling lights by Jacques Quinet are from Galerie Apaganthe.

Rather than impose a conventional beach-house aesthetic, Huniford sought to evoke the early-20th-century summer houses of New England while preserving the existing structure’s character. His references ranged from the work of architect Fred Lincoln Savage on Maine’s Mount Desert Island to Kilkare, a Victorian oceanfront estate in Wainscott, New York. New rooflines, structural bump-outs, covered porches, traditional divided-light windows, and detailing brought a sense of accumulated history, even as the house was winterized and its systems updated. Expanded interiors and improved flow were achieved with remarkably little change to the overall footprint. “To me,” says Huniford, “the luxury is in the detail of the craft.”

Garden-inspired Ferrick Mason wallpaper enlivens the jewel-box powder room, where a vintage pedestal sink is paired with a Samuel Heath faucet. Antique Italian sconces flank a vintage Italian mirror from Donzella, while a green 1970s pendant by Hans-Agne Jakobsson for Staff and a Rug & Kilim rug complete the space.

In the dining area, Carlos Mendoza chairs upholstered in a Pierre Frey fabric surround a custom table draped with an antique coarse-linen buggy blanket and topped with Vallauris woven plates and a 1960s Émile Tessier cachepot. Above hangs a 19th-century Dutch brass chandelier from Brownrigg, while Josef Albers color case studies enliven the walls. “What I love about Ford is: I was like, ‘Are you sure that we should paint the walls yellow?’” the wife recalls of Huniford’s custom paint selection. “And he’s like, ‘I don’t know. This is a whole new world for me, but yeah, I think we should.’”

Inside, the designer took his cues from the wraparound vistas and shifting natural light, particularly during the summer months, when the family spends most of its time here. “The ocean can be dark black and then it can be blue and then it can be green,” says Huniford. “I love the sea. It’s such a magical thing.” That ever-changing palette informed rooms of relaxed refinement, where the mix includes English, French, and early American furnishings, many slipcovered in upholstery washed to achieve an effortlessly lived-in quality. “They don’t like clutter, and they don’t like fussiness,” Huniford notes of his clients. “They like function, and they love form.”

In the kitchen, striped wall covering by Maresca Textiles provides a lively backdrop for vintage opaline globe wall lights, Newport Brass plumbing fixtures, and a collection of vintage ceramics and tableware. A French bobbin chair is from Andrew Spindler Antiques & Design.

In a newly added pantry adjoining the dining area, new and vintage tabletop treasures include Tiffany & Co. salt and pepper mills, a stoneware pitcher by Bennington Potters, green Dieulefit glasses, and bowls from Paula Rubenstein.

The palette of another sitting room takes its cues from the nearby ocean. Braided-rattan lounge chairs by Henry Link, sourced at Brimfield and topped with cushions in a Décors Barbares fabric, flank an armless sofa with Christopher Farr–covered seat cushions and back cushions upholstered in a Fabricut textile. The coffee table, side table, and lamp are vintage; the ceiling fixture is by Schoolhouse, and the carpet is from Stark.

Yet there is plenty of playfulness beneath the restraint. Huniford introduced an unexpected palette and, in a first for both designer and clients, chintz. In the open-plan living, kitchen, and dining areas, where windows were left free of treatments, sea-glass-green doors complement wide-plank walls painted a buttery yellow. Colorful works by Josef Albers, a favorite of the husband and sourced by Huniford, provide a graphic counterpoint near a dining table surrounded by whitewashed chairs and draped with an antique rough-linen buggy cover. In an adjacent sitting room, rattan furniture brightened by cushions in a radiant Colefax & Fowler floral take center stage. “I never thought in a million years that I’d have chintz in my house,” admits the wife, “but it now happens to be my favorite fabric.”

A dreamy layering of florals defines the primary bedroom, from Temple Studio wallpaper and a headboard and bed skirt in Bennison Fabrics textiles to a Matouk bedspread and pillowcases and a blanket from The Company Store. A vase and lamp from Huniford’s vintage stock sit atop a side table from Fernyhough Antiques.

An English neoclassical mahogany desk by Kittinger, sourced on Chairish, is where the husband prefers to work while taking in ocean views. The desk chair and lamp are vintage, while a dhurrie rug from Rug & Kilim anchors the space. The window treatments are fashioned from a Savel textile.

In the primary bath, vintage painted sconces with glass shades illuminate a custom mirror and Grand Brass plumbing fixtures. A series of framed vintage French dried botanical specimens hangs above a vintage blue firkin.

Alan Campbell wallpaper sets the tone in this bedroom, where a vintage bed and console—the latter painted in Benjamin Moore’s Casco Bay—join assorted furnishings from Huniford’s own stock to create a transportive retreat.

In a guest bedroom, twin beds feature headboards covered in a punchy Designtex print and vintage bedding. A side table sourced on Chairish stands beneath a framed collection of 1920s floral prints.

For Huniford, whose primary mission is to understand how all of his clients think and live, the goal here was not to reinvent the family’s lifestyle but to make the house better accommodate it. A windowed banquette in a new bump-out beside the dining table has become a favorite gathering spot; its overhead pottery shelf was inspired by a photograph of decorator and fashion editor Chessy Rayner’s Southampton home. The adjacent sitting room, long a center of family life, was transformed from an awkward triangle into a rectangle, allowing the space to function more comfortably. “Our lifestyle in this house,” the husband now says, “did not change.”

The three-year undertaking benefited from the couple’s willingness to give Huniford considerable creative freedom, and the time to realize his vision. The result feels as though it has evolved over generations rather than been assembled piecemeal, its newfound polish tempered by a familiar sense of ease. “I think a great house should make you feel welcome and welcoming,” says Huniford. And that, it seems, has been the greatest success. As the wife puts it, “Everyone loves to go there.”

Whenever weather permits, the family gathers for meals on the ocean-facing deck, where vintage Brown Jordan chairs with cushions in a Perennials fabric surround a Laurel Group table dressed with a vintage Italian tablecloth, napkins, and tableware.

Huniford’s design extended beyond the architecture and interiors to the surrounding landscape, where the expansive front lawn gave way to meadow grasses, and an asphalt driveway was replaced with gravel. “I’ve done a few gardens for clients before, and I think [these homeowners] understood my sensibility,” says Huniford. “They did not want a lot of maintenance or formal gardens.” The staircase leading to the second-floor entrance ranks among the designer’s favorite structural additions.

Burning Man CEO Marian Goodell to Exit: ‘I’m Not Tired, I’m Not Done — I’m Ready to Be a Mentor’

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Burning Man's CEO since 2013, Goodell will remain in the role during the search for a new leader and keep her seat on the Burning Man board.

Burning Man Project’s longtime CEO Marian Goodell has announced that she’s leaving the position.

Goodell will remain as CEO during the search for a new leader of Burning Man Project, the nonprofit organization behind the annual gathering in Nevada’s Black Rock Desert that also supports the global Burning Man community, including the worldwide network of official Regional Burning Man Events. Goodell will remain on the Burning Man board after stepping down.

Goodell tells Billboard that while there is no set goal date to have someone new in the CEO role, she’s “fairly confident that having somebody in place at the beginning of the year — so they can spend some time with me — is a reasonable expectation.” She says that as one of the co-founders of Burning Man, she’s keen to help mentor a new CEO who will serve as the next generation of leadership in the event’s four-decade history.

Burning Man has hired a search firm to assist with finding a new CEO, with the organization anticipating that it will field candidates from the global Burning Man community and beyond. Anyone can put themselves or someone else forward for the role at executivesearch@burningman.org.

Goodell first went to Burning Man in 1995 — the sixth year the gathering happened in the Black Rock Desert after its early years on San Francisco’s Baker Beach. She helped found the Burning Man Project organization in 1997 and has been CEO since 2013. The organization now has 130 full-time employees.

Goodell shepherded Burning Man through an acutely challenging era. Burning Man co-founder Larry Harvey died in 2018, and the Black Rock City event was canceled in 2020 and 2021 due to the pandemic. This gathering subsequently experienced several difficult years due to weather, most notably a rain event in 2023, along with funding challenges that led the organization to launch a $20 million fundraising push in 2024.

Goodell says that as she exits the role, the next CEO “is inheriting a company and an organization that’s financially stable, that is behaving very responsibly about managing costs.” She notes that attendance at Black Rock City 2026 was roughly 64,000, a number the organization plans on capping going forward.

“If we look at what’s happening around the world and the culture, it doesn’t make sense to keep pretending that we could be at 78,000 [attendees] while gas prices are what they are, and Canadians don’t want to come to America,” says Goodell. “We need to be able to plan for expenses, size, volunteers, leadership, ice sales, etc. Why not just cap it at a place we found really amazing this year?”

Goodell also notes that the organization is “poised to start an endowment… Philanthropy is our cutting edge.”

Here, she talks about her decision to step down from the CEO role, and what comes next.

When I took over the role that my co-founders asked me to [take] in January of 2013, the whole point was that we were only going to run it as founder-led for as long as it took for the DNA of the organization to take on the instinct that the founders had themselves built through our collaboration and work together. It was always, always going to be that.

And why now — mostly because it’s time. It’s really easy for me to see that the work I’ve done is taking hold. I’ve been working on this [exit] since about 2022, 2021, right at the tail end of the pandemic when we had time on our hands, and I really looked at what we were doing inside the organization to actually prepare us for it… For me, it was more that during the pandemic we had the time to reflect and to create the organization in a way that makes it feel like we’re on the road to success. That the mission is actually not just Black Rock City. That people are getting it. And that’s what we’re seeing. As you wrote about in 2024, the regionals are now doing Burning Man culture more than what’s happening in Black Rock City — and that’s the win.

What is the next CEO inheriting? What’s the state of the org going into 2027?

The org, the event and the culture are all in great shape. The need to bring Burning Man culture out into the world is as strong as ever. The event did fabulously this year. We expect to have an event next year that’ll be about the same size. You’ll hear announcements about how we will [plan for] size and ticket sales in the near future. Nothing smaller, nothing bigger seems like the right size for where we’re at. And the organization structurally is in fabulous shape.

The last time we spoke was November of 2024, when the $20 million fundraiser was kicking off. Where are you financially, and what will the next person be inheriting in terms of finances?

The next person is inheriting a company and an organization that’s financially stable, that is behaving very responsibly about managing costs. It’s leaning into the opportunity that philanthropy provides us, so that we can make ticket prices available to a wide variety of people. They’ll be inheriting an organization that is leaning into the real mission, which is to bring Burning Man out into the world and managing the resources towards both the endeavors of Black Rock City and being out in the world.

2024 was hard — we did not hit our mark. [In] 2025 we hit our mark, we ended up with money on the books. This year we will hit our mark; it does not look like 2024… We are not in a state of crisis at all. We don’t leave in states of crisis.

The last years have been difficult, to say the least, with the pandemic forcing two years of cancellations, then a very hot 2022 and a major rain event in 2023. How do you reflect on those crises and your role in being there through them?

If you look at my tenure from 2013 and the first number of years, there’s really no crisis you can point to until Larry passes away. And then you end up with this series of challenges, one after another.

The whole point of Burning Man culture is to continue bringing in new people. Stepping aside for the next leader, the next generation. I’m talking the talk. We’ve brought the Rising Sparks up. [Editor’s note: Rising Sparks is a collective focused on bringing a new generation of artists, leaders and other Burners into Burning Man.] I’m the one that found the gal. I met this 22-year-old who comes and says, “I want to crack the code — I don’t understand why my generation isn’t here.” We did see an increase in young people this year.

When I spoke to a woman recently here in San Francisco, she said, “Yeah, we came this year, but we were 25 [people], and we’re normally 40,” and I said, “What are you doing to recruit the next generation?” Thunderdome didn’t come this year, and one of the things she said was, “We’re getting older and having babies and don’t feel like coming.”

So I don’t know why I should stay on any longer rather than find the opportunity to mentor. If we do this right, I’m a mentor. Just like we want when we turn over good leadership. There’s no other festival that proactively looks to its community to imagine how they want to step in. I’ve been watching for the right timing for me to step aside. It’s when I’m not tired, when I’m not done, when I’m ready to be a mentor and support the next generation.

What qualities does the ideal CEO candidate need?

When we started talking about this, people were like, “Oh my god, we’re never going to find anybody.” And I said, “I don’t necessarily think that’s true.” People keep over-indexing that I’m a founder, but it’s more about finding leadership to run the organization that’s dug into the culture. It’s someone who brings people together, can make clear decisions that advance the mission, and is enthusiastic about philanthropy. It could be someone who runs a nonprofit or a business — I do not think we need nonprofit experience specifically. I think you need deep cultural experience. And not necessarily all Burning Man. If you understand culture and you understand how to hold a space for culture, you’ll do well.

You’ve gotten so much criticism over the years, and the Burning Man documentary that was released in July included footage of Burners talking about wanting you to leave the CEO role. How tough does one need to be to be in this role?

The next person is definitely going to have to have thick skin. I’ve been here long enough to see waves of feedback, whether toward me, or other founders, or leadership in different departments. One has to be firm in understanding what you’re here for, what your values are, and be committed to the big picture — not take it personally. I really, really rarely take it personally. I know that I’ve done a good job.

And you’ve got to be authentic. Anybody who comes to this job with ego and thinks they’re going to do it to be famous is going to hurt themselves. But if you come to it to be of service for something that is truly transformative — the people in the organization, the volunteers, the temporary employees, all of the community — that is the kind of person who needs to look at this.

Who will have input in deciding on the next CEO?

The board has a search committee with five board members leading it. They’re working with a search firm. The plan is to have candidates vetted by board members first, and then staff, including myself, will all have input. But I’m not a decision maker. I trust the process. The board knows that if they put someone in place that the staff aren’t excited about, that’s going to be like organ rejection. You don’t want that.

What’s been the most difficult decision you’ve made during your tenure as CEO?

Canceling the event in 2021. That was the hardest decision because it took a long time to get there, and it had factors I couldn’t control. In 2021, we were all starting to get access to vaccines, and there was a lot of pressure to vaccinate participants and move forward. But when we had to make the decision — in March and April of 2021 — children still weren’t getting vaccinated, and our international participants couldn’t come.

And then there was a divide right down the community — employees, extended volunteer leadership, theme camp leadership. Some camps said, “No way, we won’t do it.” Other camps petitioned us and said, “We need this.” Board members were saying, “Are you kidding? We need the hope. We need the community. We need to build together.” So listening to both ends of that strong opinion, while knowing we’re not going to do an event without children or without international people — that was the hardest part. 10 Hidden Controls Every 'GTA 6' Player Should Know

You can have a ton of fun in GTA 6 even if you only follow the core gameplay loop of running around, stealing cars, and completing missions and side quests. However, if you take a minute to learn a few hidden controls, I think you'll enjoy the game even more. These are fairly simple controls that let you hide from the cops, trash talk other drivers, or flip the bird when you're feeling like annoying NPCs. These are 10 of the best hidden GTA 6 controls that'll help you unlock an entirely new side of the game.

If you just want to sit back and relax as your character drives around Leonida in GTA 6, you should try the auto-drive mode. It's a relaxing way to enjoy the streets without having to worry about following traffic rules. To enable it, you need to get in a vehicle and add a waypoint on the map. This serves as your character's destination, so if you want a longer drive, be sure to drop the waypoint far, far away. Next, hold the down arrow on the D-Pad, and press the Option button. This enables cinematic mode. Then, hold the Circle button to enable auto-drive.

Your character is going to commit a lot of crimes in GTA 6. When this happens, you attract heat, and soon enough, cops will begin chasing you. Switching cars is a great way to reduce the amount of attention you get from law enforcement, but you can still be identified by your face or clothing. Until you can reach a safe spot to swap outfits, maintain a low profile by ducking while driving. Hold the Circle button while driving to duck.

EA's Road Rash was among the first racing games I played as a child, and I remember how much fun it was to kick your rivals while speeding through the game's various courses. You can have a taste of that in GTA 6 too. Press the Square button while you're riding a bike, and your character will kick anyone who's near you. If you do this in a car, it opens the door, and hits people around the car. To annoy other drivers, press R3, and your character will direct some trash talk towards NPCs.

Hold Circle to lean back against walls, or to sit on the beach. To rest on a ledge, you can hold L3. This lets you take a moment to soak in beautiful views in the game, or lounge around as you listen to NPC conversations. Paying attention to those conversations can lead to some fun side quests, and sometimes give you a tip or two for quick money or other rewards.

If you really want to rile people up in GTA 6, you can flip the bird. This works only when your character is unarmed. Hold L2, focus on a person, then press R1. Actions have consequences in GTA 6, so be prepared to fight NPCs when you do this.

When you're being chased by cops, enemies, or angry NPCs, you can use a quick shortcut to look back without turning the camera around. Just hold R3 to look behind you.

While driving, press Circle to toggle headlights (and switch to high beams). This is particularly useful when you're offroading at night. You can also hold left arrow on the D-Pad, and move the right stick up to access other vehicle controls like hazard lights, opening or closing windows, etc., in case you want to add to the realism.

Stealing emergency vehicles in GTA 6 is risky, but the reward is totally worth it. Not only do you find stashes of weapons, gear, and money in some of these vehicles, but you also have access to sirens that help you clear traffic off your path. If you steal an ambulance or a cop car, hold L3, and move the left stick up to go through different siren tones. Press L3 and the left stick up to enable the siren.

GTA 6 is a third-person game, but it has a first-person mode that partially works. It is useful for looking at smaller objects around your character, such as posters, small signs, or to look for clues in and around a house. (Yes, I am talking about Mandy's creepy house and bad vibes.) Hold L2 + hit R3 to switch to first-person mode. Sadly, you can't move when you do this, but you can look around. Try it in Mandy's property to observe a few nasty surprises.

When you sprint towards a car, press the X button to slide across the hood. This is much faster and smoother than running around parked cars or jumping over them. It also looks cool, which is reason enough to try this feature.
