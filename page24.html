Here's What the Average Social Security Benefit Actually Covers

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The average Social Security check covers only a fraction of what seniors actually spend each year.

Most retirees lean on Social Security for much of their income, yet the average check was built to replace only part of a paycheck. As of September 2026, the average retired-worker benefit is $2,088.79 per month, according to the Social Security Administration (SSA) Monthly Statistical Snapshot. That adds up to $25,065.48 per year.

A household headed by someone 65 or older spends $61,432 per year, based on the BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey from 2024, the most recent data available. While housing and healthcare are major concerns, transportation and food costs also represent significant portions of a typical senior's budget.

The average annual benefit of $25,065.48 covers only 41% of that annual senior-household spending. This is by design, as the government built the program to replace roughly 40% of pre-retirement income. It's not intended to fund a complete lifestyle on its own.

The SSA calculates your benefit based on your highest 35 years of earnings, where earlier wages are adjusted for inflation, through the primary insurance amount (PIA) formula. This leaves an annual shortfall of $36,366.52 that you'll need to cover through other sources.

For retirees without a pension or deep savings, part-time work may be the only realistic way to fill that hole. Inflation adjustments provide some relief, but they're meant to maintain current buying power rather than increase it.

A 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) will take effect in 2027, adding about $75 a month to that average check. This adjustment is designed to help your benefits keep pace with rising prices. The average check is a foundation, not a full budget, and the question is how to cover the rest.

It's possible to beat the average Social Security check by delaying your Social Security application. Every month you wait to apply increases your monthly benefits until you qualify for your largest checks at 70.

If you expect to have an average or above-average life expectancy and you can afford to cover your living costs another way in the meantime, delaying your Social Security application is a viable way to grow your checks. It could also lead to a much larger lifetime benefit.

Things like remaining in the workforce for longer or increasing your income today could also lead to larger Social Security checks in the future because the SSA bases your benefit on your average monthly income over your 35 highest-earning years. If you're earning more now than in years past, working a bit longer could boost your future benefits.

But even in the best-case scenario, your checks likely won't be enough to cover all your living expenses in retirement. Make sure you have a plan for how you'll cover what Social Security doesn't before you sign up.

TL;DR: Zcash’s NU7 upgrade activated at mainnet block 3,543,405, reducing target block spacing from 75 to 25 seconds. It also changes how fees contribute to future network security. Faster target blocks do not make every payment final instantly, and node operators need compatible software.

Zcash has crossed an important protocol milestone. The NU7 network upgrade activated in early November at block height 3,543,405, bringing a faster target block interval and changes to the economics of future block rewards. The Zcash Foundation’s Zebra 7 release notes identify the same mainnet activation height and describe the consensus changes required for nodes to follow the chain.

The upgrade is not just a software patch. It changes how Zcash produces blocks and how some transaction fees are handled, while preserving the wider coin-issuance framework.

Zcash’s new target is a block every 25 seconds, down from 75 seconds before the upgrade. In theory, that means users and services waiting for several confirmations can receive them sooner. But target spacing is a statistical expectation, not a service-level guarantee: real blocks arrive irregularly, and exchanges or payment processors choose their own confirmation policies.

To prevent a tripling of the number of blocks from mechanically tripling new coin issuance, the upgrade scales the scheduled reward per block and adjusts remaining issuance and halving intervals. The idea is to deliver more frequent blocks while keeping the rate of monetary issuance broadly aligned with the previous schedule.

The node-software detail matters as much as the headline speed. Zebra’s release notes say operators must upgrade to a NU7-compatible client to remain on the consensus chain after the activation point. A user may experience difficulties if a wallet or service depends on an outdated node even when the network itself is operating normally.

NU7 also introduces a Network Sustainability Mechanism that directs a portion of transaction fees into a public reserve for future reissuance to miners. Accounts of the upgrade put that reserve contribution at 60% of fees, with the remaining 40% available to miners at the time of collection. The stated design goal is to smooth the eventual decline in subsidy-funded security spending as the network matures.

That is a policy choice within a public cryptocurrency, not free revenue. Money reserved for future rewards is not available in the same way as fees paid immediately to today’s miners. Evaluating the mechanism means examining incentives over time, its interaction with market demand and whether it achieves the intended security effects.

The change also retires support for older version-four transactions, affecting Zcash’s original Sprout pool. This is a reminder that long-running blockchains can carry technical baggage that becomes increasingly difficult to maintain, especially when privacy features and proof systems evolve.

Zcash offers privacy-preserving transfers, so network improvements should also be assessed against wallet compatibility, scanning performance and the accessibility of shielded payments. The upgrade can improve the underlying capability without ensuring every exchange, mobile wallet or merchant adopts the same features immediately.

NewsBTC previously examined the network’s Zebra synchronisation work ahead of NU7, when the emphasis was on nodes following the chain reliably. Activation does not eliminate those operational responsibilities.

For users and operators, the practical next checks are node versions, observed block times, confirmation requirements and whether particular wallets have published NU7 support notices. The verified milestone is a mainnet rule change; its day-to-day value will emerge through reliability and actual private-payment usage.

‘Club Kid’ Scene-Stealer Kirby Howell-Baptiste on Playing a Drugged-Up Party Girl Who Turns Jordan Firstman’s World Upside Down: She’s ‘A Complete Tornado’

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

It’s a line said, in various forms, numerous times across the first half of “Club Kid,” Jordan Firstman‘s hedonistic and heartfelt coming-of-age comedy drama about a hard-partying New York club promoter suddenly forced to grapple with life and responsibilities. But the most hilariously-timed utterance comes in the middle of a crucial scene — one that shifts the entire movie — and from and by a minor, yet unforgettably wild, character. “She’s a massive party girl — she never sleeps, she only parties,” says Brit actor Kirby Howell-Baptiste of how Edison was described to her. “She’s completely lost all social boundaries. The lifetime of drugs melted half of her brain away, and all her social cues and etiquette have gone.”

Howell-Baptiste’s etiquette-free Edison is only in “Club Kid” for a few minutes, but manages to steal the crucial scene in question from under the nose of writer, director and lead star Firstman.

Roughly quarter of the way into the film, she rocks up unannounced and chaotic, sporting green braids and oversized sunglasses, at the apartment lived in by Firstman’s nightclub promoter Peter (Firstman), with 10-year-old Arlo (Reggie Absolom) in tow. While wandering around the place like it’s her own, she reveals — in a hysterically matter-of-fact manner — that not only is Arlo his, but she’s brought the kid across from the U.K. to live with him permanently. Boom.

But Peter is gay — and confidently declares that he’s never slept with a woman. Not true, Edison asserts as she casually roams the kitchen on the hunt for literally anything remotely looking like drugs she can swallow or snort. He had sex with her friend Leonine a decade earlier at one of his nights while so high on ketamine, cocaine, GHB and (probably) more that he didn’t have a clue what was going on.

“Nah, you fucked her, babes,” Edison says. “Point is, that’s your son.”

Tragically, Leonine — as her apparent friend explains while sniffing some white powder she found on the counter — struggled with mental health issues (“She was in and out of the loony bin… what do you call it, rehab?”) and recently ended her life. “Last week she goes and tops herself — she fucking killed herself, babe.”

In the midst of making these life-changing and upsetting revelations in the most wonderfully insensitive manner possible, Edison inquires if Peter and her should “do a bump” of coke (slightly shaken by how his life is quickly unravelling, he declines).

“[Edison] is just a complete tornado,” says Londoner Howell-Baptiste, adding that she thinks the character is an “amalgamation of a few party people” Firstman has encountered. “But what I really love is that, although she’s absolutely bananas, she does feel quite real and quite relatable. I think everyone knows that person, to some degree. At some point in your life, you have met that person.”

Edison isn’t seen again in “Club Kid” after the apartment scene, in which she eventually leaves Arlo in Peter’s care and — predictably — fails to return to collect him as promised later on. But as Peter begins a frantic search to find her across various New York house parties, further levels of her comically debauched behaviour come to light, as he hears how she was kicked out of several places for simply being too much of a tornado to handle.

“I wasn’t part of those scenes, obviously, but what was really cool was that you got more of a picture of Edison,” she says. “Even in her absence, you got more of a picture of what she was doing and how she was just belligerently running around New York, hoovering up drugs at everyone’s party.”

Howell-Baptiste admits she had no idea who Firstman was when she was originally invited to audition for “Club Kid” in mid 2025. As someone who’s “not really on social media much” she missed his breakout during the COVID-19 pandemic. But she checked out some of his Instagram videos and thought he was funny. Crucially, the casting was being done by Carmen Cuba, who she says has “really exquisite taste” and thought, while she hadn’t seen the full script, the role seemed fun — “and the character was a party girl and I felt like I could channel that pretty well.”

A few months later, shooting in New York, Howell-Baptiste still didn’t have a full picture of what “Club Kid” was all about (she admits to only watching it in September), but her main takeaway was that Edison was written by someone “who knows how to party.”

While the scene they shot was “almost verbatim” from the audition, there was a bit of ad-libbing, with Howell-Baptiste being the one who added the British slang term “tops herself.”

“And afterwards, everyone was like, ‘What do you you mean ‘”tops herself”?’ Because, obviously, we’re in a film that’s written by a gay man. There’s so much about it that celebrates queer culture, and topping has a completely different meaning there,” she says. Despite offering to remove the word, she was told to keep it in. “It made the cut — and it think it’s really fun and adds even more confusion when it comes to American audiences being like: ‘What is going on with this girl?'”

In terms of notes from Firstman, she says the main discussion was how he wanted Edison’s voice to sound, with the request that — alongside the British accent — she was “very hoarse, like she’s been up all night and almost always lost her voice.”

To achieve this, Howell-Baptiste channelled former Spice Girl Mel B. “She always had this slight hoarseness singing that I always thought was very cool, so that’s where my mind went,” she says.

Having shot her key scene (and the even shorter scene in which Edison and Leonine first meet Peter in the club) in the fall of 2025, Howell-Baptiste — whose long line of credits include “Killing Eve,” “Barry” and “Hacks” — moved on to other acting jobs. Several months later, while filming Season 3 of British TV drama “Sherwood,” Firstman messaged her to tell her that “Club Kid” had been accepted into Cannes. “I was like, ‘How incredible,’ but that was sort of it for me for a very long time.”

Of course, “Club Kid” wound up being the talk of the festival festival, the breakout hit that sparked an intense bidding war eventually won by A24 for the princely sum of $17 million. Howell-Baptiste was oblivious to it all (“I joke that I live under a rock”) until her manager messaged her saying “Are you aware of everything that’s going on with ‘Club Kid’ in Cannes?” Keeping with the theme, it was a makeup artist who later informed her that “Club Kid” was going to Toronto. “I just wasn’t aware of any of the buzz!”

Thankfully, Howell-Baptiste was able to attend the premiere at the BFI London Film Festival and took to the stage at the Royal Festival Hall with Firstman and Absolom, staying to watch the film to experience the laughs, cheers and tears from a hugely enthusiastic audience. Now, she can finally enjoy having a crucial — and spectacularly funny — role in one of the standout movies of the year.

“I filmed in a complete vacuum — I just did one scene with three other characters and filmed the party scene, which was just one night,” she says. “So it’s all come as a really pleasant surprise to me — it’s lovely.”

What discs can a PlayStation 5 play?

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Some of your old games and other media work on your PS5, turning it into a proper media hub.

With Sony's plans to stop making disc-based games come 2028, physical media enthusiasts may want to start stocking up soon. While some PlayStation 5 owners already veered in this direction by purchasing the disc-free console variant, many traditionalists can't stand the thought of this transition. While no new games released in 2028 and beyond will get physical copies, existing discs will still continue to work.

We're over a year away from this shift — one that could very well play into what the PlayStation 6 ends up being. In the meantime, if you have a stack of Blu-ray movies, your go-to show on DVD or some last-gen games sitting around, they should all work on the PS5. Let's go through which discs are compatible with Sony's current console.

Remember that if you own the Slim digital PS5 or a PS5 Pro, you can buy the detachable PS5 disc drive for $79. This isn't compatible with the original Digital Edition model.

Of course, all PS5 game discs will continue to work on the console, as will your old PS4 physical games. Barring a few titles, the PS5 is backward compatible with the "overwhelming majority" of PS4 titles, whether on disc or digital. From Bloodborne to Red Dead Redemption 2, plenty of iconic PS4 releases haven't received the next-gen treatment, but you're still able to enjoy them on the newer system.

Even better, the console's Game Boost tech delivers smoother frame rates for certain PS5 games including God of War, The Last of Us Part II and Days Gone. While this feature doesn't apply to every game, many of the ones that received patches upped performance to 60 fps on PS5 — an improvement from how those titles ran on a standard PS4.

According to Sony's PS5 backward compatibility page, a total of six PS4 games aren't playable. Apologies to fans of Hitman Go: Definitive Edition and some other obscure titles like We Sing or Just Deal With It! The company notes that other PS4 releases like demos and media apps might not be compatible, either.

Unfortunately, the PS5's backward compatibility doesn't go any further; the console won't play physical PS1, PS2 or PS3 games. Plenty of legendary titles from older generations are available to play digitally from the PlayStation Store, though many beloved games like LittleBigPlanet and Shadow Hearts: Covenant remain abandoned on their original consoles.

Many of the best streaming services like Netflix and Disney+ offer apps on PS5. If you're planning to treat the console like an all-in-one media hub, you'll be glad to know the PlayStation 5 supports both Ultra HD Blu-ray and standard Blu-ray discs, as well as DVDs. That means all those old movies and TV box sets you bought years ago still have some life in them. However, 3D Blu-rays are a no-go.

Home-burned video discs may work if you properly finalize them, but there are no guarantees. You can't drop raw video files onto a disc and play them on the PS5. The PS5 can't play audio CDs, which matches is predecessor (though the PS3 had this capability). If you want to listen to music while playing, Apple Music and Spotify offer apps for the console.

If this is something you want, a better option is connecting a USB drive with video or audio files, following Sony's instructions for folder naming and file types. That's a good workaround for files you'd otherwise play from homemade discs or CDs.

Finally, while it's probably obvious, Sony asserts that non-circular discs shaped like a "card, star or heart" will be rejected. Don't stick a small GameCube disc into the PS5, either.

French Cinema Is Thriving at Festivals but Facing an Uphill Battle in U.S. Theaters as Distributors Navigate a Shrinking Market

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

French cinema may be enjoying a golden run on the festival circuit and in the awards race, but getting those films into U.S. theaters, and persuading American audiences to buy tickets, has become an increasingly uphill battle.

That paradox was at the heart of a lively discussion at the American French Film Festival on Thursday, held in partnership with the CNC and Variety, where three veteran U.S. distributors painted a sobering picture of a market squeezed by shrinking theatrical opportunities. Related Stories CAA's Roeg Sutherland Says It's Too Soon to Predict Fallout From Netflix Firings, Thinks Recent Mergers Represent 'Opportunity' for Industry Why Netflix Fired Dan Lin

The panel brought together Robert Aronson, executive VP of Cohen Media Group; John Gerrans, co-president of Strand Releasing; and Paul Hudson, CEO of Outsider Pictures, who have collectively brought hundreds of international films to American audiences.

The challenges extend well beyond French cinema. With fewer distributors acquiring independent films and arthouse theaters increasingly turning to mainstream Hollywood fare, even critically acclaimed movies are struggling to secure meaningful theatrical runs.

The definition of a French-language box office hit has also changed dramatically along with the health of the special market for most foreign-language films. Jean-Pierre Jeunet’s cult film “Amélie” grossed approximately $33 million domestically a quarter-century ago. By comparison, Justine Triet’s Palme d’Or winner “Anatomy of a Fall,” one of the most successful French films of the last five years which was handled by Neon and won an Oscar, earned around $5 million in the U.S..

The most accessible French blockbusters aren’t necessarily easier to market in the U.S. Samuel Goldwyn, whose company released Pathé’s two-part “The Three Musketeers” and “The Count of Monte Cristo,” and recently acquired Antonin Baudry’s historical epic saga “De Gaulle,” told Variety in a separate interview following the panel that these films have far broader commercial potential than their limited theatrical exposure suggests.

“The only thing that makes it an arthouse movie is because it’s in a foreign language,” Goldwyn said of “De Gaulle,” adding that it could easily play on 1,000 screens if it were in English. He’s still managed to roll out the first opus of the WWII saga into theaters this weekend across key U.S. markets simultaneously with transactional VOD.

Yet while the pool of U.S. buyers has narrowed, competition remains fierce for the most coveted titles. Deep-pocketed players including Neon, Netflix, Mubi, A24 and Sony Pictures Classics continue to chase high-profile international films, particularly those from established auteurs. And even though Sony Pictures Classics, historically one of the most prolific U.S. buyers of French cinema, has become more selective, it’s still on the lookout for certain titles. The company just bought “Violette,” Jeunet’s latest film, and Louis Clichy’s animated feature “Iron Boy,” which won a prize at Cannes’ Un Certain Regard and was shortlisted as France’s Oscar submission alongside “De Gaulle.” The distributor also scored a solid theatrical run with Rebecca Zlotowski’s “A Private Life,” starring Jodie Foster, earlier this year.

But for smaller distributors, competing for films with the greatest commercial potential is often a losing game.

“We all see the same thing. So usually you go, boy, I could sell that. There’s something in here that I know I can sell. It’s got great reviews. It was in competition in Cannes or Berlin. And then Netflix buys it,” says Gerrans.

In any case, securing screens can prove even harder than winning a bidding war. Arthouse theaters that once championed foreign-language cinema are increasingly booking Hollywood releases, while others have shuttered altogether.

“We have to struggle to get into those theaters that used to be” devoted to arthouse films, Aronson said. Cohen Media Group owns the Landmark Theatres circuit, but that doesn’t guarantee bookings for its international releases. “They keep their own books and they have their own agenda and they need to be profitable,” said Aronson, whose recent acquisitions include László Nemes’ Cannes competition period drama “Moulin” and Géraldine Nakache’s “Think Good,” which played in Un Certain Regard.

The company has also picked up Marc Fitoussi’s “All About Corinne,” starring Isabelle Huppert as a struggling film extra who dreams of becoming an actress and befriends a movie star played by Sandrine Kiberlain. Aronson, who compared the comedy’s tone to the French hit series “Call My Agent” (which was partly directed by Fitoussi), said star power remains a crucial selling point.

Strand has acquired Ulrike Ottinger’s experimental German-language vampire movie “The Blood Countess,” also starring Huppert, and Gerrans said her involvement has made a significant difference.

“We’re able to book it because of her, and we’re getting a lot of festivals because of her,” Gerrans said. “So that one’s sort of booking itself, and that doesn’t happen too often.”

For less recognizable titles, the outlook is considerably tougher. Gerrans admitted that he sometimes consults exhibitors before committing to acquisitions, particularly when sales agents require a theatrical release.

“A lot of times we hold off and we ask some of the exhibitors, if I acquire this, will you play this film?” said Gerrans, whose upcoming French releases include “Flesh and Fuel” and “Daughters of Abraham.”

He recalled pitching a film he loved to a New York exhibitor, only to be turned down. “It’s a fantastic film. You’ll love it. I think the audience will love it. They just go, that storyline seems a little too small.”

Hudson, whose recent acquisitions include Cannes Un Certain Regard title “Congo Boy,” which earned Bradley Fiomona Dembeasset the best actor prize, described a similarly brutal scramble for screens.

“We’re all trying to get the IFC Film Center or the Quad or the Angelika to book the film in New York, and there’s, I don’t know, 45 of us with all eight films trying to get them into these” theaters, Hudson joked.

With major New York arthouse venues often booked months in advance, distributors sometimes resort to renting screens just to secure a theatrical launch.

And competition isn’t coming only from Hollywood blockbusters. Repertory screenings, television series playing in multiplexes and special events are also taking up valuable screen space.

“I understand arthouse theaters playing reissues,” Hudson said. “And young audiences rediscovering ‘Raise the Red Lantern.’ Amazing. But what about the new films? What about the new filmmakers? Where are the new ones going to get space to find room in the marketplace with all of this other stuff?”

The squeeze extends beyond theaters. With DVD revenues largely gone, TV sales collapsing and streaming licenses losing value, independent distributors have fewer ways to recoup their investments.

“We have a really hard time getting our films on the streaming platforms,” Aronson said. “Things have changed a lot in that market because they’re not that interested anymore in kind of single U.S.-only, after-theatrical streaming deals with independent distributors.”

Hudson was particularly scathing about Amazon Prime’s declining royalty rates. “They were paying 10 cents per hour viewed, which is awful. But then they cut the rate down to 1 cent per hour viewed. Then they cut the rate down to half a cent per hour viewed.”

He said he and other independent distributors have pulled titles from Amazon Prime and are looking to alternative outlets including Tubi, Roku, Pluto and YouTube.

Strand operates its own subscription streaming channel through Amazon, but Gerrans said “they could decide tomorrow to turn it off.”

With traditional distribution avenues under pressure, festivals are becoming an increasingly important way for films to reach audiences.

“Film festivals are kind of like another tier of distribution, another level of distribution,” Aronson said. “It’s a way most independent films get seen by anyone.”

Distributors are also looking to younger moviegoers, particularly through Letterboxd, which has built a passionate community of Gen Z cinephiles. But reaching that demographic without substantial marketing budgets remains a challenge.

“Letterboxd is a real opportunity for us to find a younger audience,” Hudson said, describing how a friend’s son has helped him navigate the platform and build a community around his films.

Yet enthusiasm for cinema doesn’t necessarily translate into ticket sales for contemporary French films.

“I can’t even get my teenage kids to go see my movies,” Gerrans said, citing Strand’s release of Agathe Riedinger’s “Wild Diamond,” about a 17-year-old aspiring reality television star. Despite a premise seemingly tailored to younger audiences, the film failed to take off.

Still, distributors are experimenting with ways to build word of mouth around films that might otherwise struggle to find an audience.

Gerrans is optimistic about “Flesh and Fuel,” which he described as “the gay trucker movie you’ve never seen before,” betting that its uplifting ending will resonate with moviegoers.

Hudson, meanwhile, plans an extended theatrical rollout for “Congo Boy,” stretching from Black History Month through Juneteenth, even if bookings remain scarce.

“I can do five months because nobody’s clamoring for this movie to go onto a subscription service. But I may only get seven bookings.”

French institutions including the CNC, Unifrance and Villa Albertine are also helping distributors promote French cinema through festivals, promotional events and talent travel.

“All that helps to generate awareness,” Hudson said. “We have no support from the government. I mean, everything we do is from our own pockets. Film’s commerce here, it’s not culture.”

The discussion was one of several panels bringing together French and American industry figures at the American French Film Festival, which is celebrating its 30th anniversary this year with a lineup of 60 films and series. The festival’s industry program also featured American filmmaker Randal Kleiser and French director Pierre Salvadori discussing artificial intelligence, as well as a panel moderated by Writers Guild of America West member Yvette Foy with French screenwriters Coralie Amedeo, Fanny Burdino, Abdel Raouf Dafri and Thomas Finkielkraut. The French delegation also included directors Baudry (“De Gaulle”), Laïla Marrakchi (“Strawberries”), Fitoussi (“All About Corinne”), Lukas Dhont (“Coward”) and actor-director Raphaël Quenard (“Le rêve américain”), among others.

Popular Dairy Alternatives Could Be Leaving You Short of Essential Nutrients

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Replacing even a modest amount of dairy with plant-based alternatives could significantly reduce essential nutrient intake, according to a new study from the University of Surrey. Researchers found that substituting just 20% to 35% of dairy products increased the estimated prevalence of inadequate iodine intake among adolescent girls from 54% to 65%. Among women ages 19 to 49, the figure rose from 32% to 42%.

The study also identified reductions in calcium, riboflavin, and vitamin B12 intake. However, researchers found that fortifying plant-based alternatives and other foods could help prevent these nutritional shortfalls.

In the first UK study of its kind, researchers from the University of Surrey and Queen’s University Belfast analyzed dietary data from more than 3,500 people, ages 18 months to 100 years. Using the National Diet and Nutrition Survey, they modeled how replacing milk, yogurt, and cheese with commercially available plant-based alternatives would affect intake of four essential micronutrients.

Dr Katie Nicol, a research fellow at the University of Surrey and co-author of the study, explained:

“With the growing need to address environmental sustainability, many dietary recommendations are focused on plant-based diets and the reduced intake of animal products.”

“With the rise in plant-based alternative foods, we need to know whether they are like-for-like replacements for the micronutrients that are found in dairy products. This is particularly important for vulnerable groups, including young children and adolescent females, who are more likely to be deficient in certain micronutrients, which could affect their overall health.”

Iodine was the nutrient most affected by dairy replacement. When researchers modeled completely replacing dairy with plant-based alternatives under current fortification levels, the estimated prevalence of inadequate iodine intake reached 80% among adolescent girls and 72% among adolescent boys. These figures reflect modeled dietary inadequacy, not confirmed clinical deficiencies.

Iodine is essential for producing thyroid hormones, which regulate metabolism and support growth and brain development. It is particularly important before and during pregnancy, when iodine requirements increase.

Dr Sarah Bath, associate professor at the University of Surrey and lead author, said:

“The UK is particularly vulnerable to iodine insufficiency due to a lack of an iodine fortification policy such as universal salt iodization, which many other countries have.”

“People may also be unaware of the importance of iodine, which is needed to make thyroid hormones to regulate our metabolism, support healthy growth, and brain development. Iodine is particularly important during pregnancy, when the requirement increases, but it is also essential prior to pregnancy to ensure that stores are optimized. People are also unaware that dairy products are a major source of iodine, particularly in the UK, and that plant-based alternatives, unless fortified, have a very low iodine content.”

The researchers found that mandatory fortification of plant-based dairy alternatives could prevent declines in micronutrient intake. However, even with fortified alternatives, an estimated 59% of adolescent girls would still have inadequate iodine intake. Adding iodine to bread reduced that figure to 37%, suggesting that combining both fortification strategies could be more effective.

Dr Nicol emphasized the importance of aligning nutrition policies with environmental goals:

“Nutrition policy must evolve in tandem with environmental targets; however, it is important that shifts towards more sustainable diets do not compromise on public health. Fortification of foods is one way that this could be achieved; however, government policy is needed to make this happen.”

Dr Anne Nugent, from Queen’s University Belfast, described how the team identified dairy-containing foods in the UK National Diet and Nutrition Survey and modeled replacing them with equal amounts of plant-based alternatives. The researchers accounted for differences in the nutritional content of available products and tested whether fortification could prevent deficiencies without causing excessive nutrient intake.

“During the analysis, we first identified all foods and recipes containing dairy produce (milk, cheese, and yogurt) in the national food survey for the UK, the National Diet and Nutrition Survey. Next, we conducted a series of replacement scenarios whereby we substituted the dairy present with an equal weight of plant-based dairy alternatives. As the nutrient composition of dairy alternatives available on our supermarket shelves can vary, we also applied a condition to make sure that every dairy alternative we had identified in our previous market survey had an equal chance of selection. The last step was to examine the impact on dietary intakes of key nutrients following either the reduction or removal of dairy from the diet, replacing it with the dairy alternatives as above, and asking whether a series of fortification options could mitigate against any shortfalls in intake while avoid situations of excess.”

Reference: “The impact of replacing dairy products with plant-based dairy alternatives on iodine and other micronutrients: a modelling study and evaluation of policy options to support population intake” by Katie Nicol, Anne P Nugent, Jayne V Woodside, Kathryn H Hart and Sarah C Bath, , The Lancet Planetary Health. By now, basketball fans are surely aware that Ernie Johnson is leaving Inside the NBA after the 2026-2027 NBA season. Johnson made the announcement at the end of October, and fans and celebrities alike have since been honoring him. One of the most emotional tributes, though, came from Johnson’s longtime co-hosts, Charles Barkley, who spoke about the news during an episode of the talk show. I was already in my feelings over the on-air tribute but, now, that’s amped up a bit since I’ve heard Johnson’s first-hand account.

Johnson initially revealed his news in a social media video that eventually went viral on October 28, and he spoke about it again at the top of Inside’s broadcast that evening. Following that opening, Johnson teed up the show, as per usual, by introducing co-hosts Barkley, Kenny Smith and Shaquille O’Neal. When Johnson threw it to Barkley, however, he was misty-eyed and momentarily at a loss for words. Johnson admitted on The Dan LeBatard Show (as seen on YouTube) that he was surprised, saying:

I was concerned, at first, because the camera was on him and he wasn't saying anything, and he had to kind of — like he was struggling and I was like, 'Oh, what's going on here?' The moments don't get much better than that for me when I think about the last few days. Because look, I've said it before, you guys have heard me, we love each other. We've been together for so long. We would do anything for one another.

That night, when the camera was on him, the usually humorous Barkley took a few seconds to compose himself. He eventually recovered, simply saying, “That was tough,” in a low and solemn voice. “Sir Charles” then proceeded to give a truly warm tribute to Johnson, which beautifully summed up the 26 years they’ve worked together. Check out Barkley’s tribute, and those of Smith and O’Neal by watching the video below:

Months ago, Charles Barkley claimed he would retire after this season and, in the process of doing so, he let slip that Johnson was doing the same. Barkley voiced his desire for both him and his co-host to step away from the show together. Johnson didn’t immediately confirm his plans for weeks, leaving much uncertainty. In the video, Johnson explained that he wanted to step away from the show as a way of slowing down a bit in order to spend some time with his family and focus on other ventures. Johnson also said this of his on-air tribute:

It was emotional for me. I mean, I was holding it together all day long and it was like, ‘Oh, man. I'm watching Chuck struggle with this.’ But that just speaks to the relationship we all have.

Charles Barkley Shares More Thoughts On Retiring From Inside The NBA, And I’m Starting To Believe He’ll Do It This Time

Johnson began hosting Inside back in the fall of 1990 and, under his leadership, the show became a must-watch program for basketball devotees like myself. The “Godfather” has been present for the series’ biggest moments, including its move from TNT to ESPN last season. Considering the veteran broadcaster’s long tenure with the program, the notion of him no longer hosting is somewhat hard to think about. What’s more is that there’s still the question of whether Barkley will truly retire (this time) as he declared.

Whatever the case, ESPN is reportedly committed to Inside the NBA regardless of Ernie Johnson and Charles Barkley’s plans. I’m not sure I’m ready to think about the prospect of the show being with two of its key members. For now, though, I’m just relishing this final season with the core four and hoping it produces even more memorable moments like Barkley’s sentimental tribute.

One of Jon Bernthal's Best Performances Takes Less Than 10 Episodes To Binge

📅 Nov 07, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Editor's note: The below article includes discussion of suicide.

Jon Bernthal has worked steadily in Hollywood for decades now. His versatility means that he's taken on projects as diverse as The Walking Dead, American Gigolo, and His & Hers. He brought The Punisher to life in multiple projects, appeared on the big screen in this summer's massive hit, The Odyssey, and won over audiences with his Broadway debut in Dog Day Afternoon. But there's one more project that Bernthal absolutely shines in, and it only takes nine episodes to binge.

The Bear premiered on Hulu in 2022 and quickly earned dedicated fans and rave reviews. The comedy series (although some people are still arguing whether it should have been classified as a drama) tells the story of a complex chef named Carmen "Carmy" Berzatto (Jeremy Allen White). Carmy has been trying to make it as a professional chef in the fine dining world, but decides to return to his hometown of Chicago after the death of his older brother, Mikey (Bernthal).

Carmy takes over the family business and, over five seasons, transforms it from the Original Beef sandwich shop into a fancy restaurant called The Bear. A heartwrenching yet beautiful portrayal of a dysfunctional family and the far-reaching effects of trauma and addiction, The Bear became a pop culture favorite as well as a critical darling, with 22 Emmy wins throughout its run.

Because Mikey has already died by suicide before the first episode takes place, The Bear introduces his character through flashbacks that emphasize him as a massive presence that casts a shadow over the actions of many characters. When Carmy's big brother is first introduced in Season 1's "Ceres," he seems joyful, boisterous, and funny, and his strong relationships with his siblings and his "cousin" Richie (Ebon Moss-Bachrach) are obvious. But the trauma behind Mikey and his addiction is much more prevalent in Season 2's "Fishes," which spotlights the many toxic qualities of the Berzatto family at Christmastime. Mikey is explosive and off-balance, evidence of the emerging mental health struggles that lead to his untimely death. Season 3 delivers a glimpse at Mikey's kinder, more charming facets when he hires Tina (Liza Colón-Zayas) to work at The Beef, while the "Gary" prequel episode released between Seasons 4 and 5 follows Richie and Mikey on a misguided errand. The alternating sides of his personality are evident — fun-loving and adventurous one minute, belligerent and angry the next.

Some actors require a lengthy amount of time to develop their characters, yet in a short amount of screen time, Bernthal forms a fully three-dimensional individual whom viewers innately understand. While Mikey is only meant to be a supporting presence, Bernthal's grounded, authentic performance highlights why so much of the story is centered around the aftermath of his character's death. In the hands of a lesser actor, Mikey would have been a footnote to the series, but thanks to Bernthal's portrayal, he becomes the heart of The Bear.

Bernthal won a much-deserved Emmy for his work in The Bear Season 2 and was later nominated two more times. Although he only appears about once per season, his episodes serve as proof of his immense talent. The Bear has come to a close, with the final season wrapping up this past June, but it's still worth a binge to catch every awe-inspiring minute of Bernthal's performance.
