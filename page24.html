Coupang vs. Home Depot: Which Consumer Stock Is a Better Buy for 2027?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's Coupang's growth potential versus Home Depot's stability and size.

By Lawrence Rothman, CFA – Updated Nov 25, 2026 at 8:36AM EST

Choosing between growth and stability often defines a portfolio strategy. Investors today are weighing the rapid expansion of Coupang (CPNG +8.62%) against the established market dominance and consistent profitability of Home Depot (HD +2.21%).

Coupang is reshaping commerce in South Korea through its proprietary delivery infrastructure and diverse digital services. Home Depot remains the premier destination for home renovation in North America, increasingly leveraging specialized acquisitions to better serve professional builders and contractors.

Coupang operates as a multifaceted technology and commerce platform, providing everything from retail and restaurant delivery to video streaming and fintech services. The company primarily serves consumers in South Korea and Taiwan, utilizing its "WOW" membership program to build deep loyalty. Its recent acquisition of Farfetch has expanded its reach into the global luxury goods market by connecting the company to high-end boutiques and suppliers.

In the 12 months ended Sept. 30, revenue reached nearly $36.2 billion, representing revenue growth of roughly 7.4% compared to the prior 12 months. Despite the top-line increase, the company reported a net loss of $1.4 billion for the period, reflecting its ongoing investments in expansion and international markets.

Home Depot serves a massive market of do-it-yourself consumers and professional contractors among retail stocks. The company has aggressively pursued the "Pro" segment through acquisitions, including SRS, a specialty distributor for roofing and pool contractors. This strategy deepens its integration into the professional building supply chain, moving beyond traditional retail aisles to provide specialized interior products and installation services.

In the 12 months ended Nov. 1, revenue reached $171.2 billion, supported by revenue growth of 3%. Net income for this period was $14.3 billion. The company has maintained steady profitability even as it navigates fluctuations in the broader housing market and consumer spending patterns.

Coupang faces significant risks related to data security, including a 2025 incident involving unauthorized access to 33 million customer accounts. The company is also under intense scrutiny from the Korean government regarding its membership bundling and search algorithm practices. Competition is fierce in the e-commerce sector from rivals like Amazon (AMZN -1.21%), and the company's dual-class stock structure keeps control concentrated in the hands of its founder.

Home Depot is the subject of a class action lawsuit regarding the alleged use of AI-powered license plate readers in its parking lots. Macroeconomic factors, particularly high interest rates and low turnover in the housing market, could dampen demand for home improvement products.

Home Depot appears more attractively priced on an earnings basis, while Coupang offers a lower multiple relative to its total sales volume.

Valuation metrics include sourcing from Financial Modeling Prep (FMP) and may differ from other data providers.

The Forward P/E is a ratio that compares the stock price to future earnings estimates. The P/S ratio measures the market's value against sales over the past twelve months, calculated as market cap divided by trailing twelve-month sales.

It's been a challenging year for Coupang, with a fire at a distribution center and a data breach. Investors certainly haven't been pleased, with the share price down 32.1% this year through Nov. 24. That compares to the S&P 500 index's 5.2% gain.

The company's third-quarter revenue growth accelerated from the prior two quarters. Coupang's top line increased 11% year over year, after removing foreign-currency translations. Still, the company lost money.

Home Depot's stock has performed better lately. This year, the share price rose 2.8%.

Despite a challenging housing market and homeowners holding off on major projects, Home Depot continues to post sales gains. Fiscal third-quarter same-store sales (comps) grew 2.2%. The period ended on Nov. 1.

With Coupang facing intense competition in global markets, it may find expansion opportunities limited. Meanwhile, Home Depot has the largest share of the home-improvement retail market and continues to perform well, despite a challenging overall economic environment.

Hence, between the two, I'd choose to buy Home Depot's stock.

Real Estate Tycoon Mauricio Umansky Reveals What Luxury Buyers Want Most—From Secluded Estates to Private Spas

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Mauricio Umansky has a read on what makes a multimillion-dollar home irresistible.

For some of his buyers, it’s enough acreage to put real distance between them and the neighbors. For others, it’s the luxury of stepping into a sauna or taking a cold plunge without leaving home.

“Privacy is becoming a bigger priority for luxury buyers,” The Agency founder revealed while speaking to Mansion Global.

He said buyers are drawn to properties where they can retreat from view, feel secure and build a wellness routine around amenities such as a private gym or spa.

Umansky is putting his read on the market to work in Miami’s Edgewater neighborhood, where he is leading sales for HQ Residences Miami.

The project brings together singer Marc Anthony, hospitality entrepreneur Sam Nazarian and SBE. Its pitch borrows from the hotel world, pairing private residences with wellness amenities, places for neighbors to gather and an emphasis on service.

Even at the top of the market, buyers are watching what they pay. Umansky said there’s room to negotiate in some areas, while a well-priced home in a coveted location can still draw strong interest.

He recommends finding an agent who knows the neighborhood and being ready to act when a property comes along that you can see yourself living in for years.

Umansky’s own homes span Los Angeles, La Quinta, California, and Aspen, Colorado.

Los Angeles gives him access to city life, while La Quinta is where he sees the appeal of a slower pace, sunny days and a round of golf. In Aspen, the draw extends from ski season to summers in the mountains and the sense of community he finds there.

If he were to add another address, Umansky said he could picture himself in Europe.

He singled out the South of France, Italy and Spain, with particular affection for Madrid. Miami also appeals to him as a place to own a home, especially after spending more time in the city through his work on HQ Residences.

The broker also reflected on what television has done for his business. Appearances on “Buying Beverly Hills,” “Dancing With the Stars” and “Special Forces” have introduced him to people he might never have met through real estate alone, he said, and brought more attention to The Agency.

The trade-off is that some people form an impression of him from what they see on camera. He said he wants his reputation to rest on his work as an agent and the trust he earns from clients.

As for the prospect of running for mayor, Umansky spoke about what he would focus on if he pursued the job.

“Los Angeles is my home, and I care deeply about its future,” he told Mansion Global. He said he’d look at how the city handles housing approvals and development, with the goal of making it easier to build and attracting more investment and jobs.

The candid glimpse into what wealthy buyers are looking for comes one week after his stepdaughter and, Kyle Richards' daughter, Farrah Aldjufrie, gave birth to a baby boy—three months after revealing the identity of her unborn child's father as none other than her "Buying Beverly Hills" co-star and The Agency colleague, Zach Goldsmith.

The 37-year-old property mogul, who is the daughter of Richards and her ex-husband, Guraish Aldjufrie, shared the news in July that she was expecting her first child.

Then, one month later, she hard launched her relationship with Goldsmith while sharing a video of the two of them celebrating their gender reveal.

Then, a source told Page Six that the TV alum has welcomed her son; however, Aldjufrie has yet to confirm the news herself.

The pregnancy marks a joyful personal milestone for the real estate mogul, whose professional achievements have often played out in front of TV audiences.

Aldjufrie has previously revealed how Umansky helped to cultivate her love of real estate, explaining in a 2016 interview with The Daily Dish that she used to drive around to different listed properties in L.A. with her stepfather as a child and was fascinated by everything that went into selling them.

“I always have been interested in real estate since I was a little kid. I’d drive around to open houses with my mom and Mauricio when I was 6, 7, 8 years old. I’d love to see all the houses,” she said.

“I know L.A. geographically really, really well, having grown up here. I was an only child for the first eight years, so I spent a lot of time with them and was very interested in everything Mauricio was doing. But I have my own love for property and being in L.A., there's some of the most amazing properties and lifestyles in the world.”

According to her profile on The Agency's website, Aldjufrie first became an agent in 2013—and has since helped the company to achieve "over $5 billion in real estate sales."

Interestingly, while she continues her professional and personal relationship with The Agency's founder, Umansky, her mother made clear just a few months ago that she believes she is entitled to a significant chunk of her estranged husband's empire—telling her Bravo co-stars that she will fight to claim her half of the business that she says she helped to build should the couple end up filing for divorce.

Umansky founded The Agency alongside Billy Rose and Blair Chang in 2011, 15 years after he wed Richards—and by May 2022, the business had facilitated more than $57 billion worth of real estate sales, according to Forbes.

According to The Agency's website, the company currently operates more than 145 offices in 14 countries, while Umansky's bio notes that he has "achieved nearly $5 billion in real estate sales and holds the distinction of selling the most homes in the country priced above $20 million.

"He has represented some of the world's most noteworthy properties, including the Playboy Mansion—the first house in L.A. to sell above the $100 million mark—the Walt Disney Estate, and residences owned by Michael Jackson, Michael Jordan and Prince."

Indeed, in the 15 years since it was founded, The Agency has built up a wealth of clients, working with the likes of Lady Gaga, LeBron James, and Ben Affleck.

While it's unclear whether Richards would seek to be involved in the day-to-day running of the business that she hopes to claim a 50% share in, it's likely that she could be in line for a hefty payout if Umansky wishes to keep The Agency to himself.

However, Richards noted that she isn't quite ready to pull the plug on their marriage once and for all.

Microsoft's 24-hour outage had no hacker behind it, and 10 lessons follow for business

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A dormant Windows flaw, not a hacker, caused Microsoft's 24-hour outage. Here are 10 risk lessons for businesses that rely on one cloud vendor.

Most corporate risk plans imagine a cloud outage that begins with an intruder, such as a stolen password, a ransomware gang or a hostile state. A global Microsoft $MSFT outage began with no intruder at all, and no hacker caused it. Even so, people around the world filed more than 7.4 million reports of trouble during the failure, according to ABC News. A fault inside one company was enough to disrupt work across the globe. The 2024 Microsoft IT outage was linked to CrowdStrike $CRWD, a cybersecurity firm, according to TIME. This time the fault sat inside Microsoft's own systems, in what CBC News reports was the company's second disruption of 2026. For a business, the question is less about who might break in and more about what happens when its provider breaks its own systems.

When a provider of this size fails, whole institutions stop working, not just their email. The Next Web reports that trading firms were delayed and airline communications were disrupted. Finance and air travel usually break down for different reasons, but this time they failed together because they rent the same software, and the weak point sat in a layer that no customer controls.

News18 laid out what went wrong behind Microsoft's "Code Red" and what enterprises should take from it. Its lessons cover a dormant flaw that no hacker planted, the backup channels a company needs when Teams and Outlook stop working, and continuity plans built for days rather than minutes. Together, they move the job of staying online from the vendor's promises to the customer's own preparations.

Microsoft $MSFT caused its own day-long outage with a change it had made months earlier. The root of the failure was a hidden flaw that Microsoft added to its gateway servers in July, not an attack by hackers, according to News18. The flaw was a scalability bug that nobody detected for months, The Next Web reports, and if Microsoft's own engineers missed it, no customer could have found it. A bug of this kind stays hidden until the load on a system grows large enough, so a clean record means only that nobody has triggered it yet.

The cost of a dormant flaw is nothing for months and then everything on one day. Once the flaw surfaced, users lost Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams and Copilot together, which left staff with no fallback app inside the same suite. The component that broke had seen "roughly ten years' worth of acceptable failures," Charles Lamanna, Microsoft's executive vice-president for Copilot, Agents and Platform, said in a Code Red review, as relayed in a memo by Amanda Silver and reported by The Next Web. Customers absorbed that cost during the 24-hour outage rather than in small amounts spread across years.

Vendor reviews built on uptime history and incident counts cannot detect this kind of risk, because nothing about a dormant flaw appears in the record until the day it fails. A company that rents its email and collaboration tools should assume that a flaw already exists somewhere in the stack and plan for the day it surfaces.

No customer could have reached the defect itself, but what triggers a hidden flaw and how fast Microsoft can fix it decide how much damage it does.

Microsoft $MSFT's day-long outage began on an ordinary day of operations, with no launch, attack or unusual event behind it. News18 reports that a routine traffic shift, combined with reduced capacity, set off the dormant flaw. The result was a full stop rather than a slowdown, with Microsoft's servers left "overwhelmed and unresponsive," according to ABC News.

An ordinary trigger makes this kind of failure harder to see coming, and it makes the failure no less serious. Engineers tend to flag rare events, such as a major product launch, for extra caution. A traffic shift belongs to the normal running of a service, and nothing about it marks it as a moment of risk. Walayat Hussain, an associate professor at Australian Catholic University, sees that ordinariness as the cause for concern. "This looks like Microsoft's own systems tripping over themselves, and that is the real warning," Hussain told ABC News.

For a customer, the trigger sits inside operations it does not run and cannot schedule around. A company gets no notice of routine traffic shifts inside its provider's systems and has no say in how much spare capacity the provider has on hand.

A company's defense has to begin the moment service stops, whatever the cause turns out to be.

A failure like this is only partly about the broken code. The rest depends on whether the people fixing it can agree on what is broken. Microsoft $MSFT's engineers exchanged more than 11,000 messages during the outage response, News18 reports, split across 11 different Teams chats rather than gathered in one. The weakness this exposed was coordination, not manpower.

Every one of those chats ran on Teams, Microsoft's own chat product. Teams was itself degraded during the outage, and the responders were coordinating through a tool damaged by the same failure they were trying to fix. A diagnosis scattered across many threads, on a tool that is failing along with everything else, is a risk any company could face in its own outage. For an enterprise, the lesson is to decide before a crisis where the diagnosis gets assembled. A company needs one named record that brings the separate threads together, with a person responsible for keeping it, and that record matters more than the number of people typing. It also helps to keep that record somewhere other than the chat tool the company depends on every day.

Responders can trade 11,000 messages and still lack a clear answer if nobody owns the full picture.

An outage ends when engineers have delivered a repair to every affected system, not when they find the bug. Microsoft $MSFT's services were out for 24 hours, according to Microsoft's internal memos reported by News18, and customers had no part in the repair for that whole time. No company can shorten a schedule that another company sets, and its staff could only wait for Microsoft to finish.

The obvious lesson is that Microsoft should push fixes out faster, but its chief executive points to the growing number of patches as a source of risk. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella sees patching speeding up rather than lagging, CNBC reports, and he credits artificial intelligence with finding more bugs for engineers to fix. "The thing that AI has done is it allowed us to find these bugs and fix these bugs. But that means you've got to then patch them, and that creates instability," Nadella said. Every fix is also a change to systems that are already running, and each change carries risk of its own. Patching is therefore a trade-off rather than a race. Leaving flaws unpatched keeps them open, while a steady stream of patches can unsettle the systems that receive them.

Neither faster nor slower patching addresses what made this failure global, which was the lack of a limit on how many systems one bad change could affect. Nadella acknowledges that Microsoft should have tested the change before releasing it and should have built barriers between its systems so that a single bug could not cause a worldwide outage, according to CNBC. For enterprises, the useful questions are whether the vendor releases updates in stages and keeps regions separate. They should also ask whether their own IT staff patch in-house systems in stages, so that nobody installs one update on every machine at once.

The measure that matters is not how fast a vendor delivers a good fix but how many systems it lets one bad change reach.

Microsoft $MSFT's promise to recover from an outage within an hour is a commitment about the next failure, not a record of this one. News18 reports that the company's Code Red plan sets a one-hour recovery target. Traffic management, the way Microsoft moves traffic inside its own systems, is one of the areas the company is overhauling to reach that target. Even if Microsoft meets it, the hour measures how quickly Microsoft resolves a disruption. It does not measure when a customer's courtroom, clinic or trading desk gets back to work.

The accounts of this outage's length do not agree. It lasted 24 hours across Nov. 12–13, 2026, yet The Associated Press reports that Microsoft did not announce full restoration until Saturday, Nov. 14. By either account, Microsoft's actual recovery took far longer than an hour, and a business that writes the hour into its own plans is counting on a performance Microsoft has not yet delivered.

Microsoft restored its services in stages, and customers lost work the whole time. Outlook problems piled up into Friday even as Microsoft kept restoring service, according to The Associated Press, and in Manchester, England, a trial was halted and the jurors were sent home. The recovery time a customer actually faces is Microsoft's gradual restoration plus whatever fallbacks the customer has built for itself.

Plan against the record, not the pledge, and test the hour the next time Microsoft's services go down.

Most companies picture a single point of failure as a target, a system that an attacker aims at and the security budget pays to defend. Microsoft $MSFT's systems stayed out of service for 24 hours without any attacker involved, according to News18. The company said it had found nothing to suggest that anyone acted with malicious intent, according to TIME. A routine update or a hidden design bug can knock out core systems as thoroughly as an attack, and defenses built to stop intruders are not designed to catch either one. The harm a company suffers depends on how much of its work rests on the failed component, not on whether anyone meant to break it.

While systems are down, nobody outside the vendor can tell an accident from an attack, and a dead inbox looks the same either way. In Australia, the outage cut off the Australian Federal Police and even ASIO, the country's domestic spy agency, according to TIME. No cyberattack was behind their losses. Their skill at spotting enemies gave them no protection against a fault with no enemy behind it.

Ruling out an attack became public work of its own. Australia's Home Affairs and Cyber Security Minister Tony Burke passed on Microsoft's early view of the cause. "They believe they are dealing in some ways similar to what we saw with respect to CrowdStrike $CRWD, where there is an update that has gone awry," Burke said, as reported by TIME. Microsoft gave that account while its recovery was still under way, so customers were weighing the failed company's own explanation at the moment they were least able to check it. A company's response therefore has to start from the damage, not wait for a verdict on the cause.

The more useful audit asks which single components, at the vendor or in-house, could stop the company's work for a day, whoever or whatever breaks them. Under that test, an accident at a vendor counts as a threat as serious as a break-in and should get the same contingency planning.

Most companies picture a backup channel as a list of phone numbers kept for emergencies, a way to keep talking rather than a way to keep working. Microsoft $MSFT had an outside channel of that kind during the outage, and it posted its public updates on X $TWTR, according to ABC News. Its engineers, though, ran the repair on Teams, the product that was failing. Microsoft had a separate line for speaking to the public and none for fixing the problem.

A fallback needs a trigger that the company controls as much as it needs a place to go. Microsoft's first update described a problem "in the Americas," ABC News reports, while Immigration New Zealand had stopped processing every visa application. A company that waits for the vendor to confirm the scope may lose hours it could have spent working. An SMS bridge runs entirely outside Microsoft's systems, and a company can switch it on by its own decision. The plan should state the trigger in plain terms: Staff move to the bridge when they lose mail or chat, not when the vendor's status update agrees. A bridge that nobody has practiced on is just a contact list again, and employees need to rehearse using it before a crisis.

Small companies have the least room to absorb a lost day. Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams and Copilot failed together, News18 reports, so a small firm that kept all of its mail and chat in that suite had to find a workaround somewhere else or go without. Some small businesses lost up to $30,000 in a single day, according to ABC News.

The time to set up and test a route outside the suite is now, while every channel still works.

Buying an AI assistant from the company that already runs a firm's email and meetings ties more of the working day to one provider and spreads none of the risk. Companies tend to budget for Copilot as a new capability with risks of its own. During the outage, it failed alongside the rest of the suite. Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams and Copilot all went down at the same time, according to News18. Microsoft $MSFT said AI agents had nothing to do with the cause, according to CBC News. Copilot went dark because it runs on the same foundation as the mail and the meetings, not because of a flaw in the assistant. Each product a company adds from the same provider rests on that foundation too, so it deepens a single dependency instead of adding a separate system.

The remedy is a second set of tools, but building one is a long project. "Ultimately, it has to be diversification. We have to make sure that when a system goes down, we have a backup," Francis Syms, associate dean of applied sciences and technology at Humber Polytechnic, told CBC News. Bill Laboon, vice-president of technical operations at the Web3 Foundation, says companies don't see the value of spreading their systems across providers until one of them fails.

The first step is knowing which work stops when the vendor does. A map of the processes that would halt in a vendor outage gives a company that knowledge before the next failure, and it can also show which critical work already sits outside the shared stack. Santé Québec does not handle patient communications by email, according to CBC News, so those exchanges did not depend on the email service that failed.

The useful question today is not which vendor to switch to, but which work stops when this one goes down.

A continuity plan prepares a company only for the outage it imagines, and a short outage is the easiest one to imagine. News18 reports that a company drafting a formal Continuity of Operations Plan should not count on a quick failover of 15 minutes. CBC News reports that Microsoft $MSFT's outage was in its second day on Nov. 13, so a plan built around a 15-minute switch would have been measuring the wrong scale. A plan that expects service back within minutes has no instructions for the second business morning, when staff, customers and deadlines are still waiting. Companies should size their plans to the failure that just happened, counted in days, rather than to the failure the plan was written for.

The organizations that held up did not recover sooner than anyone else. They had a way to keep working that did not depend on the failed service. Aviva kept its calls going on alternative numbers and moved recordings by Bluetooth, according to CBC News. Its staff could use those low-tech routes because they did not run through Microsoft's cloud. Where a company runs its software is also a continuity decision. Companies that hosted Microsoft software in-house came through somewhat better, and they had made that choice well before the failure, not in the middle of it.

Planning for a multi-day outage means deciding now which work must continue without the cloud, then building that route through manual processes or self-run systems before a company needs it for a second day.

The Government Tested How Dangerous AI Could Get—Then Four ChatBots Helped Plan a Terrorist Attack

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Government Accountability Office secretly tested ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok and found that all four would help make plans when prompted.

The dawn of artificial intelligence brings with it both rewards and risks. With the ability to sift through unfathomably large data sets, AI could help detect cancers in their infancy, unlock some of the most enduring mysteries of mathematics, and even help contain the beating nuclear heart of future fusion reactors.

Unfortunately, the risks of this technology are far from minuscule, including its impact on human education, the environment, and potentially the survival of the human race (an all-too-plausible scenario put forward by AI tech CEOs). Even the everyday LLMs that now litter our digital lives—Claude, Gemini, ChatGPT, and Grok—can aid and abet some of the most dangerous actors on the planet.

At least, that’s the latest conclusion by the Government Accountability Office (GAO), a nonpartisan watchdog housed in the legislative branch of the U.S. government. Typically, GAO turns its investigative gaze on efforts to limit fraud and wasteful spending in the government itself, but in a new tactic at the urging of U.S. Senator Maggie Hassan (D-NH), the office instead performed real-world tests on the platform to see if these four popular AI models would aid in planning a terrorist attack.

“A terrorist can log onto an AI platform and in less than 12 hours walk away with a concrete playbook for how to carry out a deadly attack,” Hassan, incoming Chair of the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, said in a press statement. “Today’s new report makes it clear that the AI public safety threat is no longer theoretical.”

GAO conducted this test in the spring this year, and earlier this week reported some disturbing results. Two of the platforms, according to an official GAO release, provided guidance on building and detonating an improvised explosion device, or IED, and two other AI models showing differing methods for targeting the electrical grid. Within 12 hours, all four models also gamed through potential locations and methods for carrying out such an attack.

Although these violent lines of questioning triggered each platform’s safeguards, GAO got around them using common safeguard-bypassing techniques. Additionally, they confirmed the validity of the information provided with experts. The results of this investigation were published in a 61-page report that will be central to future senate hearings on AI safety.

“We set out to do two different types of evil,” GAO director Kevin Walsh said in an interview posted on YouTube. “The first type is we wanted to create a bomb. And we didn’t just want to know how to create a bomb, we wanted it to help pick out a target, a location, and a time, and to give us instructions all the way…Evil scenario two, we wanted to do a bunch of economic damage, and in both cases that ended up being targeting the electrical grid.”

In one case, Walsh reports that one AI model would refuse to answer questions or divert the user to more safe behaviors but said that “it wasn’t always successful.” Walsh says that companies theoretically call law enforcement when suspicious activity is detected, but whether that occurs and at what frequency is uncertain.

“As AI capabilities evolve, AI providers will continue to refine their safeguards,” the report concludes. “However, in its current state, this technology can potentially be dangerous and its misuse represents a growing risk to national and economic security.”

The GAO officially released these terrorist-abetting findings on November 18, 2026, the same day Anthropic, creators of Claude, went public on the stock exchange. Despite the company’s own admission of AI being an existential threat to humanity, its IPO at one point reached a meteoric a $2 trillion valuation before dipping back down to a (measly) $1.6 trillion.
