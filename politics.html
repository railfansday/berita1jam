Air Serbia Flight To Nowhere: A320 Forced To Return To Belgrade Over Mysterious Security Concern

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

An Air Serbia flight to Moscow made it as far as Lithuania before turning around and returning to Belgrade over a security concern involving one passenger. Serbian media identified that passenger as the country’s former intelligence chief, who allegedly boarded without completing security screening. He denies wrongdoing.

As flagged by One Mile At A Time, Air Serbia flight JU134 departed Belgrade for Moscow on November 3, 2026, but turned back over southern Lithuania. According to Serbian media, the Airbus A320 left at 6:48 PM and returned shortly before 10:00 PM. Passengers spent more than three hours flying only to end up where they started.

Air Serbia confirmed that airport authorities requested additional security checks concerning one passenger. The airline said it returned in accordance with the instructions it received, but did not publicly identify that passenger.

The passenger in question has since been identified as Aleksandar Vulin, Serbia’s former intelligence chief. It appears that someone helped him board without completing the required screening and that the airport’s corporate security director ordered the return after reviewing surveillance footage.

Vulin insists he completed the required checks, was carrying no prohibited items, and was being targeted for political reasons (his pro-Russian party lost in a snap election last month). He said his trip concerned negotiations over Serbia’s gas arrangement with Russia.

Passengers and their luggage underwent additional screening in Belgrade. The flight eventually continued to Moscow with a replacement crew, arriving about seven hours late.

If the reports are accurate, the obvious question is how someone was allowed to board without completing the required checks. Political prominence should not provide an exemption from an aviation security procedure, though I understand how easy it is to open an employee access door for a VIP).

But Vulin disputes that account, and the airline’s statement establishes only that authorities requested additional screening. That leaves important questions about what happened before departure and what prompted the return. Vulin was allowed to continue on the delayed flight to Moscow.

Belgrade’s Higher Public Prosecutor’s Office has requested an investigation into the circumstances, including whether procedures were followed, who authorized the aircraft’s return, and what security assessment supported that decision.

That inquiry is appropriate. If someone bypassed screening, there should be accountability for how it happened. If the checks were properly completed, authorities need to explain why an aircraft full of passengers was brought back.

Seven flights across Europe targeted by suspected bomb threats

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Watch: Sniffer dog inspects Ryanair plane in Spain after suspected bomb threat

Seven passenger planes across Europe, two of which departed from the UK, have been inspected following a series of suspected bomb threats.

Two Ryanair flights, one originating from Dublin and the other from Lisbon, were searched after landing in Rome following anonymous tips but no explosives were found, sources at the city's Fiumicino airport told the BBC.

The Dublin flight was escorted by the Italian Air Force until landing. Authorities have also searched planes in Poland, Spain and Bulgaria.

While officially there is no link between them, authorities from various countries will be co-operating to assess whether this was a co-ordinated attack, designed to disrupt airspace.

The UK government described today's incidents as "malicious hoax threats".

In a statement it said it had "one of the most robust aviation security systems in the world, with well-tested processes for dealing with any potential threats, as well as malicious hoax threats, like those we've seen today".

Aviation security expert Paul Mason said bomb threats on passenger planes "don't happen that frequently", which made the latest incidents "unusual".

"It sounds like they've been made in a credible manner across a broad range of airlines," Mason told BBC Radio 5 Live. "That would suggest to me that it's been a very deliberate act by a typical actor rather than a load of coincidental, malicious calls."

He said "there are a lot of processes" that the onboard crew and people on the ground must go through "to validate the credibility of the threat".

A flight may be asked to divert if the credibility of a threat "is of a certain level", Mason added, and in some cases the onboard crew may not even be advised on what the ground operators were dealing with.

Two of the four flights were en route to Warsaw Chopin airport

In Rome, sources at Fiumicino airport told the BBC police bomb disposal experts carried out standard security protocols to ensure the safety of all passengers and crew.

Inspections of the passengers, luggage, and the aircraft yielded negative results, and the alert has since been cleared.

One plane which landed in Murcia, southern Spain - a Ryanair flight coming from Manchester - was also found not to have any explosives on board, Spanish authorities said.

One passenger later said she was unaware of the bomb threat until after the plane landed.

"We weren't told anything during the flight," Sarah told BBC Radio 5 Live. "The plane stopped quite suddenly and then we were kept on the runway for around 30 minutes."

"Then the fire engines turned up, the bomb squad turned up and we were asked to evacuate," she continues.

Sarah said the plane had not been on the ground a minute before it was "surrounded by fire engines and police and a bomb squad". All the passengers were then escorted by police, leaving all their belongings.

"We were only later told that it was an actual threat, a bomb threat to the plane itself," she added.

Two flights heading to Warsaw were also affected, including one from London Heathrow.

The Heathrow flight was originally meant to land at the Polish capital's main airport, Chopin, but was diverted instead to the smaller Modlin airport, briefly forcing its closure.

A Scandinavian Airlines flight from Copenhagen to Warsaw-Chopin landed as planned.

Later on Tuesday, Bulgarian Transport Minister Georgi Peev said there were bomb threats against two planes travelling to the country's capital Sofia.

He told the state-owned Bulgarian News Agency (BTA) the flights were from the northern Italian city of Bergamo and Ireland's capital Dublin.

The flights into Sofia were not diverted and no explosives were found when the planes were checked, Peev said.

Bulgaria's border police said in a statement sent to the BBC that at around 10:35 (08:35 GMT) it had received an email about "the presence of an improvised explosive device on a flight returning from Bergamo".

"Standard procedures for such incidents were immediately activated, and an operational headquarters comprising representatives of various institutions was convened," the statement said.

The border police added that the same email also reported a "similar threat against aircraft arriving from Dublin".

Initial checks found no explosives on board the Crown Iris cruise ship

In a separate development, a bomb alert triggered the evacuation of 1,537 passengers and crew from an Israel-bound cruise ship in Greece, media reports from both countries say.

Initial checks of the Panama-flagged Crown Iris vessel currently docked at a port on the Aegean island of Syros found no explosives, they said citing the Greek Coast Guard. A second inspection is expected shortly.

Tuesday's aircraft incidents come a day after a Wizz Air flight from London Luton made an emergency landing in Budapest, escorted by a Hungarian fighter jet, after a bomb threat.

Wizz Air said later that security checks had been made and nothing was found.

BBC Verify has consulted aviation safety experts about what happens on board a plane when a bomb threat is issued.

Marco Chan, a former pilot for Cathay Pacific Airways and Hong Kong Airlines who is now a senior lecturer in aviation operations at Kingston University, said a bomb threat is considered "genuine until it is resolved, even though most turn out to be hoaxes".

Chan said crews will "quietly search" toilets, galleys and storage areas and will not touch a suspicious item unless it has to be taken to an area on the aircraft designated as the least risky bomb location.

The aircraft's captain will have multiple factors to assess, according to former airline pilot Terry Tozer, an aviation safety commentator.

He said these include what facilities are available at the airport they are being diverted to and how the plane can continue onwards should it turn out to be a false threat.

Chan added that once on the ground, the plane is generally "taken to an isolated stand, passengers are disembarked, and police and bomb disposal teams search it".

My Parents Took Me Voting. Now I'm Doing The Same With My Daughter.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Your candidate might not always win. But, as my parents taught me, it’s important to show up anyway.

My earliest memories of voting take place at the elementary school up the street from my parents’ house. Every Election Day, my parents would bring me and my younger sister along with them to the polls. I remember standing in the long lines impatiently wondering when it’d be our turn. I remember at least one year where there was a mock ballot for kids. And, perhaps most importantly to elementary-school-aged me, I remember receiving the prized Future Voter sticker at the end.

At the time, I didn’t think of any of this as civic education. My parents weren’t necessarily sitting us down for lectures about democracy or giving us pop quizzes on the Voting Rights Act of 1965 (although that would come later during my AP U.S. Government class). My parents were simply voting and we were watching. But, as it turns out, we were learning by observing.

My parents made it abundantly clear to my sister and me that voting was both a right and a responsibility — and that this wasn’t always the case for people who looked like us. As Black girls growing up in America, that history mattered. We learned that Black people had marched, organized, been beaten and even killed for the right my parents exercised so routinely at the elementary school around the corner.

So, when Election Day came around, we went. And not just for the presidential elections every four years. My parents participated in every election — local, federal, didn’t matter. The responsibility was the same.

By the time I was finally old enough to vote myself, I couldn’t wait.

I missed being able to vote in the 2004 presidential election by a year, which meant the 2008 election would be my first chance to vote for president. And I felt elated that my first presidential vote would be for the country’s first Black president.

At the time, I was working as a reporter at my community newspaper in a conservative county in Maryland. On Election Day, a colleague and I spent hours driving around to various polling places, interviewing voters and reporting on turnout before returning to the Board of Elections to wait for results.

After work, I went home and watched the national returns with my dad. When Barack Obama walked onto the stage in Chicago’s Grant Park with his beautiful Black family, I remember staring at the television in disbelief.

Wow, I thought to myself, soaking in the moment. I just witnessed history.

But elections don’t always end with the history you hoped to witness.

In 2016, I proudly cast my ballot for Hillary Clinton and went to bed believing we were about the elect the country’s first woman president. We all know how that turned out.

Four years later, I voted for Joe Biden, but I was especially excited about his running mate, Kamala Harris, whom I’d supported in the Democratic primary. Watching a Black and Asian woman become vice president meant something different to me. Of course, representation isn’t the only reason to vote for someone and I think we’re all doing ourselves a disservice if we pretend as if identity is entirely separate from politics. As a Black woman in America, I’d spent most of my life looking at the highest echelons of power and rarely seeing anyone who looks like you.

In the spring of 2024, I picked up my then two-year-old daughter Violet from daycare, went to swim lessons and then swung by our neighborhood elementary school to vote in the Illinois presidential primary. With her little puffs and binky in tow, she naturally became the star of the polling place.

Our next-door neighbor, Mr. Steve, was working the polls that day and gave Violet a Future Voter sticker. She was entranced. To her, the sticker was probably the best part of the whole outing, which, when I thought about it, was exactly how I felt when I was her age (and, let’s be real, is also how I feel as an adult).

Afterward, we went outside to the school playground and, as I watched her run around, it hit me what a full-circle moment this had become. And then I had another thought: This could be a children’s book.

I wanted to write a book that could explain voting to children around Violet’s age: something that would talk about walking into a polling place; why grown-ups stood in line to fill out ballots; what even is a ballot; and why any of this matters anyway.

That fateful afternoon eventually became the premise of Violet Goes Voting, an early reader about a little girl accompanying her parents to the polls and learning that voting is one way we use our voices. But the book was just the beginning.

That fall, my husband and I took Violet with us when we voted in the presidential election. This time, I had a very specific picture in my head of what the moment might look like in both real life and the book version. My daughter would sit on my lap as I cast my ballot to elect the first Black woman president of the United States.

My parents had taken their Black daughters to the polls so we would understand that our voices mattered. Decades later, I would take mine with me as I helped elect a Black woman to the White House. Except, of course, that’s not what happened.

Like so many people, I woke up the next morning devastated. The history I had imagined sharing with my daughter would have to wait. Since then, I’ve thought a lot about what, exactly, I want Violet to inherit from me and learn from all of this.

Because maybe the lesson was never supposed to be that if you show up, your candidate will win. My parents certainly never promised that to me. What they taught me was the importance of showing up anyway.

Voting is a small part of civic engagement, but it was my introduction to it nonetheless. Long before I knew what a primary was, before I understood the harsh reality of voter suppression or could name any of my congressional representatives, before I was old enough to fill in a bubble next to a candidate’s name, I knew what Election Day looked like.

It looked like my parents making time to go to the polls. It looked like my sister and me eagerly tagging along. It looked like waiting our turn. And, yes, it looked like getting a sticker at the end.

Now, as a mom, I understand that those seemingly ordinary trips were teaching me something much bigger: This is what our family does. We show up. We pay attention. We responsibly exercise the rights people before us fought to secure. We don’t take them for granted.

Only time will tell who Violet will vote for when she’s old enough to cast her first ballot. And, frankly, we have a while before that happens. She’s much more interested in playgrounds than politics at the moment, which is exactly how it should be for an innocent five-year-old.

But I hope that when her first Election Day arrives, she’ll look back on our family selfie from outside the voting place in 2024. I hope she’ll remember me reading to her the book that she inspired. I hope she understands why walking into that booth and making her own choice carries a history older than the both of us combined.

But most of all and most importantly, I simply hope she shows up. Because that’s the inheritance my parents gave me and my sister. And now it’s mine to pass on.

L’Oreal Thompson Payton is an author, journalist and the founder of Zora’s Place, a Black feminist bookstore and community hub in Evanston, Illinois. Her books include Stop Waiting for Perfect, Amanda Gorman: Poet and Activist, Violet Goes Voting and the highly-anticipated memoir Infertile Black Girl (Fall 2027). Her byline has appeared in outlets such as Essence, Fortune, New York magazine and SELF. Learn more at lorealthompsonpayton.com.
