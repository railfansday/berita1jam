Don't expect more information on The Elder Scrolls 6: Sentinel "for some time," Bethesda says

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

After Bethesda formally named The Elder Scrolls 6: Sentinel earlier this month, some series fans were hopeful that more news wasn't far behind. Skyrim is 15 years old today (11th November) after all, but Bethesda Game Studios has since dashed those hopes into oblivion.

In response to a Reddit post speculating about possible Sentinel news on Skyrim's birthday, the official Bethesda Game Studios account chimed in to temper everybody's expectations.

"Hey everyone, wanted to pop in to say that we aren't going to have any big announcements tomorrow," the social media account wrote. "We're excited you're excited but we will not be sharing anything new on Sentinel for some time."

The Elder Scrolls fans have already been waiting for the series' next mainline follow-up for, erm, also quite some time. Specifically, Skyrim came out in 2011, The Elder Scrolls 6's first reveal trailer reared its head in 2018, and BGS' Starfield took to the stars in 2023 - a wait so long that it had even the most faithful among us questioning if a new Elder Scrolls could ever recapture the series' magic.

So, there you have it - don't expect to see or hear about Sentinel anytime soon. Better yet, lock it up in some corner of your brain and be pleasantly surprised when it does miraculously reappear in 20XX.

In the meantime, Bethesda isn't letting Skyrim's 15th anniversary go by without a few celebrations. For one, the seminal role-playing game is deeply discounted on all platforms, in the very unlikely scenario you haven't tried it out yet.

The Elder Scrolls Online Season Two: Echoes of Skyrim is also joining the festivities by adding the Greymoor Chapter into the base game for free, giving everyone access to a few famous Skyrim locales and more. A brand-new, separate Golden Pursuit campaign will let you earn some Skyrim-centric goodies up until 2nd December, as well.

Tether's Tiny AI Agent Beats Models 2.5 Times Its Size at Controlling Your Phone

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tether's Tiny AI Agent Beats Models 2.5 Times Its Size at Controlling Your Phone

Tether, the company behind the world's largest stablecoin, USDT, is getting surprisingly good at making tiny AI models punch above their weight.

The company on Wednesday unveiled QVAC BotPsy, an 800-million-parameter AI agent designed to carry out everyday tasks on personal devices. Parameters are essentially the knobs that determine how much an AI model can do, and more of them generally translate to improved performance. And for reference, frontier AI models today run in the trillions of parameters.

In the company's own testing, Tether’s relatively tiny model outperformed competitors with up to 2.5 times as many parameters, suggesting that AI assistants may not need enormous models to get things done.

Unlike traditional chatbots that simply respond to questions, BotPsy is agentic, meaning it’s designed to take action. It can identify which tools an application needs, execute commands in the right order, and adjust when something goes wrong.

For example, Tether described a benchmark scenario in which an AI assistant needed to send a message but encountered two problems: Wi-Fi was disabled and the inbox was full. BotPsy turned on Wi-Fi, cleared enough storage, and completed the task, while the underlying model repeatedly attempted the same failed operation.

That's a fairly mundane achievement, but it's precisely the kind of problem that makes AI agents useful outside a chatbot window.

BotPsy is built on Alibaba's Qwen3.5-0.8B architecture, a relatively small language model that Tether trained further to specialize in interacting with software tools. Its 800 million parameters represent the numerical values the model learned during training, and a smaller parameter count generally means less memory is needed to run it.

According to Tether's technical documentation, BotPsy scored 44.0% on BFCL v4, a benchmark that evaluates an AI system's ability to select and execute software functions across different tasks and conversations. Scores reflect a weighted percentage of successful performance across tool-use categories.

The underlying Qwen3.5-0.8B model scored 23.4%, while the considerably larger Qwen3.5-2B reached 37.7%.

BotPsy also scored 58.7% on ACEBench, which evaluates the accuracy of function calls, including selecting the appropriate tools and arguments. Its base model managed 30.1%, while the 2-billion-parameter competitor scored 55.4%.

The company tested five models across six tool-use evaluations, with BotPsy posting the highest results on five. Its average score across those tests reached 45.0%, compared with 38.8% for the larger Qwen model.

BotPsy is the latest addition to QVAC, Tether's effort to build an AI ecosystem that runs on devices controlled by users rather than relying entirely on centralized cloud infrastructure.

The company first introduced QVAC in May 2025, positioning the project as an alternative to AI services operated by technology giants. Its longer-term ambitions included autonomous agents capable of interacting with applications and potentially making payments using Bitcoin and USDT.

Tether expanded that initiative in October 2025 with a 41-billion-token dataset for training science and engineering models, alongside QVAC Workbench, an application that lets users run AI models locally.

Earlier this year, Tether released its SDK, providing developers with tools to build offline AI applications across mobile and desktop operating systems.

The company also developed QVAC MedPsy, a medical AI system designed to run on consumer hardware. Its 1.7-billion-parameter version outperformed Google's much larger MedGemma-27B on HealthBench Hard, a test of realistic clinical conversations evaluated using physician-developed criteria.

BotPsy is currently available as an open-weight model under the Apache 2.0 license, meaning developers can download, modify, and integrate it into their own applications. Tether has also released compressed versions intended to reduce memory requirements for local deployment.

The company has not announced a release date for a finished consumer assistant powered by BotPsy. Its published model card also says the training code and training data are not included in the release.

Kai Havertz can solve Arsenal attacking problem by addressing Premier League-topping flaw

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Correspondent AUTHORITY Sports journalist with experience at GamingINTEL and GRV Media; University of Wolverhampton graduate. FOCUS Data content, opinion, and general news coverage across the Breaking Media network. Kai Havertz is known for his link-up qualities at Arsenal, but he may be hampering attacks this season more than ever before.

The German is not your typical centre-forward, but one who can drop deep and collect the ball to play to other teammates.

Havertz is also capable of getting on the end of chances, as his goal against Liverpool showed.

But the 27-year-old has been showing a significant flaw in his game this season, coming out on top in an unwanted stat.

In fact, his numbers suggest that Havertz is becoming an increasingly frustrating presence in the final third.

And one that Mikel Arteta must address if Arsenal are to continue their fine form.

Arsenal's defence has become famous for how organised and difficult it is to break down.

But their attacking style of play can often leave a lot to be desired.

Despite being top of the league, Arsenal are fifth for xG created, with 16.2, as per FotMob.

They are 10th for big chances (24), showing that when they get attacks, they need to make the most of them.

Of course, those stats are not awful, and have been good enough for the Gunners to stake their claim in their quest to retain their title.

But one issue could come back to haunt them, especially if they find themselves in games where they struggle to break down teams.

This season, Havertz is leading the way in a stat that no centre-forward will want.

As shared by Squawka, Havertz is the player in the Premier League who has been called offside the most times in the opening 10 games, with a total of 15.

Other than offsides, Havertz has also been poor at giving away fouls, with his 11 fouls committed placing him in the 26th percentile.

Allowing the opposition the ball through a foul or offside is not ideal, even if Arsenal have the strong defence to deal with it.

And if Havertz can remain onside, they could begin creeping up the big chances list.

While offsides are not ideal, so is the German's ability in front of goal, or at least it was in the past.

Havertz has scored three goals from just 2.78 xG, while his xG has converted into 3.78 xGOT (expected goals on target).

The fact that Havertz' xGOT is higher than his xG shows that his shot placement has been good this campaign.

And he will feel unlucky not to have scored at least one more goal.

With $720M round, Britain’s Fractile gets a leg up in the race for AI chips

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Fractile, a British startup developing AI chips, has raised a $720 million Series C backed by Britain’s Sovereign AI Fund with hopes of “ensuring the U.K. has a greater stake in the hardware that will underpin its future economy,” its government stated.

Alongside the likes of Axelera, Etched, and OLIX, this makes Fractile one of the top-funded startup players in the race for purpose-built AI chips. Such chips are one of several avenues being explored to reduce the cost of inference — aka using increasingly demanding AI models in production.

However, Fractile seeks to go beyond hardware with a full-stack approach that also includes a machine learning compiler to maximize the speed at which LLMs will get executed on its chips.

As a result, the company frames itself as a frontier AI company, and its latest round led by Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed Venture Partners and Redpoint was priced accordingly. According to the British government, the valuation was “over £5 billion” — quite possibly $7 billion, a figure reported both by the Financial Times and a Reuters source.

Still, Fractile’s main differentiator will arguably be its hardware design. After a pivot, its approach now revolves on DRAM, the most common type of RAM, but stacked on top of each chip in such a way that it is hoping will bypass the usual tradeoff between memory and capacity.

This tradeoff has become more problematic with the rise of agentic AI, as companies seek to perform longer chains of tasks with large models without compromising on speed. As compute struggles to keep up, hopes are high for AI chips, despite their limited availability to date.

In a LinkedIn post, Fractile CEO Walter Goodwin wrote that “there is still a lot of work ahead” for the company, and that the new funding will enable the startup to “ship [its] first chips and systems next year.”

Bose has issued a recall of more than 670,000 lithium-ion battery packs used in the company's S1 Pro and S1 Pro+ Bluetooth speaker systems due to overheating issues.

According to a Consumer Product Safety Commission announcement, Bose has received reports of the battery packs overheating to the point of melting, producing smoke or catching fire. Fortunately, no injuries have been reported.

The recall specifically affects battery packs that include any one of the following six digits in the serial numbers: 083723, 078352, 084105, 078591, 078592, 084106, or 086409. You can find the 17-digit number on the label printed on the bottom of the pack.

These packs were sold both with the S1 Pro and S1 Pro+ speakers as well as accessories, for between $100 and $160 from December 2017 through July 2026. The speaker systems cost $700.

In total, 634,759 were sold in the US, with an additional 35,400 in Canada.

If you have the S1 Pro or S1 Pro+ Portable Bluetooth Speaker Systems, Bose requests that you stop using the battery packs immediately and remove them from the speakers. You can still use the speakers connected to AC power.

If you have an uninstalled battery pack, you just need to find the serial number and send it to Bose.

Bose is asking owners to register and provide the serial number at http://www.bose.com/support/s1-battery-recall. The company is providing a free replacement battery pack.

You'll need to dispose of the battery safely, which means do not just throw the battery pack in the trash or in used battery recycling boxes at retail or home improvement stores. Lithium-ion batteries must be disposed of differently from other batteries because of the greater risk of fire.

You'll need to look up your local municipality's household hazardous waste collection center. You should contact the center because not everyone accepts lithium-ion batteries. They will generally be able to point you in the right direction.

United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby Bans AI Agents From Booking Tickets, Doesn’t Want “Another Middleman”

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

United Airlines is banning personal AI assistants from booking flights. Its new contract of carriage goes beyond United.com to agents working through travel agencies, and applies even when a customer supervises or approves what the software does. The airline cites privacy, unintended purchases and regulatory requirements. But CEO Scott Kirby revealed the real reason at an event in Chicago. “To be honest, I don’t want to create another middleman.” I get that they want to negotiate the terms before a new company gets between United and its customers. But United can’t just decide that customers will have to do the work themselves and expect that all customers will go ahead and do the work to buy tickets from United when it becomes easier just to buy from someone else. And writing a ban into a contract doesn’t mean United can actually identify an AI booking that comes through Expedia. The Ban Reaches Beyond United’s Website United’s November 11 contract of carriage revision adds AI agents to its prohibited ticketing practices. Rule 6.J.6 bans: The use of AI Agents to make, modify or service a Ticket, and/or to purchase any service or product. The definition includes assistants accessing United through a travel agency or another intermediary. Human supervision or approval doesn’t circumvent that. And its Rule 5.H gives United the right to cancel reservations made, modified or serviced by these agents. The Wall Street Journal says agents can still present United flights and prices. AI research, followed by the customer making a purchase on their own, doesn’t run afoul of United’s rules. United Wouldn’t Know If An AI Agent Bought Through Expedia? Expedia already has a ChatGPT integration. It returns flight and hotel options with prices and availability. Expedia’s current instructions then send the customer to Expedia to complete the purchase. It’s not an autonomous purchase inside ChatGPT, but if an agent completes the purchase on Expedia United just sees an Expedia booking not how the booking was made. United says its systems can identify booking sources. Sure, but that is a non-sequitur. A contractual ban isn’t a technical one. United may push intermediaries to enforce the restriction, but there are lots of intermediaries out there. But we’ve seen airlines go to war with online travel agencies before, cutting off their own sales. The Strongest Case For United Is About Bargaining Power United may want to limit access to its sysems: The next middleman could become expensive. An assistant that decides which flights customers see can eventually demand referral fees, sell placement or favor suppliers that agree to its terms. Sam Altman says that if an AI does this, no one will trust it, and the stakes are too high to compromise trust for a travel commission. Someone has to own the mistakes. An agent can buy the wrong dates, misunderstand a fare restriction or cancel a ticket the customer wanted to keep. Agencies have commercial arrangements for ticketing and servicing. An AI assistant operating a browser may not bring the same accountability. And the law is evolving here. I’ve covered Delta’s own AI canceling a return flight it said it would preserve. Delta acknowledged a bug and restored the itinerary. Shopping consumes resources even when nobody buys. An agent can compare far more combinations than a human. The International Air Transport Association has examined the cost of growing search volumes. But United isn’t banning search, just completing the transaction. United can do this now, but it won’t last. The airline says agent bookings are still rare. That makes this a relatively inexpensive time to withhold permission. Every customer who gets used to buying another airline through an assistant becomes harder to win back. And the same customers most likely to use an AI assistant are the ones most likely to come over to United for Starlink. United has a hub in San Francisco. It’s ultimately not going to work for Scott Kirby to stand athwart history yelling “stop!” Customers Have A Reason To Hire The Bot United’s safety argument is weak. The federal hazardous-materials rule requires passenger notices, including at ticket purchase where practical and at check-in, and passenger acknowledgment in self-service transactions. It doesn’t require the customer to manually complete every other step of a booking. An assistant can stop and ask the passenger to review a disclosure or give a required confirmation. United’s definition still covers an agent whose actions the customer supervises and approves. Nor does accepting an agent have to mean giving unfamiliar software unrestricted use of a MileagePlus password. Visa is developing ways for merchants to recognize approved agents and connect their actions with customer and payment information. The customer’s time is part of the cost of buying a flight. If I can tell an assistant my dates, preferred airports, seat requirements and budget, there’s value in having it compare the choices and handle the purchase. Sometimes United’s schedule will be good enough that consumers will do the extra work. Sometimes another airline will offer a comparable trip and let an assistant finish it. We’ve already seen an airline overestimate its ability to make customers use its preferred distribution channels. American backed away from its plan to deny mileage earning on certain travel agency bookings. Customers didn’t all abandon the agencies and processes they preferred just because American wanted them to. There’s also an obvious reason airlines might dislike an assistant that works well for the passenger. It can compare the total price, recognize benefits the customer already gets from status or a credit card, and decline add-ons that aren’t worth buying. United has the worst value prop of any U.S. airline for basic economy! I’ve made the same argument about rewards credit cards: companies are excited about using AI to sell more, but customers get better tools too. 18 leaders on supporting diverse cognitive, physical, and learning needs

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The workplace doesn’t work equally well for everyone. Some workers face obvious physical challenges that require accommodation. Others have diverse physical, cognitive, and learning needs that can stay hidden, overlooked, or brushed aside. When those needs are unmet, workers can struggle to do their best work. When their needs are understood and addressed, workers can flourish and so can the workplace.

We asked members of the Fast Company Impact Council what workplaces should consider doing to better support people with diverse cognitive, physical, and learning needs. Here, 18 of those leaders share insights on what does and doesn’t help when it comes to supporting all people.

1. DESIGN WITH PEOPLE AND LIVED EXPERIENCE

The goal should be to design with people, not simply for them. Lived experience can reveal barriers that may be invisible to those creating the workplace and, when incorporated into the design, often leads to solutions that are more intuitive, flexible, and effective for everyone. That means involving people with diverse cognitive, physical, and learning needs in shaping the workplace itself from the beginning—from the physical environment and technology to communication, policies, and organizational practices. Accessibility should not be an afterthought but a fundamental design principle. — Gail Zauder, AbleFly Inc.

2. MAKE COMMUNICATIONS ADJUSTMENTS

I have an employee with autism, and she has taught me a great deal about how differently people process information. When I provide clear direction, specific responsibilities, and complete expectations, she performs exceptionally well. I’ve learned to be patient and adapt my communication style to what works best for her. She is genuinely one of my best employees and consistently exceeds expectations. As leaders, we need to recognize that everyone works and learns differently. Sometimes small adjustments in how we communicate make an extraordinary difference. — Kim Wileman, No Makeup Makeup

3. DON’T MAKE PEOPLE DISCOVER THEIR ACCOMMODATIONS

Proactivity and clarity are a great starting place. Our Neurodiversity at Work Survey found that 70% of neurodivergent employees don’t know what accommodations they’re entitled to, and 60% don’t know who to ask. Leaders can change that by making accommodations known from the start, rather than something employees have to uncover or disclose to get. Tell candidates what’s available during interviews, when they join, and throughout their tenure. Flexible workspaces, meeting pre-reads, and transcription tools can make a big difference. These are things that benefit everyone and are especially helpful to those with cognitive differences. — Nathan Friedman, Understood.org

4. UPDATE POLICIES AND LISTEN

Most policies exist because of something that happened before there was a policy. So when a new hire asks why something works the way it does and the answer is “that’s how it’s always been,” that’s a good sign it’s time for an update. Beyond that, listen. Not a survey whose feedback sits untouched for months, but active listening in one-to-ones, Slack channels, team meetings, and the break room. A generic response that ignores what people said is worse than no response at all. So listen, then be clear about what can change, what can’t, and why. — Colby Proffitt, Seekr

5. DESIGN WITH FLEXIBILITY FROM THE START

Most workplaces were designed around a narrow definition of how people should think, communicate, and perform. That model is outdated. The better approach is to design for flexibility from the start, rather than forcing people to ask for exceptions just to do their best work. — Ran Mullins, Psympl

6. ASK PEOPLE WHAT HELPS THEM DO THEIR BEST WORK

Ask people what they need to do their best work, then listen. It’s harder than it sounds and worth the effort. Share agendas ahead of time, let people weigh in live or afterward, and caption and record meetings. One of my team’s culture intentions is “Clear is Kind.” Clarity takes effort and sometimes courage, and it drives better outcomes. — Michele Walsh, UNICEF USA

7. RETHINK WHO GETS TO LEAD

Living with a vision disability has taught me that systems designed for the “average” person leave talent behind. That insight fuels my commitment to inclusive leadership, and my belief that accessibility and inclusion are drivers of innovation. Across education, technology, executive search, and leadership advisory, we need to challenge organizations to rethink who gets to lead, how leadership is defined, and how different perspectives create stronger organizations. Difference is a strength, not a barrier. — Meredith Rosenberg, NU Advisory Partners

8. MAKE IT COMFORTABLE TO ASK FOR HELP

I think workplaces should spend less time trying to make everyone work the same way. People think, learn, communicate, and solve problems differently, and that can be a real strength. Leaders should be clear about the outcome they need while giving people flexibility in how they get there. It’s also important to create an environment where people are comfortable asking for what they need without feeling singled out. Some of the best ideas come from people who see a problem differently than everyone else. If we build workplaces around only one way of thinking or working, we shouldn’t be surprised when we get the same kinds of answers. — Yuping Huang, Quantum Computing Inc.

9. MAKE FLEXIBILITY THE DEFAULT

Workplaces are strongest when they embrace diverse perspectives, experiences, and ways of thinking. Innovation often comes from people who see challenges differently. By fostering an open-minded, inclusive environment and providing flexibility for different problem-solving approaches, organizations can unlock new possibilities and empower everyone to contribute at their highest potential. — Chris K Bailey, Bailey Brand Consulting

10. LOOK AT CULTURE AND POLICY TOGETHER

First, people with diverse cognitive, physical, and learning needs bring real strengths. Many colleagues with dyslexia or ADHD are standout big picture thinkers, and colleagues with autism often catch patterns others miss. Next, look at culture and policy together. Do your rigid schedules, meeting norms, or accommodation process bring out their best, or get in the way? Written agendas and flexible hours go a long way. Finally, partner with Understood.org, 2Gether-International, and Easterseals, whose research and expertise can help everyone contribute at their best. — Muneer Panjwani, Engage for Good | The Halo Awards

11. FOCUS ON HOW PEOPLE LEARN

Having someone on our team with severe depression made us rethink our interview process and how we work. We focused more on how people learn and apply what they know and asked what support they need to do that well. Workplaces should give people different ways to demonstrate their skills and flexibility in how they do the work. That can mean written instructions, accessible tools, or time to process information before responding. Ask the person, then follow through. — Andra Vaduva, SafeSpace

12. ADJUSTMENTS SHOULD BE STANDARD PRACTICE

Start by asking people how they work best and then action the answer. Intentional process can cover most of what is typically needed: agendas sent before meetings, written follow-ups after them, captions turned on, and a choice between written and spoken updates. These changes cost almost nothing and help everyone, including people who will never ask. Adjustments can be standard practice for the whole team. — James Chester, WVN

13. MAKE ROOM FOR DIFFERENCES

Make flexibility a core part of your workplace culture. Some people hit their productivity stride at 6 a.m., others at 4 p.m. Some respond quickly and others need time to digest the information for a bit before offering an idea that strengthens the plan. Some work better in silence, others with background noise or music in their headphones. Don’t put roadblocks in front of talent by thinking there’s only one way of working. Make room for those differences so everyone has an opportunity to do their best work. Make sure people feel comfortable requesting the tools or accommodations they need to thrive. — Matt Mandrella, City of Huntsville, AL

14. UNDERSTAND HOW YOUR PEOPLE WORK

The best place to start is recognizing that there is no single way people do their best work. Some need schedule flexibility, some process information differently, and others need different tools or environments to stay focused. That puts responsibility on managers to understand how their people work and create room for those differences. It should be easy for an employee to say, “Here’s what helps me do my best work,” without feeling they have to justify it. When you give people the flexibility to play to their strengths, you build a better workplace for everyone. — Matt Conlin, Fluent

15. DON’T TREAT FLEXIBILITY LIKE AN EXCEPTION

Workplaces should start by making flexibility normal instead of treating it like an exception. That means offering different ways to communicate, contribute, process information, and structure work. The goal should be to judge people by the quality of their output, not by whether they work in the exact same style as everyone else. — George Kailas, Prospero.ai

16. IDENTIFY BARRIERS AND DEVELOP PRACTICAL SOLUTIONS

I believe workplaces should focus on adapting their environments, communication, and training to different cognitive, physical, and learning needs. This matters because providing the same resources to everyone is not always enough. Employees may need accessible technology, flexible schedules, or different ways to receive information and demonstrate their abilities. Businesses should involve their teams in identifying barriers and developing practical solutions. Today, workplace inclusion should not be treated simply as a compliance issue. It should be part of how organizations develop talent and support performance. — Manuel Freire-Garabal, Gioya HEI

17. DON’T WAIT FOR AN ACCOMMODATION ASK

The best workplaces don’t wait for someone to ask for an accommodation. They design the workplace with differences in mind, recognizing that people process information, learn, and communicate in unique ways. When we reduce barriers, we enable people to spend less energy navigating the workplace and more energy contributing their talents, hopefully making the workplace better for everyone. — Namrata Yadav, Truist Foundation

18. FOSTER A CULTURE OF EMPATHY AND OPEN ACCESS

Supporting diverse needs requires shifting from only reactive accommodations to intentional workplace design. At The Trevor Project, accessibility is central to our core value of belonging. Part of why we have embraced remote work is that we’ve learned it is invaluable for accommodating disabilities, chronic illness, and learning differences. While no organization can proactively anticipate every requirement, leaders must foster a culture of empathy and open access where employees feel comfortable and empowered to request the individualized support they need to thrive. — Jaymes Black, The Trevor Project

Singapore Airlines Unveils the ‘Largest Double Bed in the Sky’ As Part of a $1.6 Billion Redesign

Getting a good night's sleep on a long-haul flight isn't easy, even when you're lucky enough to be flying first class. But Singapore Airlines—consistently voted as one of the best airlines in the world by CNT readers—is taking things to a whole new level, unveiling what it claims will be “the largest double bed in commercial aviation” as part of a major revamp of its long-haul cabins.

The airline announced the $1.6 billion redesign on Friday, November 6, with the first aircraft expected to enter service in the second half of 2027.

Singapore Airlines's new first class cabin

The biggest change is coming to first class, where passengers traveling together will be able to transform two adjoining seats into a double bed measuring 80 inches long and 60 inches wide—the same size as a standard queen bed in the US. The seats can also be converted into a two-person sofa, while curved sliding doors and retractable dividers will offer additional privacy.

And the luxury doesn't stop there. The new suites will feature Italian Poltrona Frau leather upholstery, personal wardrobes, dedicated dining spaces, and 32-inch 4K entertainment screens. Passengers can also expect proper bedding, including cotton sheets, duvets, a choice of pillows, and mattress toppers from German bedding specialist Paradies.

London’s Fractile raises $720M for memory-first AI chips and hits $7B valuation

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

London-based chip designer Fractile has raised $720 million in a Series C round, betting that memory bandwidth, rather than raw computing power, is the main constraint holding back AI performance.

The funding values the four-year-old company at $7 billion, according to the Financial Times, which cited people familiar with the deal.

Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed, and Redpoint led the round. Other investors include Alphabet’s CapitalG, DST Global, BlackRock, Bain Capital Ventures, T. Rowe Price, Shopify Ventures, Balderton, the UK’s Sovereign AI fund, and the British Business Bank.

The valuation is a sharp jump from May, when Fractile raised around $220 million at a valuation of roughly $1 billion. The new figure puts it among the UK’s most valuable startups, alongside companies such as Revolut.

The Financial Times also reports that Anthropic has expressed interest in becoming an early customer for Fractile’s chips, citing people familiar with the discussions.

Founder Walter Goodwin’s argument starts with a growing imbalance between computing power and memory bandwidth.

Over the past 25 years, chip performance has increased roughly a millionfold, while memory bandwidth has grown only about 40 times. That leaves a 25,000-fold gap between the amount of data processors can calculate and how quickly they can access it.

Goodwin told the FT that today’s frontier models are “drinking through a straw”.

Fractile’s proposed solution combines custom DRAM and logic in a three-dimensional stack. The company says this architecture can deliver substantially higher bandwidth than high-bandwidth memory (HBM), while retaining enough capacity to run large models with long context windows. Goodwin claims it could make inference, the process of generating responses to user queries, up to 10 times faster.

Those performance gains have yet to be demonstrated in a production chip. Fractile has not released a commercial product, and its first chip is planned for next year. For now, the $7 billion valuation is based on its technology and prototypes rather than a product in the market.

The company is also trying to avoid a growing constraint in the AI supply chain. Many competing chipmakers rely on HBM to move data quickly between memory and processors.

The technology is expensive, and demand from AI companies is putting pressure on suppliers such as SK Hynix and Micron.

Fractile is entering an increasingly crowded market. Nvidia acquired Groq last year and launched its own inference chip in March, while startups including Etched, MatX, and fellow British company Olix are developing alternatives.

The opportunity, according to Thrive Capital’s Philip Clark, is an inference market that could soon become “at least as large as the market Nvidia is addressing today”.

He sees particular potential in AI agents, which need to run continuously for hours or days rather than respond to a single prompt.

The funding is also a significant moment for the UK’s effort to build domestic AI infrastructure. Goodwin described the government’s Sovereign AI fund as a driving force behind developing frontier AI technology from London.

Fractile also has offices in the US and Taiwan, however, and is building for a global market. Its next challenge is to turn its prototypes into working chips that can deliver the performance it has promised.
