Children were among the injured people seen being carried into a nearby hospital

Media linked to the Houthi movement in Yemen say at least 33 people, including women and children, have been killed in air strikes by Saudi Arabia.

There has been no comment from the Saudi-led coalition backing the internationally recognised government in Yemen.

Live video footage on Al Masirah - a television station run by the Houthis - has shown rescuers pulling bodies out of the rubble of several buildings in al-Shariyah, a town to the east of the capital, Sanaa.

The bodies of young children can be seen among those dug out from the ruins.

The television station has also shown a number of injured people being carried on stretchers to hospital.

Saba news agency, which is also run by the Houthis, said the attack was carried out by Saudi Arabia on Saturday. It added that 39 people had been injured.

The Saudis have led a coalition that has been fighting the Houthis for more than a decade in support of Yemen's internationally recognised government.

The war in Yemen had been dormant for several years but reignited as a result of the Iran war.

A truce began to fall apart when Saudi Arabia struck the airport in Sanaa - which is controlled by the Houthis - to prevent an Iranian plane landing there.

The Tehran-backed Houthis subsequently targeted Abha airport in Saudi Arabia, then began hitting Saudi ships that have been using the Red Sea off the coast of Yemen as an alternative oil export route to the Strait of Hormuz.

Major fighting has since resumed in Yemen between the Houthis and forces fighting for the Saudi-backed government of Yemen.

On Friday, an attack on a market in Taiz province, which has been the focus of much of the recent fighting, is reported to have killed 30 people.

The Houthis accused the Saudis of carrying out the strike, while government forces accused the Houthis of having fired a missile at the site.

The United Nations says more than 1,600 people have been killed and more than 300,000 displaced since fighting resumed.

The Saudis have regularly bombed Houthi positions, while the group has fired multiple missiles at targets inside Saudi Arabia, including airports and oil facilities.

Hours before the strike that hit al-Shariyah, the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a missile heading towards Riyadh.

A Houthi leader, Mahdi al-Mashat, has vowed that the group will take revenge for the latest attacks in Yemen.

Auto-detecting file types taught me guesses need an escape hatch

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 8 • Originally published at imapp.blogspot.com

I shipped a single endpoint that takes any document and returns Markdown, and the first week was mostly about the bytes before the conversion. PDF, DOCX, PPTX, XLSX, EPUB, HTML — none of them announce themselves reliably. Magic numbers lie, extensions lie more, and a PDF renamed .txt is still a PDF.

The lesson I keep returning to: when you auto-detect, you are making a guess on behalf of the caller, so the guess has to be inspectable. I added a detected_type field to the response and a way to force the parser when the guess is wrong. That turned silent corruption into a boring error the agent can correct on the next call. Most "bad output" reports weren't converter bugs; they were wrong sniffing.

The second lesson is that one door only works if the door tells you what went through it. Eight endpoints felt honest, but callers really wanted "give me text" plus metadata: source type, page/slide/sheet count, truncation flags, warnings. Agents chain these tools; they need the receipt, not just the result.

If you're shipping in this space, build the failure path first. The happy path is a demo. The unhappy path is the product.

Apache APISIX Admin Exposure: The Gap Between a 9180 Listener and a Key-Protected Control Plane

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apache APISIX uses its Admin API to change routes, upstreams, consumers, and other gateway configuration. The official documentation shows the Admin API on port 9180 and uses an X-API-KEY header for administrative requests. That makes the control plane an attractive asset to inventory. A public listener is not proof that the API is unauthenticated, and a missing key in a scan is not proof that the service is vulnerable. The useful question is how many assets can be associated with APISIX, and which of those associations are strong enough to inspect.

On November 8, 2026, ZoomEye count-only queries for this assessment used sub_type=all, page 1, and page size 1. The exact application fingerprint app="Apache APISIX" returned 0. The title query title="Apache APISIX" returned 11,114. A listener-oriented query, port="9180" && service="http", returned 3. These are matched asset counts at one collection time. They are not counts of vulnerable gateways, and the page-size setting limits returned records without changing the matched total.

The gap between the title and application counts is the first finding. The title result is much larger and may include documentation sites, dashboards, login pages, reverse proxies, or pages that mention Apache APISIX without exposing its Admin API. The application result is precise within the ZoomEye fingerprint vocabulary, but a zero means the selected fingerprint did not match this corpus at that moment. The listener query is narrower but still only identifies an HTTP service on a commonly documented port.

That distinction matters during triage. An asset with the product title but no Admin API listener should not be treated as a control-plane exposure without another signal. An asset on 9180 should not be assumed to be APISIX merely because the vendor documents that port. The matching application fingerprint, certificate data, response headers, and a safe non-destructive request can help classify the asset. Missing evidence should move the asset to "unknown", not to "confirmed vulnerable".

The authenticated control plane needs a separate ownership model. APISIX documentation describes an Admin key and access-control settings such as allow_admin. Teams should restrict the listener to management networks, require TLS and a strong key, rotate keys on a planned schedule, and review whether the key is present in configuration management or logs. An internet-facing Admin API should have a documented exception and monitoring for configuration changes.

ZoomEye is useful here as an inventory source, not as a vulnerability oracle. The title query can find candidate deployments. The port query can find exposed listeners that need ownership confirmation. The application query can support a clean asset match where the fingerprint is available. A team should preserve the query, collection time, type scope, count, and the reason each asset was selected. If a query returns an error, it should remain an error rather than being recorded as zero.

The next step is to join external observations with internal evidence. Compare the candidate address with the gateway inventory, certificate inventory, DNS ownership, cloud account, and change tickets. Use an authenticated, authorized request from a controlled network to check the service version and configuration. Confirm whether the Admin API is bound to localhost, a management interface, or a public address. Confirm whether the key is enforced before any route-changing endpoint is reachable.

A count alone cannot answer those operational questions. It can tell the team where to look and how the public fingerprint differs from the internal inventory. APISIX's Admin API is powerful because it changes traffic behavior. That makes the exposure review an identity and network-control problem, not a search-result problem.

AI Ships Fast, but Focus Drifts

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

One incident still sticks with me. Our application services have to update a batch of data in shared memory atomically. That memory is managed centrally by the system: cross-process locks, memory ordering, and version switching are all wrapped inside the .so library, and callers reach it through a single shared header.

At the time, though, the library offered no interface for "atomic bulk update". The AI's approach was about as blunt as it gets: grab the physical address through mmap, overwrite field by field according to its own reading of the layout, and casually invent a flag on the side to serve as a "currently being modified" lock.

In a single process it ran fine, and the tests passed. The trouble is that what it bypassed was not an ordinary function but an entire system contract: the cross-process lock no longer held, the memory-ordering guarantees were gone, and any other process reading that memory could hit half-written garbage at any moment. Worse, the bypass leaves no trace. When the library later needs auditing or a memory layout change, those "ghost call sites" poking straight at physical addresses will be impossible to track down.

The AI wasn't out to sabotage anything. It simply took the path of least resistance when nothing provided the capability it needed. And that path happened to water down the contract "atomicity is guaranteed by the layer below" into "every call site makes up its own guarantee".

The longer I use it, the more of these moments I collect: locally flawless, globally full of holes. They tend to show up in three ways:

First, abstraction boundaries collapse. Like the mmap case above. In ordinary development, when a library lacks an interface, the caller and the provider sit down and agree on where the boundary is and how to define it. The AI makes no such judgement; wherever the rules don't reach, it fills in the gap itself.

Second, nothing accumulates in the foundation layer. An experienced engineer will pull common logic into a shared class along the way. AI tends to solve in place: this feature needs it, so add a method to the current file; the next feature needs it too, so write it again in another file. Nobody breaks the foundation layer — it simply never gets built.

Third, layered conventions get ignored. In any moderately complex system, technical constraints come in layers. Take the industrial control and data acquisition system we build: the platform layer is strict C++11 with exceptions disabled, while the application layer above it may use C++14. That means when creating a smart pointer, the platform layer has to write std::unique_ptr p(new T(args...)) by the book, while the application layer can use std::make_unique. To the AI, though, the whole repository is flat. The gcc in the dev environment is new enough that make_unique slips into the platform layer and still compiles; the problem only surfaces at integration testing.

A person might remember that C++11 rule. What a person can't do is spend an afternoon driving the AI through a dozen-plus sessions and five features in quick succession, and still remember which cell of the layering diagram the current session is sitting in.

Pull these observations together and they point at one thing: when AI coding spans sessions, the contracts between features lose their carrier.

System software is inevitably assembled from hundreds or thousands of features. You can't opt out of contracts; you can only choose whether to write them down, or to keep them in your head and carry them in prompts. Experience shows the head doesn't retain them, and stuffing them into prompts is a long-shot bet — miss one layer of context and you have a production incident.

Someone might ask: aren't SDD (spec-driven development) and Code Plan First (plan-first mode) meant to do exactly this?

No. The reach of both patterns stops at "get the current task right". You spell out the interface rules in session 1's spec, and nothing guarantees the AI still remembers them in session 50. You can, of course, copy "must go through the library's single entry point" and "no make_unique in the platform layer" into every feature's detailed design, over and over. That is unreasonable to expect of anyone, and it will inevitably miss something. A problem that sits at the boundary of a pattern can't be patched by "using the tool better".

Traditional documentation is even less help. It gets split by role into isolated islands of requirements, architecture, and testing, in wildly varied formats — I have seen companies whose requirements docs are PowerPoint decks because that makes them easier to present upward. A few rounds of code iteration later, the documents contradict each other. You can't ask an AI to consult a pile of stale dead artifacts like that.

So the problem is not AI's comprehension or its output quality — those have actually exceeded my expectations. To put it plainly: AI is not the problem. Our way of organizing is. Our current model of project management cannot keep up with this new productivity.

To hold the contracts between features in system software, we need an independent carrier that outlives a session — one that both a person and an AI can read precisely, and whose structure makes explicit which rule governs which layer.

So along what dimension should this long-lived document be organized, if we don't want to repeat the same mistake?

Jurassic Park Meets Fallout In New Steam Game You Can Try For Free

There is, frustratingly, a severe lack of games like Fallout. The series has been burdened with a reluctant ownership of the retrofuturism aesthetic and the first-person shooter RPG subgenre, something it, unfortunately, has utilized to poor effect on account of their having been only two Fallout titles released in the past decade. Beyond the likes of Atomfall and The Outer Worlds, there have been too few Fallout-likes to satiate the endless appetite of fans smitten with its alluring blend of 60s Americana and the apocalypse.

Fortunately, while the trickle is painfully slow, there are a handful of games like Fallout slipping through the cracks in the hopes of usurping Bethesda's legendary RPG or, at the very least, offering fans an alternative. One such title is a brand-new first-person game that blends Fallout with Jurassic Park to surprisingly good effect, delivering a truly unique experience that prospective fans can try for free on Steam. If you've been longing for a game like Fallout, this may just be the title for you.

In lieu of a Fallout 5 release date announcement or really any news regarding a new Bethesda release, fans are understandably turning to indies and AA experiences. Fortunately, Wilderdark, the aforementioned Jurassic Park meets Fallout game, aims to appease those fans, so long as they don't mind a little bit of Dino Crisis-esque horror thrown in for good measure. Wilderdark is a first-person title focused on investigating an island inhabited by dinosaurs, all while desperately attempting to survive.

Much like how Atomfall veered more toward enhancing Fallout's focus on survival, Wilderdark leans closer to being a survival horror experience than a full-blown RPG. Indeed, in many ways, it appears to have beaten the upcoming survival horror-focused Jurassic Park game to the punch, thus appeasing the fanbases of two criminally underserved franchises. Wilderdark, naturally, also leans further into the horror elements of Fallout, leveraging its developer, Team Junkfish's expertise in the survival horror genre to deliver some genuine scares.

In Wilderdark, you explore assorted areas in search of evidence and samples, all while avoiding the blighted dinosaurs roaming around. You'll need to take photos using your trusty camera, gather resources in order to survive, and pay close attention to your surroundings as you piece together the mystery of the island and its prehistoric inhabitants. Team Junkfish's last survival horror title, Monstrum, proved it understands the genre, and Wilderdark looks set to further its ambitions of delivering cinematic horrors.

If Wilderdark sounds even remotely interesting, you can try it for free via its official Steam store page. The available demo offers a small slice of the overall experience, ostensibly serving as a proof of concept for the full game. Some users have reported that the demo is riddled with bugs and crashes frequently, which is something to take into account should you encounter any issues. The full game doesn't have a release date yet, but one can hope that this Fallout-like title ends up releasing soon, if only so that fans have something to enjoy while awaiting Fallout 5.

Paramount’s 4-Part Action Fantasy Epic Has One of the Best Seasons in TV History

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With one of the best villains of all time

Few things feel more grown-up than dealing with the consequences of your actions. Whether you stay up too late and still have to get up the next day or make a mistake at work and have to answer for it, adulthood means no one’s coming to fix things. For the most part, it’s all up to you.

The Legend of Korra has been the subject of much criticism over the years, but it’s always been a show about its young hero Korra (Janet Varney) growing from a feisty teen into a relatively less feisty adult. It’s not until season 3 that she experiences her biggest change, dealing with the consequences of one of her biggest decisions: leaving the spirit world portals open.

It’s one of the best decisions Korra makes, but it also creates her most thought-provoking villain.

Season 3 is appropriately titled “Book 3: Change,” and it picks up after the Harmonic Convergence, the planetary alignment that flooded the world with spiritual energy. After a great battle (literally, Korra becomes a kaiju via astral projection), Korra leaves the portals open, believing that people and the spirits should live in harmony together. It’s a decision with vast ramifications, from spirits adorably popping up throughout Republic City to the awakening of air-bending skills in random people, reviving a culture that had been nearly lost.

One of these new benders is Zaheer (Henry Rollins), a member of the Red Lotus, which is a secret anarchist organization whose mission is to disrupt world order and eliminate the Avatar cycle. Zaheer uses his airbending to escape his high-security prison and rescue his comrades, who are some of the coolest villains in animation. These include the lavabender Ghazan (Peter Giles), waterbender Ming Hua (Grey DeLisle), and combustion bender P’li (Kristy Wu).

Meanwhile, Korra and her team travel the world, slowly collecting new airbenders in the hopes of rebuilding the Air Nation.

“Change” is a blockbuster of a third season, taking full advantage of Studio Mir’s animation capabilities. It’s the one season the studio animated from start to finish, and it shows. While The Legend of Korra always had incredible fights, in this season, the bending takes center stage. Ming Hua offers a new flavor of waterbending; she doesn’t have arms, so she fashions several out of water, looking like a terrifying spider. P’Li’s combustion is accompanied by a high-pitched whine, with the energy launching like a rocket through her third eye. Her hair is braided, and she’s huge. On a design level, these are the types of characters you only need to see once to remember forever.

Increasing the sense of peril is the fact that the Red Lotus feels so adult and experienced when compared to Korra and her friends. Zaheer and P’Li are devoted partners. Together, these four are close comrades, with their experiences and crimes weathered on their skin. It results in some of the franchise’s best showdowns, often involving characters that were previously sidelined, like Tenzin (J.K. Simmons), who has a standout battle in the Air Temple, and Korra’s father Tonraq (James Remar), who comes to her aid and prevents her from being kidnapped by the Red Lotus.

Then there’s Zaheer himself. He is a blank slate, the quietest and least charming member of a gang of incredibly cool villains. He’s so detached from the world and so at home with his ideologies that he feels inaccessible — no one’s convincing this guy of anything. His one tether to the world is P’Li, a relationship that the season cashes in on in spectacular fashion.

In arguably one of the coldest moments in kids' animation, Zaheer kills the Earth Queen by taking the air out of her lungs. Her death throws Ba Sing Se into chaos. And while the series never backs away from his extremism, like every great villain, Zaheer makes a lot of sense. His beliefs cause Korra to wonder if maybe the world would be better off without corrupt leaders like the Earth Queen, who lives surrounded by obscene wealth and imprisons her subjects for her own gain while the people in her kingdom suffer.

When it comes to Korra, there’s so much breathing room when the show finally drops her love triangle with Mako (David Faustino) and Asami (Seychelle Gabriel). It’s a development that allows Korra to focus on other things, like how bad she is at talking to bureaucrats or her having to hold back when speaking with people that are incredibly annoying.

Revenge is a dish best served 12 years later

Korra gets to explore her dynamics with other characters, including Suyin (Anne Heche) and Lin Beifong (Mindy Sterling), two standouts in the franchise. She’s also frequently paired with Asami, with the two finally moving past the fact that they dated the same guy (even if they’ll end up falling in love with each other the following season).

“I think Korra was more likable in Book 3 because — but that was actually part of her arc that we’d set out to do — is that she was becoming more selfless, and it wasn’t just about her own stuff,” co-creator Bryan Konietzko told The Mary Sue in 2014.

By the end of the “Change,” Korra has saved the world once more, but is more depleted than ever. After being poisoned by the Red Lotus, she’s in a wheelchair, watching her past self’s civilization restored as the Air Nomads rise once more. Another adult lesson Korra learns? Sometimes life’s greatest moments are bittersweet.

The laundering trail from the huge October cryptocurrency theft attributed by investigators to North Korean-linked actors continues to evolve. In a November 6 update to its technical analysis, Elliptic said the value of stolen assets converted into Bitcoin had reached approximately $124 million.

The headline figure is striking, but the more useful story concerns how the funds moved, what remains traceable and why conversion into Bitcoin is not the same as spending money through the conventional financial system.

Elliptic’s investigation describes activity involving tens of thousands of wallets, multiple networks and an assortment of services. By its November 6 checkpoint, analysts had identified about 1,650 BTC held in new addresses following conversion from other stolen assets. The firm said roughly $50 million worth of XRP had begun moving largely towards Bitcoin, while stolen Solana tokens had also been swapped, bridged or mixed.

The firm presented this as an ongoing, rapidly changing investigation. An estimated figure tied to a timestamp should not be reported as a permanent total, and wallet clusters can change as further transactions occur.

Cross-chain movement complicates tracing because the deposit and payout sides of a transfer may occur on different networks. It can also make the trail harder to follow when funds pass through pools, aggregators and intermediaries. However, the public nature of blockchain transactions and the ability to connect wallet activity through known services can still help investigators reconstruct substantial portions of the flow.

Elliptic reported that approximately $2.85 billion of the original stolen assets had been frozen and about $3.11 billion remained in addresses linked to the theft at its latest checkpoint. The firm cautioned that only a negligible portion appeared to have been cashed out. Such estimates may change with later tracing, and asset values vary with market prices.

This matters because a criminal actor may hold large amounts of Bitcoin or Ether for some time before attempting to move value through brokers or exchanges. Public addresses can show coins being shuffled, but they generally cannot prove the real-world identity of each subsequent controller or the final ownership arrangement.

The distinction is especially relevant to compliance services. Screening against attributed addresses can alert businesses to potential sanctions or illicit-funds exposure, even when the funds have crossed networks. Yet address blacklists and automated risk scoring are not perfect substitutes for investigation and human judgement.

The evolving theft occurred alongside a different security incident affecting Chainalysis, another blockchain-analysis provider. The two incidents should not be conflated. Chainalysis described its own systems and credentials issue as unrelated to the Binance theft, while Elliptic’s work here concerns tracing funds from that separate exchange incident.

For operators, the lesson connects to the custody, counterparty and incident-response themes covered in NewsBTC’s reporting on digital-asset infrastructure restrictions. Protecting users involves controls over withdrawals, counterparties and incident communications — not simply watching one chain’s transaction explorer.

The next meaningful updates will concern verified freezes, attributed movements to identifiable services and evidence of actual cash-out. Elliptic’s data show the stolen funds are still moving through complex paths. They do not yet tell a complete story about who ultimately receives them or how much can be recovered.

Hank Brown, No. 21 Iowa defense combine to give Northwestern first loss at new stadium, 24-10

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

EVANSTON, Ill. (AP) — Hank Brown threw for a touchdown and ran for another, and Braeden Jackson returned a kickoff for a TD for the second time this season as No. 21 Iowa beat Northwestern for the fifth straight time and dealt the Wildcats their first loss at the new Ryan Field, 24-10 on Saturday night.

The Hawkeyes (7-2, 4-2 Big Ten) have allowed only two touchdowns in the past three games. They recorded six sacks and didn't allow the Wildcats inside their 26-yard line until the fourth quarter.

Brown was 16 of 21 passing for 126 yards with an interception after going 20 for 24 in last week's win over Wisconsin.

Northwestern has dropped two straight after a 6-1 start. The Wildcats (6-3, 3-3), who won twice at their temporary lakefront stadium before winning the first three games at their new $875 million home, were trying to move to 6-0 at home for the first time in 122 years.

Iowa, which scored on 98-yard drives in each of its previous two games, had a 97-yard drive Saturday.

Brown was 4 for 4 on Iowa's opening possession before sprinting for a 21-yard touchdown on a quarterback draw. He found Tony Diaz for a 24-yard TD catch early in the second quarter to cap the 97-yard drive and make it 14-0 after L.J. Phillips had a 45-yard run to get the Hawkeyes near midfield.

Phillips, who ran for 1,920 yards at South Dakota last season, had his best game in an Iowa uniform with 128 yards on 21 carries and two catches for 14 yards.

Northwestern's Nicco Marchiol, a West Virginia transfer filling in for injured starter Aidan Chiles, was 24 for 32 for 181 yards and one touchdown to go with 52 yards rushing, but Iowa held Northwestern to 2.8 yards per carry and stuffed the Wildcats twice on third-and-1 and twice on fourth-and 1.

Robert Fitzgerald picked off an underthrown pass by Brown midway through the third quarter to give the Wildcats some life, but the drive stalled and Northwestern settled for a 45-yard field goal from Jackson Kleather to break up the shutout.

Jackson returned the ensuing kickoff 96 yards to make it 21-3. He had a 99-yard touchdown return in Iowa's win at Michigan in September. Caden Buhr added a 52-yard field goal later in the third.

Marchiol connected with Joseph Hilmon II on a 2-yard touchdown pass with 1:51 to play.

Iowa: Linebacker Cam Buffington had 12 tackles to lead the Hawkeyes for the fifth time this season, his fourth game with double-digit tackles.

Northwestern: The Wildcats had been converting third downs at a 46% clip and were 5 for 7 on fourth down with the dual threat Chiles at quarterback this season, but Iowa held Northwestern to 1 for 11 on third down and 1 for 4 on fourth down.
