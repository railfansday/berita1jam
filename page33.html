The No Surprises Act's independent dispute resolution process cost employer plans $8.39 billion in 2025, according to a new analysis.

Researchers at the Employee Benefit Research Institute combed federal IDR data and found that these additional costs equal about 63 cents per $100 spent by employer-based insurance plans. The total includes $4.73 billion in costs incurred in the first half of the year as well as $3.66 billion in the second half of 2025.

Even when EBRI analysts used different thresholds to exclude outlier awards, such as costs that were unusually high, they still landed on an estimate of at least $8.02 billion, per the study.

The researchers note that this data doesn't necessarily paint the full picture, as it only encompasses cases that proceeded to IDR. The study doesn't include information on claims that were settled outside of arbitration, administrative costs, any changes in networks or provider rates or the cost associated with patient protections.

The study calculates additional cost by taking the IDR award amounts and subtracting the plan's initial payment and any cost-sharing from the patient.

"These new data provide a clearer picture of what health plans owe for out-of-network services beyond their initial payments, after accounting for patient cost sharing,” said Paul Fronstin, Ph.D., director of Health Benefits Research at EBRI, in the announcement. “Separating those amounts from arbitration awards allows us to measure additional plan obligations more directly."

The study also doesn't project the impact of IDR on premiums, and the researchers said that having greater insight into the administrative expenses and other ancillary costs would be necessary to understand how the No Surprises Act's processes may impact patients' costs directly.

The IDR processes have gained increasing scrutiny from health plans and plan sponsors as the number of disputes, and their associated payouts, rise. When a claim reaches the arbitration phase, the results overwhelming favor providers, as they won in 85% of disputes last year.

An analysis from Georgetown University, released in August, estimated that total costs associated with IDR reached $22 billion in 2025. There were 2.6 million disputes initiated in 2025; by comparison, federal estimates projected about 22,000 cases annually reaching the arbitration phase.

Providers have pushed back on criticism of IDR, arguing that the root cause is payers undercutting them during the opening negotiation phase by setting low qualifying payment amounts for claims.

How Kelly Rowland Keeps Her Eczema Flare-Ups Under Control During Winter

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Wintertime can bring everyone down with its colder weather and less sunlight. But for those with eczema, this time of year can wreak havoc on their skin, too.

That’s the case for Grammy-winning singer, actress, and Destiny’s Child alum Kelly Rowland, who has been living with eczema since her 20s. During the winter months, she often dreads the cold.

“[You] don’t know what the environment is going to give you—because mine is also environmental—or what the weather is going to give you,” Rowland told Healthline.

Cold weather and indoor heating can reduce humidity, drying out the skin and triggering or worsening eczema flare-ups.

“[What we know] about the skin barrier is that it really is like a gate or wall against the outside world,” Camille Howard-Verovic, MD, a dermatologist at Active Dermatology, told Healthline.

In healthy skin, the barrier controls what enters and leaves the skin. However, for people with eczema, Howard-Verovic said they have skin barrier dysfunction, which allows substances such as allergens and irritants to penetrate the skin.

Cold weather can increase transepidermal water loss, a process in which moisture escapes through the skin’s outer barrier and evaporates into the surrounding air.

“People with eczema have a tremendous amount of transepidermal water loss, so you’re just drier,” said Howard-Verovic. “[You’re] not holding onto the water as well, so that causes the itching and the rash that we feel when we have eczema.”

Rowland first noticed eczema flares behind her ears as well as on her elbows, arms, and the back of her knees.

“[It] looked like a rash, so that’s what I thought it was all this time, and I dealt with it for so long until after I had my son and then it started to get worse on my face,” she said.

She eventually went to a dermatologist who diagnosed her with moderate-to-severe eczema, a chronic inflammatory skin condition that can cause intense itching, painful skin cracking, thickened skin, and redness or swelling.

When Rowland learned that eczema can be genetic, it made sense to her.

“I remember seeing big rashes on [my mom’s] neck and her arms…so she had it, and I guess I was the lucky one,” said Rowland.

At first, she tried eliminating foods that she thought might be triggers. She also bathed in oatmeal.

“[I] literally have had a tub full of oatmeal, like literally went to the store to get store-bought fresh oats,” she said. “I tried everything, and it doesn’t help until you go talk to a doctor about it.”

At first, she tried prescription topical medications but found them cumbersome.

“I don’t have the time, if I’m being honest, and we live in a world that is moving so fast…I started buying like three tubes of whatever it was I was using and losing it; it was just too much for me,” Rowland said.

Rowland said she found relief with Ebglyss, a prescription injectable medication for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis. She has also partnered with Eli Lilly and Company, its manufacturer, to raise awareness about eczema.

Rowland said she’s sharing her experience to help others living with eczema, including people she’s met who have experienced greater challenges managing the condition.

“One lady was telling me about how she’s having a tough time [looking] in the mirror cause she doesn’t know who’s looking back at her, and she saw her skin before eczema and after,” said Rowland.

When she first had symptoms, she said she felt embarrassed, too.

“[Because] it was just in the middle of my arm space and…[if I] put out my hand to shake someone’s hand, you don’t know if somebody’s judgy,” she said.

Rowland hopes being open about her condition will encourage others to advocate for themselves and talk with their doctor about treatment options.

Because eczema can look different on deeper skin tones, Howard-Verovic noted that a dermatologist can help make the correct diagnosis.

“Some people may not even know that they have eczema,” she said. “We know that eczema is a rash; it can appear red on certain skin tones, and it can be grey or white on certain skin tones, so it can look a little different.”

In addition to medication, Rowland said managing stress helps reduce her eczema flare-ups.

“Before [when] I went on tour, because I was under so much stress, it flared up like crazy, like you wouldn’t believe,” she said. “We were in the back trying to figure out how to cover certain things up, and we figured some of it out, and some of it was really tricky, but what helped was when I actually just calmed down…not that it helped overnight, but it did just kind of bring the flare down.”

She said red light therapy, feeling grounded outside without her shoes on, getting vitamin D from natural sunlight, and cooking are ways she reduces ongoing stress.

“Cooking calms me when my kids aren’t here, and I have a really good [Zen] playlist on,” said Rowland. “[If] you ask me a question [in those moments], I might give you my heart; I’m just so happy.”

She also tries to cut back on sugar, which she believes triggers flares.

Another way to reduce flares, according to Howard-Verovic, is to avoid hot showers, especially in the winter.

“[We] know eczematous skin likes lukewarm water,” she said.

Because Howard-Verovic and her daughter both live with eczema, she set a rule about when it’s time to get out of the shower.

“[If] you see condensation on the bathroom mirror, you’ve spent too much time in the shower,” said Howard-Verovic. “[That] really hot, hot water feels really good in that moment…but when you get out all that water evaporates, and there’s no moisture in the atmosphere, and it dries your skin out, and now your skin is unhappy again.”

If you prefer to take baths, she said use gentle cleansers.

“[There] are bleach baths for really, really bad flare-ups,” she noted.

Diluted bleach baths are sometimes recommended for people with eczema, but they should only be used according to a healthcare professional’s instructions.

The Argentine has come close to becoming Manchester United manager on three separate prior occasions. Now the stars appear to have finally aligned, but is it too late for either the man or the club?

On Sunday evening, Mauricio Pochettino got the call he had been waiting for.

He and long-time assistant Jesus Perez had frequently asked about Manchester United over the last decade. They would often contact friends in the game who were connected to Old Trafford, looking to find out what was happening, whether there could be an opening.

Pochettino came very close to taking one of those openings on at least three occasions: 2018, 2021 and 2024. Now, elements might finally have aligned.

It is a call that others at Old Trafford have long waited for, too. Back when they had a much publicised dinner in 2016, Sir Alex Ferguson essentially told Pochettino he should be the United manager. The Argentine was enthralled at that meeting by stories from one of the game’s great glory eras, and a sense of magic he had felt just standing in Old Trafford had been deepened.

Pochettino had been especially taken by Ferguson’s account of rebuilding United from the 1980s, initially drawing comparisons with what he was doing at Tottenham Hotspur. Now, he’s set to take on that exact same job, if one that has become so much bigger than in 2016. It is arguably even bigger than in 1986.

Pochettino, however, is fired by the idea of being the man to finally fix United. It has become one of the great managerial quests. The United hierarchy have ultimately gone for him because of that personality, and his standing. They don’t want a coach who hasn’t worked at this level. They want someone ready. Both the Glazers and Sir Jim Ratcliffe can sense that, since the two sides of the United ownership have known Pochettino for years.

If this is an appointment that always felt destined, though, that does raise one big question. Is this the right moment for it? Is it like Jose Mourinho, in that it came too late? Is it typical of modern United in that sense? Is it the same old “magical thinking” of hoping a famous name saves them, when the game has changed and the actual problems are now so structural?

Pochettino probably should have gone to United in 2016 or 2018, when he was at a peak in terms of being one of the most exciting coaches in football. The club did consider a move in the summer of 2016, but sources say an exit clause was allowed to expire. How different things could have been.

Old Trafford insiders are now talking about the value of “a name”, but the hierarchy did pass on Pochettino in 2024, when they initially considered sacking Erik ten Hag before ultimately deciding to stick with the Dutchman. His interview wasn’t seen as convincing as that of Thomas Tuchel or Roberto De Zerbi. Those who know him also say he is closer to being “a manager” now, in terms of a wider outlook, rather than the on-the-grass coach that so drove Spurs.

The very fact United are negotiating to pry him from the United States national team, without wishing to be disrespectful, also illustrates how his stock has recently dropped. Some close to Pochettino would nevertheless put that down to necessary choices at certain moments. Taking charge of the hosts of the biggest World Cup ever, that had so much pomp around it, was too enticing. Before that, he probably should have pushed to go to United or Real Madrid in 2018 but he was convinced he could do something special with Spurs. He’d put too much into it.

And that does point to one of the other great twists of fate in modern football, where Pochettino’s profile could be completely different. His Tottenham team were genuinely one of the best in Europe over 2016-18, and one of the great Premier League teams. To achieve 86 points in 2016-17, on what was then only the sixth highest wage bill in English football, was genuine alchemy. It was immense overperformance, fostered by a side that could just overrun you.

We also now know that the wage bill of the eventual champions for that season, Chelsea, had in part come from signings that had been secured by payments against the rules. The punishment from that guilty verdict still looks weak. There is a strong argument that Chelsea should have been stripped of the 2015 and 2017 titles. Pochettino himself said as much in a wide-ranging interview with The Independent and other media in September: “I’ve said that, that we should win one title, one Premier League. We should have been awarded it… for sure, we deserve to be champions for that year.”

Had that justice been done, Pochettino would not just be a “name” who created an exciting Spurs once. He would be an English champion, and one responsible for one of the most historic titles given the much wealthier competition. A great institution would have won just their third title, and first in 56 years.

Both he and Spurs could have had their futures transformed, as well as their place in history. It would have been a modern equivalent of what Ferguson himself did with Aberdeen.

Now, he wants to follow Ferguson in a different way. This is the job he has been waiting for.

A certain apathy towards the US job was displayed in that September interview when he spoke about how his contract didn’t necessarily mean he’d stay the full term.

For all of that, and how their World Cup campaign ended in the chaos of Donald Trump’s disruptive interference in the Falorin Balogun case, there were very impressive spells when the US really looked like a classic Pochettino team. The way they ripped into Paraguay was reminiscent of Spurs 2016-18, right up to some of the flourishes. And that was in a much more limited and restricted environment than a club team.

This is the other thing about just taking the job, too. Regardless of United’s current problems, and perceptions of where Pochettino is in his career, he is energised by this. He has that bite. As one figure who knows him well says, “he has got the IP, he has got the brand, he has got the experience”.

Having already worked for Chelsea and Paris Saint-Germain, he’s been at big clubs with demanding owners. He has the mettle for that. And he now looks set to have the big job he’s always wanted.

Scout Motors has 212,000 vehicle reservations as $2 billion South Carolina plant nears completion

📅 2026-11-12  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

BLYTHEWOOD, S.C. — Electric vehicle startup Scout Motors has notched roughly 212,000 reservations for its upcoming EVs as the company nears completion of its new $2 billion plant here, CEO Scott Keogh told CNBC.

The reservations are for the Volkswagen-backed company's Terra pickup truck and Traveler SUV, which are expected to start full production next year before deliveries begin to customers in 2028.

Reservations do not necessarily translate to sales, as the U.S. automotive industry has learned during the past decade, especially with highly anticipated all-electric trucks such as the Ford F-150 Lightning, Tesla Cybertruck and others that garnered strong interest that didn't lead to meaningful numbers of purchases.

Keogh said he expects Scout could roughly double those reservations by the time customer production begins, but he said the more important thing is to convert early interest into actual sales as production nears.

"It's been a consistent drumbeat," Keogh said during an interview at Scout's plant site outside Columbia. "I think we could easily be up over 400,000 with reservations without doing too much effort. The big thing for us is how much of the initial production can we 100% presell."

Keogh said achieving an order book of 12 to 18 months "would be great" and is something he thinks the company can do before production. Scout on Thursday said plant construction is "in its final phase."

Scout's vehicles will be offered as all-electric versions as well as electric hybrid variants, known as extended-range electric vehicles, or EREVs, that include an internal combustion engine as a backup generator for the electric motors and batteries.

Scout, a former American vehicle brand from 1961 to 1980, was expected to exclusively offer EVs in a bid for the German automaker to expand its presence in the U.S. However, slower-than-expected adoption of EVs and higher costs led the company to decide to offer EREVs as well.

Keogh said extended-range and SUV models are dominating reservations thus far, with roughly 87% choosing EREV models and 75% being SUVs.

The CEO also said the company is drawing interest from its target markets, which include owners of off-road vehicles such as the Ford Bronco and Jeep Wrangler as well as full-size pickup trucks and U.S. EV leader Tesla.

Given the interest signaled by reservations, Keogh said the company is expected to launch with the EREVs first, followed by the EVs. He also said the company plans to launch with mid-level models first, a deviation from many companies that begin with their highest-priced models.

Both the Traveler and Terra are expected to start at under $60,000. The EREV models are expected to feature more than 500 miles of range, according to the company, compared with 300 miles of range for the all-electric models.

"The No.1 thing for me is I want to go get the best volume as soon as possible," Keogh said. "In my mind, it's how quick can you get to 100,000 units? It's at 100,000 units where you can start to punch through."

His comments come months after Rivian Automotive received criticism online for launching its R2 SUV with its highest-priced models first, despite that being a common practice across the automotive industry.

Keogh said Scout's South Carolina plant is about 85% complete, but supporting infrastructure and a $300 million supplier park continue to be installed.

Reservations for Scout's vehicles initially opened in October 2024, when it revealed the Traveler and Terra, which feature sleek, yet rugged, exteriors that pay some homage to the brand's historic models.

Jensen Huang’s affair with a woman U.S. officials say is a Chinese spy has drawn attention to the role of intimate relationships in espionage.

At the height of the Cold War, a top official in China’s intelligence agency said that his country would never use seduction to entrap a person.

He looked at the few female comrades in the audience. “Rest assured, we will not be asking you to go run beauty traps.”

Supabase vs Firebase: Which Backend Fits Your Startup?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at tjvex.com

Every startup reaches the same fork in the road. You have an app idea, a small team, and a launch date that will not wait. You need user accounts, a database, file storage, and realtime updates. Building all of that from scratch takes months you do not have. Supabase vs Firebase is the comparison most founders end up making. Switching backends later is the kind of migration nobody budgets for.

This post compares the two platforms on the things that actually decide a startup backend: the database, auth, realtime, pricing, and how hard it is to leave.

Firebase is Google's backend platform, and it has been around long enough to be boring in the best sense. It gives you a NoSQL database called Firestore, authentication, file storage, cloud functions, hosting, push notifications, and analytics, all under one roof. You wire up the SDK, and your app talks to Google's infrastructure directly. There is no server for you to manage.

The biggest strength of Firebase is how fast it gets a product out the door. A solo founder or a two-person team can ship login, a database, and file uploads in days, not weeks. The documentation is extensive, the community is huge, and almost every problem you hit has already been answered somewhere.

Firebase also scales without you thinking about it. Your app goes from ten users to ten thousand, and Firestore keeps answering. That is genuinely valuable when you are small, because your team is building features instead of babysitting infrastructure.

Supabase is the newer challenger, and it takes a different approach. Where Firebase is a set of Google services with its own way of doing things, Supabase is built on open source tools you may already know: PostgreSQL for the database, GoTrue for auth, and standard storage and realtime layers on top. It calls itself the open source Firebase alternative, and that framing is accurate.

The practical difference is that Supabase gives you a real relational database. Your data lives in Postgres tables with rows, columns, foreign keys, and SQL queries. If your team already knows SQL, and most backend developers do, Supabase feels familiar from day one. You can write a complex query, join three tables, and get exactly the rows you need, instead of structuring your whole data model around the limits of a document store.

Supabase is also self-hostable. You can run the whole stack on your own servers if you ever need to, which matters to teams selling into regulated industries, or to clients who ask where their data lives.

The database is the decision you will live with longest, so it deserves the most attention. Firestore is a document database. You store collections of documents, each one a bundle of fields, and you query them with the rules Firestore allows. It is fast for simple lookups, and it handles nesting naturally.

The trouble starts with relationships. A marketplace with sellers, products, orders, and reviews needs joins, and Firestore does not do joins. You end up duplicating data across documents, or making several reads where one query would do. That works, and plenty of successful apps do exactly this, but it pushes complexity into your app code.

Postgres, the database behind Supabase, handles relationships natively. Orders link to users, products link to sellers, and a single query pulls the whole picture together. For products with connected data, like booking systems, marketplaces, or CRMs, that difference shows up fast.

There is a counterpoint worth stating plainly. If your data is simple, like a chat app or a feed, Firestore is easier. You will write less backend code and move faster.

Both platforms handle the part every app needs and nobody enjoys building: sign-up, login, password resets, and social login with Google or Apple. Firebase Auth is polished and battle tested, with phone auth and anonymous sign-in built in. Supabase Auth covers the same ground, including social providers, magic links, and phone auth.

One practical difference is how user data connects to the rest of your app. In Supabase, your users table is a normal Postgres table, so linking a user to their orders or subscription is one foreign key. In Firebase, user records live in Auth and app data lives in Firestore, so you connect the two by user ID and keep them in sync yourself.

Both platforms offer realtime updates, which is how a chat message or a booking change appears on screen without a refresh. Firebase built its reputation on realtime, and it shows. Firestore listeners are simple to use and reliable. Supabase realtime is built on Postgres replication, which broadcasts database changes to connected clients. It works well for most cases, but Firebase still has the edge in maturity here.

File storage is similar on both. You upload images or documents to a bucket, set access rules, and serve them through a CDN. Supabase storage is S3-compatible, which makes it easy to move files elsewhere later.

Both platforms offer serverless functions for the logic that should not live in the app: sending an email after an order, charging a card, or cleaning up old data. For a startup, the honest summary is that both are fine. The functions you write in the first year will be small, and either platform runs them well.

This is where founders get caught out, so read the pricing pages carefully before you commit. Firebase charges by usage: reads, writes, deletes, storage, and bandwidth. The free tier is generous, and for a small app the bill can stay at zero for months. The surprise comes with scale. A chatty app that reads a document every time a user opens a screen can rack up millions of reads, and each one is billed.

Supabase charges by project tier, with a free tier and paid plans that include a bundle of database size, storage, and bandwidth. The pricing is easier to predict, because you are paying for a plan rather than counting individual reads. Heavy read patterns that would sting on Firebase are often cheaper on Supabase, since a SQL query that returns a hundred rows is one query, not a hundred reads.

Every backend platform locks you in to some degree. The question is how much, and what leaving costs. Firebase lock-in is real. Your data lives in Firestore, your auth lives in Google, your functions run on Google Cloud. Moving away means rewriting data access code and migrating users, which is a project, not an afternoon.

Supabase lock-in is lighter, because the core is Postgres. Your data is in a standard database you can export with ordinary tools, and you can even self-host the whole stack. That does not make migration free, since your app code still talks to Supabase libraries, but the data layer, which is the expensive part to move, is portable by design.

There is no universal winner, but there are clear patterns. Pick Firebase if you are a small team shipping a mobile app fast, your data is simple, and you are comfortable inside Google's ecosystem. It remains an excellent choice for MVPs and consumer apps where speed to launch beats everything else.

Pick Supabase if your data is relational, your team knows SQL, you expect complex queries and reporting, or your clients care about data portability. It is also the stronger choice when your product is a dashboard, a marketplace, a booking system, or a CRM.

In our experience, teams that choose Firebase for speed and later regret it usually regret the document model, not the platform. Teams that choose Supabase and regret it usually wanted a faster start. Knowing which regret you are more likely to have is half the decision.

It pays holders no interest and lives on several chains, including Ethereum, Base, Solana, Avalanche and Stellar. If stablecoins are new to you, start with how stablecoins work.

Axis Robotics' ODE Policy Threads a Nut Onto a Bolt, With Three More Assembly Skills Queued

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at x.com

Axis Robotics has shipped ODE Policy, a visual policy distillation method that teaches a Franka robot to thread a nut onto a bolt in simulation — the first of four assembly skills the company says it intends to convert into policies that act from camera images. The method is built around learning how actions evolve over time, an approach the company argues gives a distilled policy a more natural way to capture the continuity of robot motion. Axis Robotics describes the early results as strong.

https://x.com/i/status/2120078708879102220

Visual policy distillation is the practice of taking a policy that already solves a task — typically one with access to privileged information such as full simulation state — and compressing it into a student policy that must act from camera images alone. The student is what ships, because cameras are cheap, non-invasive and already present on most industrial cells. The teacher is what makes training tractable, because simulation can supply ground truth that a real cell cannot.

ODE Policy's contribution, as described in the company's own account, is temporal. Rather than supervising the student on isolated action labels, it learns how actions evolve over time, so the policy models the trajectory of motion rather than a sequence of independent decisions. That distinction matters most in contact-rich tasks, where the correct action at any instant depends heavily on the momentum and contact state established a moment earlier. Threading a nut onto a bolt is a compact example: the tolerance is tight, the failure modes are subtle, and a policy that reacts only to the current frame tends to oscillate or stall.

The demonstration reproduces a task the company attributes to SGS, run in simulation on a Franka arm. Axis Robotics says the next step is to widen the set of skills distilled this way, naming gear fitting, peg insertion and connector joining alongside the nut-threading result.

The company's own figures describe a narrow proof of concept and the broader plan behind it.

The ratio is the interesting part. One skill is shown; three more are named in the same breath. Those three are not variations on a theme — gear fitting, peg insertion and connector joining are distinct contact regimes with different failure modes, different tooling and different tolerance budgets. A distillation method that generalises across all four would be doing something meaningfully different from a method tuned to one task.

Contact-rich assembly is the part of factory automation that has resisted the economics of scale. Welding, painting and palletising became commodity robot applications because the motion is repeatable and the sensing requirements are modest. Inserting a peg or seating a connector is not: it demands force awareness, compliance and error recovery, and the traditional answer has been bespoke engineering per part, per fixture, per tolerance. That is why assembly cells remain expensive to commission and slow to change over.

If a policy can be distilled from simulation into a camera-only controller, the marginal cost of adding a new skill shifts from mechanical engineering to data generation and training. Cameras replace instrumented fixtures. Simulation replaces physical trial and error. The cost curve bends in the direction that has made every other branch of machine learning cheaper: more skills per unit of engineering, not more engineering per skill.

The competitive implication is a squeeze on two fronts. Generalist robot foundation model efforts argue that one large policy should absorb many tasks; task-specific distillation argues that narrow, well-supervised policies will reach production reliability first. Axis Robotics is placing a bet on the second, with a method that tries to import the temporal structure of motion rather than relearning it from scratch.

The open question is the one the demo does not answer. Everything shown runs in simulation, and the sim-to-real gap is where contact-rich policies have historically failed — friction models, sensor noise, and the small deformations that simulation approximates poorly. Distilling to camera images is the right target, but evidence that it survives contact with hardware will come from the next set of skills, not this one.
