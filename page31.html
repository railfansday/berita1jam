The most recognizable spam email type is way older than the internet

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From a Spanish prisoner to a Nigerian princes, this familiar email scam has a surprisingly long paper trail.

Your email's spam folder has most likely seen a Nigerian prince or two pay a visit. These types of emails often claim to come from people with a tremendous fortune, and conveniently enough, all of them have issues accessing their wealth. If you're kind enough to cover the fees first, they generously offer to share the wealth with you. To nobody's surprise, the promised millions never reach your bank account, but the requests for more money probably will.

Infamously known as the Nigerian prince or 419 scam (a nod to Nigeria's criminal code), it's a typical advance-fee fraud that has become an internet meme of sorts. The perpetrator might pose as royalty trying to claim their inheritance. Other times, it's some wealthy banker or a government official who wants to move funds out of the country. Although the details change, the art of the steal doesn't: pay upfront for a fortune that isn't there.

The interesting part is that this scam predates email. Different versions of the same con were out in the wild as physical letters long before computers made mass messaging easier (and cheaper). In fact, the scheme had already been around for decades when the first widely recognized spam message was sent over ARPANET in 1978 to advertise computers.

Long before the Nigerian prince scam showed up, one of its earliest documented relatives was the Spanish Prisoner scheme. The script was similar: someone stuck in a foreign prison needing financial help to recover their "hidden fortune." The unsuspecting recipient was asked to reply via telegram. For being a good Samaritan, they'd supposedly get rewarded with a pretty handsome cut of that nonexistent fortune.

Surprisingly, one such letter showed up all the way back in 1897 in an Australian Police Gazette, offering £25,000 for helping recover £75,000 in bank checks. Apparently, all you needed was a convincing story and the hope that an impressionable victim would fall for it.

The "royal" approach was already in play by 1949. According to a National Archives blog, a 16-year-old Nigerian calling himself Prince Richard asked Americans to send clothes and money in exchange for jewelry and other goods. Your Highness reportedly went silent once the requested items arrived, and unsurprisingly, investigators found no connection to Nigerian royalty.

This trick wasn't exactly unique either. A 14-year-old Nigerian going by the name Prince Bil Morrison promised ivory and jewels in exchange for cash or clothes, per the National Archives. According to investigators, he adopted the fancy title "just for fun's sake." US postal officials issued fraud orders against both teens, but records don't say what became of the youth.

By the 1980s, Nigerian advance-fee letters were gaining traction internationally, and as email spread in the 1990s, scammers could cut the cost of envelopes, postage and international delivery. While the story barely changed, it was a lot easier to reach people without using a single stamp.

These days, the prince has plenty of competition, with supposed lawyers handling unclaimed inheritances or bankers who really need your account details to release funds. If a stranger is promising you a truckload of money and asking for upfront fees or personal details, ignore the message and any links attached. It's best to report the message as spam or phishing, and then send it to the trash can. While blocking the sender does help with that specific email address, the next attempt could simply come from somewhere else.

Even though email is a lot cheaper and easier, scammers haven't given up on physical mail. As of July 2026, the FTC was still warning consumers about letters supposedly sent by law firms offering shares of unclaimed life insurance policies worth millions. You guessed it: The letters promised recipients a large share of the funds, but the supposed lawyers were really after financial information or cash.

GTA 6 Parody In Binding of Isaac Used AI Without Creator's Knowledge

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A GTA 6 parody within The Binding of Isaac: Repentance+ was sullied by the use of AI-generated music

The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus was recently at the center of gaming's latest generative AI controversy. Creator Edmund McMillen claimed music made with generative AI was used without his knowledge for a new daily run riffing on Grand Theft Auto 6. Repentance Plus was released in full on Nov. 19 alongside its new console ports.

A Nov. 19 update for The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus on PC let players access a daily run inspired by GTA 6. It has unique music, lets players drive around in a cart like it's a car, and even implements GTA's star rating and cop chases the more that players kill enemies.

While this unique run was meant as a silly parody, playing on the fact that the console ports were going up against GTA, reception to the update quickly turned sour. Reddit user u/ShweetsStuff discovered that the run's special music was made with the generative AI tool Suno, since the songs still have Suno metadata in the game's code.

This discovery led to backlash and review bombing of The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus on Steam. After that, McMillen, the game's creator, finally responded on X, saying he "obviously" didn't know AI-generated music had been used for the daily run's music. "Looking into how that happened and getting it removed, stay tuned," he said.

The music was subsequently removed in a Nov. 21 update and replaced with tracks that presumably don't use generative AI at all. McMillen has voiced some harsh opinions about AI use in game development, calling AI-generated games "uninspired and bland" in a 2024 TikTok. Clearly, the implementation of AI music within the latest update was done without its knowledge, although it does shine a lot on the fact that McMillen is not the only one actively working on updates for The Binding of Isaac right now.

If you want to check out the now AI-free GTA 6 parody within The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus, all you have to do is use "V1CE BSMT" as the seed for your next run.

George burst on the pop scene in 1982, one of the first MTV stars with the New Wave hit “Do You Really Want to Hurt Me?” Challenging gender and cultural norms in an oversize hat, Rasta dreads, drag makeup, and a T-shirt that spelled Culture Club in Hebrew (Havurat Aguda), Boy George was an instant sensation and ruled the pop charts for years to come.

Four decades later, Boy George is still advocating for multiculturalism and LGBTQ+ rights, but his hometown of London feels less tolerant. Broncos Ring of Fame linebacker and Hall of Fame broadcaster Tom Jackson dies at 75

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Denver Broncos legend and Ring of Fame linebacker Tom Jackson has died at the age of 75, the team announced on Sunday. No cause of death was reported.

The Denver Broncos are heartbroken by the passing of Ring of Fame linebacker Tom Jackson, a cornerstone of the legendary Orange Crush defense and one of the most beloved players in franchise history. pic.twitter.com/MdcpkuyKYs

Jackson was a major piece of the "Orange Crush" defense, playing for the team from 1973 to 1986. He was selected by the Broncos in the fourth round of the 1973 NFL Draft and spent his entire career in Denver.

The three-time Pro Bowler played in 191 regular season games, with 177 starts, along with 10 playoff games. He also has two Super Bowl appearances, starting in the 1977 championship game, the Broncos first trip to the big game, and 1986.

His 20 interceptions are tied for the most by a linebacker in team history and his seven picks in the 1976 season are the most by a linebacker in a single season in Broncos history. The first-team All-Pro has a career 40.0 sacks and three touchdowns.

Jackson's excellence throughout his 14 years on the team made him a fan favorite and put him in the Broncos Ring of Fame in 1992.

After he retired, he stayed in the football world as a broadcaster. He was given the Pete Rozelle Award for broadcasting by the Pro Football Hall of Fame in 2015.

I can't imagine Home Alone fans expected this much Kevin McCallister news ahead of Thanksgiving. Macaulay Culkin is reportedly returning to act in the franchise that made him a household name. But just minutes before that story hit amid the 2026 movie calendar, Chris Columbus, the director of the first two movies, was explaining why he didn't think another sequel could happen. And Columbus has a pretty heartbreaking reason for that.

Per Variety, Columbus had no idea Disney was developing another Home Alone movie when asked about the franchise's future. Shortly afterward, reports surfaced about Culkin working with Seth Rogen and Evan Goldberg on a new installment. The project is in its early stages, with Rogen and Goldberg shaping the script and producing through their Point Grey banner. Columbus, however, kept coming back to one person who helped make the original movie such a Christmas staple:

Catherine, as I’ve always said, is 50% of ‘Home Alone.’ There’s the yin and yang of ‘Home Alone’: Catherine O’Hara and Macaulay Culkin. Without those two in the film, there is no film, because they create the emotional epicenter of that film.

He's got a point. We all remember Kevin setting traps for Harry and Marv, screaming after putting on aftershave and eating a ridiculous amount of ice cream. But the first film also follows a frantic mother trying to get back to the child she accidentally left behind. That film's final reunion is still one of my favorite parts, and the late Catherine O'Hara, who played Kevin's mother, Kate McCallister, is at least 50% of why it works so well. O'Hara died in January at 71, and Columbus believes her absence would be impossible to overcome. As he explained:

I think you potentially could still entertain the idea of doing another ‘Home Alone’ with the cast. Not any of these ridiculous follow-ups to the first. And I only say ridiculous because it’s weird with filmmakers. They don’t understand why that cast works so well. It’s always about the cast. It’s like ‘The Goonies.’ It’s about the cast.

Unfortunately, the reunion he pictured is no longer possible. That makes the timing of his interview particularly surreal. He continued:

It was really about Catherine and Macaulay, if anyone ever wanted to do a sequel, and unfortunately that can’t happen now.

As essential as O’Hara was to the success of the first two movies, the bumbling baddies also made the film unforgettable. I'm, of course, talking about the Wet Bandits, played by Daniel Stern and Joe Pesci. Columbus also wanted to make sure those two other actors got their due, adding:

Let’s not downsize Harry and Marv. Daniel Stern and Joe Pesci are the ultimate comic duo for that particular film. Everyone has tried in subsequent sequels to be as funny or as interesting as Dan and Joe, and they just had a chemistry that is undefinable. So you can’t do that.

I can’t argue with the longtime filmmaker. The subsequent installments have struggled to reproduce the burglars' chemistry. Persci and Stern are hilarious in these iconic roles and not one single actor in any of the follow-up movies holds a candle to their performances in both of the first two movies. Getting hit in the face with a paint can is only funny so many times, but those two made it an art form.

After Columbus directed 1990's Home Alone and 1992's Home Alone 2: Lost in New York, the series continued without Culkin in Home Alone 3, Home Alone 4, Home Alone: The Holiday Heist and 2021's Home Sweet Home Alone. 10 HBO Crime Shows That Are Perfect Weekend Binges

Crime shows have been a TV staple for decades, and the reason is fairly simple. The truth is that everyone loves a good mystery. The problem, however, is that the genre is so crowded that most crime shows have started to blur together. Too many of them lean on the same familiar formulas featuring brooding detectives with a dark past and last-minute twists that only exist for shock value. This means very few have the potential to leave a lasting impression.

HBO has always taken a different approach, though. The network uses the genre as a vehicle to explore broken families, corrupt institutions, and personal demons. In doing that, these series are never afraid to leave viewers a little uncomfortable, instead of offering them perfect closure. Speaking of which, many of them also tell a complete story in a season or two. Here is a list of short HBO crime series that make for a perfect weekend binge.

The Night Of shows just how quickly one bad decision can unravel someone's entire life. Nasir “Naz” Khan (Riz Ahmed) is a quiet Pakistani-American college student from Queens who takes his father’s taxi without asking so he can go to a party in Manhattan. Instead, he ends up spending the night with a young woman named Andrea, and when he wakes up, she has been stabbed to death. Naz is arrested within hours, and with no memory of what happened, his only hope is John Stone (John Turturro), a low-rent lawyer who usually handles petty cases.

What sets The Night Of apart from other crime dramas is how closely it follows the legal system itself. The show walks the audience through every step of Naz’s case, from the police station to Rikers Island and finally the courtroom, and it becomes painfully clear that nobody involved is really interested in the truth. Everyone on both sides is more focused on winning the case than on what actually happened that night. By the end, the question isn’t just whether Naz is guilty, but what the system has turned him into. The Night Of is slow and patient, and it remains one of the most gripping crime dramas HBO has ever made because it asks the hard questions instead of delivering a typical whodunit.

Mare of Easttown feels less like a crime show and more like an accurate portrayal of how toxic and suffocating small-town life can be. The series features Kate Winslet as Mare Sheehan, a Pennsylvania detective who is still grieving her son’s death while fighting to keep custody of her grandson. To make things worse, she is also haunted by a missing persons case she never managed to solve, which has led to the entire town growing increasingly skeptical of her. When teenage mother Erin McMenamin (Cailee Spaeny) is found dead in the woods, Mare is put in charge of the investigation, and every lead points back to people she grew up with.

Most crime dramas rush to the next clue, but Mare of Easttown takes its time to show what the job costs the person doing it. Mare can’t separate her work from her life, because in a town this small, every suspect is someone she has a connection to. The mystery is full of clever twists, but the show is really about grief and how people carry on after the worst has happened. It’s also surprisingly funny at times, especially in Mare’s prickly scenes with her mother, Helen (Jean Smart). Mare of Easttown is a tight mystery with real heart, and its final reveal is one of the most devastating in recent TV, and the fact that it manages to do all that in just seven episodes makes it all the more brilliant.

Big Little Lies is surprisingly gripping, even if it is a story about a bunch of rich, suburban moms. The series is set in the wealthy coastal town of Monterey, California, and opens with news of a death at a school fundraiser without revealing who died or who is responsible. From there, the story rewinds to follow this group of mothers whose lives become tangled together. Madeline (Reese Witherspoon) is outspoken and loves a good feud, while Celeste (Nicole Kidman) appears to have the perfect marriage. They soon befriend Jane (Shailene Woodley), a young single mom who has just moved to town with her son, and that’s when things start to take a turn.

The genius of Big Little Lies is how it hides a heavy story inside what looks like a glossy soap opera. At first, it’s easy to get caught up in the playground drama and petty rivalries between the moms. Then, the show peels back the surface to reveal the trauma and abuse some of these women are living with behind closed doors. The mystery of the fundraiser death keeps the story moving, but what keeps the viewer coming back is the messy, complex friendship that forms between these women who start as rivals. Big Little Lies is still airing, with Season 3 in production, but its first two installments work perfectly on their own for an addictive weekend binge.

Perry Mason tells the story of TV’s most famous lawyer and gets right into his gritty origin story. The series, set in 1930s Los Angeles, introduces Perry Mason (Matthew Rhys) as a broke, burned-out private investigator who is still haunted by his time in World War I. His life takes a turn when he is hired to dig into the kidnapping and murder of a baby, a case that quickly grabs the entire city’s attention. As the investigation pulls him into a web of corrupt cops and powerful church leaders, Perry starts down the path that will eventually lead him to the courtroom.

Fans of the classic Raymond Burr series might not recognize this version of Perry at first, and that’s the point. The show strips away the lawyer’s polished image and replaces it with a flawed man who has to earn his place as a lawyer, often by bending the rules along the way. The show’s version of 1930s Los Angeles is gorgeous, but the city feels rotten because here, money and power decide who is guilty. The courtroom scenes in Perry Mason are a masterclass in suspenseful storytelling, and they make the lawyer’s hard-won victories feel genuinely earned. Perry Mason ended after two seasons, but it’s a stylish, old-school crime drama that never stops feeling exhilarating.

The series is based on Jake Adelstein’s memoir and follows Ansel Elgort as the crime reporter who lands a job at a major Tokyo newspaper in 1999. Now, Jake is eager to prove himself and starts digging into stories his editors would rather he leave alone. Naturally, it doesn’t take long before he’s tangled up with the yakuza. Along the way, he teams up with veteran detective Hiroto Katagiri (Ken Watanabe), who is fighting his own battle against the city’s criminal underworld.

Tokyo Vice treats Tokyo as more than just a backdrop. The neon-lit streets and smoky hostess clubs feel alive, and the show takes its time explaining how the city’s unwritten rules shape its citizens’ choices. Jake’s fish-out-of-water story keeps things accessible, but the show’s best moments often belong to the yakuza themselves, who are written as complicated people rather than one-dimensional villains. The series is slower than a typical crime thriller, and with two seasons, it’s a longer binge. Still, Tokyo Vice is one of the most immersive crime shows on HBO Max.

The Penguin plays out much more like a classic mob drama than a typical comic book show, and that’s exactly why it works so well. The show pairs his rise with Sofia’s story, and the clash between two outcasts fighting for the same throne is easily the most gripping part of the story. Victor’s relationship with Oz adds another layer of complexity to the drama, and it’s hard to watch him slowly fall under Oz’s influence. Viewers don’t need to know a thing about Batman to get hooked, and by the finale, The Penguin stands shoulder to shoulder with HBO’s greatest mob dramas.

The Undoing is a glamorous Manhattan thriller about how little people really know about the ones they love. The Jinx is the only documentary on this list, but it’s every bit as gripping as any scripted show. The Jinx is fascinating because Durst himself is the most compelling thing about it. He is both awkward and strangely charismatic in his interviews, and it’s impossible to tell what he is lying about as he tries to explain himself. The series builds its case slowly, and by the final episode, the tension is almost unbearable. The Jinx’s ending is one of the most shocking moments in documentary history, one that actually had real-world consequences. The Jinx Season aired in 2024 to follow what happened next, and together, the two installments make the docuseries essential viewing for any true crime fan.

We Own This City comes from The Wire creator David Simon, so it had pretty big shoes to fill. Thankfully, the series stands on its own as a look into the true story of the Baltimore Police Department’s Gun Trace Task Force, an elite unit that was meant to take illegal guns off the streets. The show also spends time with the people who pay the price for that corruption, without ever feeling preachy. The story jumps between timelines, so it takes some focus, but the payoff is well worth it. By the end, We Own This City leaves the viewers angry but informed, and that is exactly what a show like this can aim to achieve.

The Staircase revisits one of the most debated true crime cases of the 21st century, and it somehow makes it even more fascinating. Michael’s children grew up loving and trusting him, and the show captures how painful it is for them to see their father in a brand-new light, not knowing whether they can trust him any longer. Michael himself is a writer who knows how to tell a good story, and the show cleverly leaves the audience asking whether he’s doing the same thing with his own life. The Staircase is less about the fall itself and more about everything that came after, and it’s the perfect show to close out a weekend.

Every Car Brand Has To Install Seat Belts, But Wearing Them Still Isn't Universally Mandated

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Buckling a seat belt is second nature, or it should be. Once the car door is closed, next comes that click of confirmation. But safety belts were introduced long, long, long after 1769, when the first car ever made came into existence. Not that seat belts were an afterthought; they simply weren't thought of at all. Not for cars, anyway.

Early iterations of the seat belt were developed in the 19th century and intended for aircraft. Seat belts weren't added to consumer cars until 1949, when Wisconsin-based Nash Motors Company introduced them. Still, not until 1968 were seat belts federally mandated for every seating position in passenger cars. This standard was later amended to include buses and trucks; however, this was merely a manufacturing requirement. Actual seat belt usage and enforcement were left to the states. Because of this, there remains one state that doesn't require adults to wear a seat belt: New Hampshire.

In 1984, New York became the first state to make seat belt use mandatory. The exceptions were children who, depending on age and weight, are to be secured either in a child safety seat or booster. Keep in mind that Volvo, the maven of automotive safety, introduced the three-point seat belt in 1959. Despite evidence showing seat belts not only minimize injuries but also save lives, automotive safety systems receive plenty of pushback, at least initially.

Following New York's lead, other states enacted seat belt laws throughout the '80s and '90s. Maine was the last state to make seat belt use required, putting its law into effect in late 1995. In the decades since, New Hampshire has remained a holdout, though its nonchalance toward seat belts is strictly an adult matter, to be fair.

All other states and the District of Columbia require front-seat occupants to buckle up. For backseat passengers, only 34 states and D.C. say it's a must. And kids being kids have different rules entirely. Some states necessitate seat belt use for front occupants only. Others require that each person be clicked in, regardless of seat location. For children, because standard seat belts cannot properly restrain them during a crash, we've got car seats, booster seats, and other safety equipment to consider.

The rules in New Hampshire do account for minors, requiring occupants younger than 18, including teen drivers, to be properly fastened. There are exceptions, though. Children don't need to be buckled if they're riding in a vehicle built before 1968 (or in a bus or taxi), have a physical condition that prevents seat belt use, or fall under certain special-education conditions. Everyone else? They take the state's motto of "Live Free or Die" to heart.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, seat belts reduce serious injuries and deaths by about 50%, and saved nearly 15,000 lives in 2017. Before seat belt use became law, only 14% of people wore them. Usage increased to 59% within a decade. In 2025, 92% of drivers and 90% of front-seat passengers buckled up, according to the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety. But in New Hampshire, less than 80% of travelers bother to buckle.

New Hampshire authorities are aware of this less-than-stellar safety record. The New Hampshire Office of Highway Safety (NHOHS) promotes "Join the NH Clique," which coincides with NHTSA's nationwide Click It or Ticket seat belt enforcement campaign. Law enforcement takes a no-excuses approach during the program, but they might as well be yelling at clouds from their front porch because it achieves the same effect. After all, police can't write tickets when a law hasn't been broken.

The state legislature has tried multiple times to introduce bills requiring seat belts for all, but every effort failed. A 2023 attempt was even soft-pitched as a secondary offense. Had the bill passed, actual usage would have depended on whether seat belt violations were treated as primary or secondary offenses. As of November 2026, just 35 states and D.C. have primary enforcement laws, and numbers support that reasoning. Nevertheless, whether old-timey lap belts or innovative multi-adaptive safety belts (another Volvo invention), no amount of safety tech matters unless people are compelled to wear them.

The National Safety Council (NSC) compiled NHTSA data from 2024 that showed seat belt usage at 91.2%, yet unbuckled occupants accounted for 48.4% of passenger vehicle deaths. Among 21- to 34-year-olds, about 60% of occupants killed weren't buckled. The NSC notes that in California — where seat belt use is a primary-enforced law for everyone aged 16 and up – observed usage in the front seats was 97.5% and unrestrained occupants accounted for 43% of occupant deaths. North Dakota, which only switched to primary enforcement in 2023, saw 79.8% usage while unbelted occupants accounted for 53% of deaths. The statistics from New Hampshire are more somber, showing only 78.2% wear their belts, while unbelted occupants accounted for 59% of deaths.

Jared hated his soon-to-be ex, Ashley, more than words could say. In his eyes, she'd used him for his money, cheated on him with his best friend, Miguel, and then dumped him like garbage. He was hurt, betrayed, and furious. Who wouldn't be?

Nan never expected her marriage to end. She'd always been anxious, but now the bottom had fallen out of her world. Had he ever loved her? Would the kids be traumatized? Would she lose the house—the one place she felt safe? Would she have enough money to live on? Could she end up homeless? Would she have a breakdown like her mother did?

Sean felt nothing when his husband left a note saying he'd moved out and wasn't coming back. He sat in front of the TV for days, barely eating or getting off the couch. Friends called, but he let everything go to voicemail. He was emotionally numb.

Although these reactions may seem extreme, they're not unusual after a sudden split or an ugly breakup. Normal, that is, in the beginning.

Divorce is one of the toughest transitions anyone will go through. Even under the best circumstances, it can destabilize your home, finances, parenting, friendships, and sense of identity.

Looking at divorce through Maslow's hierarchy of needs, many folks feel knocked down to the two lowest levels: basic survival and safety. The higher-functioning levels of love, esteem, and self-actualization take a back seat.

When you're dividing assets, potentially doubling your expenses, and perhaps splitting time with your kids, you're understandably more focused on getting through today than on what you might gain tomorrow.

The amygdala is a small structure deep within the brain that helps detect threats and trigger emotional responses. It's part of a larger system involved in our fight, flight, or freeze reactions.

Jared's rage, Nan's fear, and Sean's shutdown illustrate different responses to overwhelming stress. Clients have told me they feel like their big emotions mean they're "going crazy." I tell them they're probably not going crazy. They're being stretched emotionally, sometimes beyond anything they've experienced before.

Ideally, as the initial shock wears off, rage softens into anger, terror becomes manageable fear, and numbness begins to thaw. But not everyone moves out of survival mode easily—especially if the loss triggers previous unresolved losses or traumas.

Fortunately, another part of the brain can help: the prefrontal cortex.

Located just behind your forehead, it helps with reasoning, planning, impulse control, and thinking through actions and their consequences. It’s the part of the brain that asks, "Is this really a good idea?" “Should I say exactly what’s on my mind right now?”

Under intense stress, these skills can become compromised. You're more likely to react impulsively, see things in black-and-white terms, and seek immediate relief, rather than consider long-term consequences.

The amygdala and prefrontal cortex work together, but when our threat-response system is on high alert, thoughtful decisions become harder.

That's why people sometimes do things during divorce they'd never dream of doing otherwise. They send vicious texts, threaten to take the kids away, spend thousands fighting over possessions worth far less, or make financial decisions that hurt them more than their spouse.

These actions may feel justified in the moment. But there's a difference between reacting to pain and making decisions that serve you.

Just because your spouse sends an inflammatory text doesn't mean you have to answer immediately—or at all.

Your first impulse may be to defend yourself or fire back. Don't. Unless there's an emergency, give yourself time to settle down. Sometimes an hour is enough. Sometimes you need a day.

2. Distinguish immediate emotional discharge from long-term benefit.

There can be something satisfying about telling your spouse exactly what you think of them or making sure they don't "get away with" what they've done.

But that satisfaction is usually short-lived. The consequences may not be.

Ask yourself: "Will this help me build the life I want after divorce, or am I just trying to feel better right now?"

3. Consider your children's needs without disregarding your own.

Some parents become so consumed with their hurt that they lose sight of what their children are experiencing. Others sacrifice their own well-being because they believe putting the kids first means putting themselves last.

Neither extreme is helpful. Children need stability and emotional safety. They also benefit from parents who take care of themselves.

I've seen people spend more on attorney fees fighting over furniture than the furniture was worth. I know of one wealthier couple who spent thousands of dollars fighting over cleaning products (I couldn't make that up)!

At that point, it's clearly not about the item being fought over as much as it is about feeling wronged, wanting control, or needing to win.

Before digging in your heels, ask: "Will this matter five years from now?"

Some things absolutely are worth fighting for, including safety, your children's well-being, and financial security. But not every disagreement deserves a battle.

5. Be willing to compromise without abandoning yourself.

One of the hardest things about divorce is accepting that you probably won't get everything you want. In some cases, you'll feel like your spouse is getting more than they deserve. It can perpetuate, or even exacerbate, your pain.

Ask yourself if you really want to keep fighting, or do you want to be done so you can move on? That's not to suggest that you should surrender or let your spouse take advantage of you. It means recognizing that a workable resolution may be better for your long-term mental health than winning every argument.

Just a note about safety: When abuse, coercion, or intimidation is involved, safety and legal protection come first.

You're not going to feel calm and rational every minute. Divorce hurts. Sometimes terribly. You're allowed to be angry, frightened, or devastated. The problem isn't having these feelings. It's what you do with them.

Here's how to engage your higher brain: Before taking action, ask yourself:

"Am I trying to solve a problem, protect myself, or punish someone?"

Sometimes you're protecting yourself, and that's appropriate. Other times, you may realize you want your spouse to hurt as much as you do. That's human, too. But acting on that impulse rarely gets you where you want to go.

You may not control your spouse's behavior or how the divorce unfolds. But you can recognize when you're operating from fear, anger, or overwhelm.

The goal isn't to divorce without feeling deeply. It's to make sure temporary emotions don't create permanent consequences.

After all, divorce isn't the last chapter of your life. You still have to live with your decisions long after it's over. The intense feelings you feel are in your body, not theirs. How long do you want to live with those emotions hijacking your pleasure and taking away your power? Practice letting go daily. Join a support group. Get into individual therapy. Read helpful books. Meditate. Your power is in where you are headed, not where you've been. Keep moving forward.

I'm Raising My Fair Value Estimate for Berkshire Hathaway Stock. Here's What I Think It's Worth.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

My new number is around 22% over today's stock price. And one part of the business carries most of it.

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 22, 2026 at 10:23AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Berkshire Hathaway ›

I looked again at Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -0.47%)(BRKB -0.36%) on Nov. 17, and I ended up with a higher estimate of what the stock is worth. I now think the Class B shares are worth around $700 each, or about 22% over the stock's price near $575 as I write.

My estimate climbed mainly because of how CEO Greg Abel is managing Berkshire's money. He's been patient with the company's huge cash pile, while the businesses Berkshire owns outright keep growing.

The $700 comes from adding up four parts: the cash and Treasury bills, the stock portfolio, the operating businesses and the insurance operation. But they don't carry equal weight.

Berkshire's balance sheet does a lot of the work before any business is valued. As of Sept. 30, its insurance and other businesses had around $352 billion in cash, cash equivalents and Treasury bills (net of Treasury purchases that settled in October). Its stock portfolio was worth about $336 billion more. Spread over the outstanding shares on that date, that's around $166 a Class B share in cash and about $158 in stocks. Together, they cover more than half of today's stock price.

I'm counting both at full value, which is a bit generous. Berkshire would owe taxes on its $227 billion in unrealized gains if it sold, and the parent had around $20 billion in debt. Sure, taxes are due only when Berkshire sells, and it has owned some of its largest holdings for decades.

And Abel doesn't look to be in a rush to sell. Berkshire's stake in Apple has stayed at around 228 million shares for three quarters running, according to its 13F filings. Berkshire last trimmed it in the fourth quarter of 2025, Warren Buffett's final quarter as CEO.

That leaves around $377 of my estimate for the rest.

In the past four quarters, BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy and the manufacturing, service and retailing group earned about $25.4 billion after tax. And insurance underwriting added around $6.4 billion. Altogether, these operations earned around $31.8 billion.

At $700 a share, I'm valuing them at around $800 billion, or about 25 times their total earnings. That's about the same price-to-earnings ratio the S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) carries today, based on the last year of earnings.

At today's price, the market's valuing Berkshire's operations at just around 17 times earnings.

Put another way, the businesses are the swing factor. Almost all the gap between $575 and $700 depends on how much credit the operating companies get.

First, Berkshire's operating businesses are growing. Showing the momentum outside insurance, after-tax earnings at BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy and the manufacturing, service and retailing group rose around 13% year over year through September. The OxyChem chemicals business Berkshire bought in January helped.

And BNSF's pre-tax earnings climbed around 15% in that time, with growth picking up to about 19% in the third quarter.

Insurance is the bumpier part. Third-quarter underwriting profit dropped around 43% year over year, driven by higher claims costs at GEICO. That sort of swing is normal for an insurer, and Berkshire itself warns that its underwriting results can be volatile from period to period.

The bigger prize might be the float (premiums Berkshire keeps before paying claims), which stood at about $178.5 billion at the end of the third quarter. Through September, its average cost was negative, so Berkshire was effectively paid to keep it.

Abel's patience shows in Berkshire's trading, too. In the third quarter, the S&P 500 gained around 2%, and Berkshire sold a little more stock than it bought. Meanwhile, it spent about $9.6 billion on buybacks at average prices around $500 a Class B share -- two-thirds of its $14.4 billion nine-month total.

This restraint is also why I see Berkshire as a hedge against the artificial intelligence (AI) trade. Yes, Alphabet is one of its five biggest stock holdings. But most of what a buyer gets is cash, insurance, a railroad, utilities and factories, which might hold up better if AI spending ever slows.

In the end, I think around $700 is a fair value for a company whose cash and stocks alone cover more than half of today's price. The market is paying about 17 times earnings for the businesses, and I think they deserve more credit than that.

With the stock near $575, I'd be comfortable buying shares now and holding them for years. Berkshire is still my best stock pick for the next 10 years.

Memo To Musk: Denial Is Just The First Step On The Long Road To Recovery

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla CEO Elon Musk made headlines yet again last week, when he publicly denied any affiliation with US President Donald Trump’s “MAGA” movement. If Musk intends to stage a sort of political recovery, he still has a long way to go. Denial is just a precursor to recovery, which begins in earnest when you stop denying the reality of your situation and start admitting you need help from a higher power or some such. And in fact, a higher power is coming for Musk —  if not in spiritual form, then in the form of Congress, where the incoming Democratic majority plans to exercise its authority, and call Musk to account for manipulating SpaceX satellite services in Ukraine.

During an interview for the New York Times DealBook Summit last week, Elon Musk claimed that he was not affiliated with MAGA, though he did not apologize for supporting MAGA candidates this year, or for supporting Donald Trump in 2024. Musk simply said he “felt foolish” for wasting his money on losers.

In the same interview, Musk insisted — falsely — that Russia was the aggrieved party when Russian President Vladimir Putin invaded the Donbas region of Ukraine back in 2014. Justin sayin’…

For those of you wondering why Elon Musk’s politics should be a matter of concern to CleanTechnica, that’s a good question, and here’s the answer: Elon Musk sailed into clean tech history on the coattails of the EV startup Tesla Motors (soon to become simply Tesla) in the early 2000’s, only to emerge in more recent years as a champion of the far right alongside President Donald Trump’s notoriously racist, fossil fuel-supporting “Make America Great Again” movement, thereby setting back the US decarbonization movement by years if not decades.

Musk has also made his own contribution to the resurgence of fossil fuels in the US. His new xAI data centers are well known for deploying loosely regulated (or illegal) on-site gas generators, and his SpaceX venture has been gobbling up natural gas for rocket fuel while laying plans for future expansion.

So much for Elon Musk’s footprint on the US energy profile. Let’s move over to Ukraine, where rich fossil energy and mineral resources in the nation’s Donbas region prompted Russian President Putin to send unmarked soldiers — aka the “little green men” — to annex that portion of Ukraine in 2014. A ceasefire temporarily calmed the situation until February of 2022, when Putin sent a fully equipped convoy to the Ukrainian capital city Kyiv, aiming to take over the entire seat of government in one fell swoop — literally, in one day.

The effort failed, both militarily and in terms of Putin’s energy goals. Ukraine promptly cut Russia off from its electricity grid and hooked up with the European grid, a complex effort assisted by the Biden administration. Ukraine also set the wheels in motion to join the European Union as a significant source of clean energy, including green hydrogen gas alongside its copious potential for clean power including biomass, wind, and solar power.

To put that into perspective, consider that Ukraine is almost 90% as large as Texas, which has staked out a leadership position in wind and solar power among the 50 US states.

With Ukraine as a leading renewable energy supplier, the EU would have a fresh opportunity to meets its decarbonization goals on schedule while also reducing imports of fossil energy from Russia… And… Well… That’s where Elon Musk enters the picture.

Musk first emerged as a full blown MAGA supporter in 2020, when he helped Trump stir up conspiracy theories and confusion over the federal response to the COVID-19 crisis, in a failed effort to support the President’s bid for re-election that year. Had Trump won, he would have continued to implement a key part of his foreign policy agenda, namely, withdrawing the US from NATO, the North Atlantic Treaty Alliance. NATO formed in 1949 as an effort to curb Russian expansion in Europe, an effort Trump has upended on account of his longstanding friendship with Putin.

The election of Joe Biden in 2020 did not prevent Musk from providing additional aid and comfort to Trump. Musk used his media-grabbing public profile to undercut the authority of President Biden at every opportunity, including on the world stage as well as here at home. The stream of insults extended into January of 2022, even as the reality of a fresh round of Russian expansion into Europe began to materialize.

Musk had an additional opportunity to challenge Biden’s authority in January of 2022, when the so-called “Freedom Convoy” of truckers barricaded government offices in Canada, ostensibly in protest of a vaccine mandate. Musk gleefully cheered the effort, which disrupted transportation systems and distracted public attention in two key NATO allies — Canada and the US — even as Russia began ratcheting up its plans to take over Kyiv.

However, once it became clear that Putin’s one-day takeover of Ukraine was a failure, Musk about-faced. He took credit for providing “free” communications aid to Ukraine in the form of satellite services through the Starlink branch of SpaceX.

JK! A later assessment revealed that other sources shouldered about 85% of the cost of Starlink terminals initially supplied to Ukraine, including USAID here in the US (not a typo — that’s the US Administration for International Development, the same agency that Musk later destroyed in 2025).

Poland and other groups also contributed initial assistance to purchase  the terminals alongside an estimated 30% of connectivity costs. In addition, by September of 2022 Musk was making the case for US taxpayers to foot the bill.

Don’t just take my word for it. In 2023, the Belfer Center for International Affairs at Harvard recapped SpaceX’s negotiations over payment for its services to Ukraine during the previous year, concluding that “we can assume the U.S. government agreed to pay some, if not all, of the costs for continued operation of the technology.”

“SpaceX, and particularly Musk, was able to play the role of wartime hero, showcasing the power and value of the technology products the company developed, while simultaneously avoiding sustained and hefty bills,” Belfer Center observed.

US Department of Defense contract or not, by 2023 SpaceX was already making unilateral decisions that restricted Ukraine’s ability to deploy the technology, with SpaceX CEO Gwynne Shotwell publicly insisting that the company never intended for Ukraine to use it “for offensive purposes.”

Of course, “offensive” is in the eye of the beholder. Since Ukraine is the invaded party seeking to assert its rights over internationally recognized borders against Russia, one could argue that every action Ukraine takes is a defensive one until Putin agrees to concede defeat and cede back his illegal claims over Ukrainian lands.

Of course, that’s not how Musk portrayed the situation in his DealBook interview last week. Nevertheless, one also could argue that the US government, not the vendor, controls the use of contractually obligated resources. Be that as it may, Musk raised the bar considerably in October, when he — or Trump — or both — once again dialed down satellite service in Ukraine, exposing that country to further attacks.

Musk attempted to mollify bipartisan outrage over the debacle with yet another publicity stunt for SpaceX. However, service interruptions have continued into this week. Meanwhile, Trump has spent the same week cozying up to both Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping, aided and abetted by an obedient Republican majority in Congress.

That majority will evaporate, though, in January 3, which is just around the corner. The incoming Democratic majority is already gearing up for a fight. In a letter dated November 20, the incoming Chair of the House Intelligence Committee, Jim Himes (D-CT), demanded an in-person briefing from senior SpaceX officers, seeking an explanation for the disruption of military-grade Starshield service in Ukraine. As leverage, Himes warned Musk that a Democratic majority in Congress will do what the Republican majority failed to do, namely, exercise its constitutional authority over federal contracts.

Referring to specifically to SpaceX’s military-grade Starshield satellite services in Ukraine, Himes warned Musk that his entire suite of present and future SpaceX security contracts with the US government is at risk. “SpaceX’s potentially unilateral modification of a U.S. Government contract, combined with its lack of responsiveness to congressional oversight, calls into question every contract the U.S. Government has with SpaceX,” Himes explained.

“For that reason, it is difficult for me to continue supporting the authorization of funding for these SpaceX- developed, government-owned systems until I receive satisfactory answers to my questions about the Starshield service in Ukraine,” Himes continued.

Will Musk simply ignore the request for a briefing? After all, as Himes notes, Musk failed to respond to earlier requests. Stay tuned…
