Polestar has unveiled a concept that makes you wish it would quietly slip into production tomorrow. The Formula 2030 is low, wide, and muscular, with four doors and enough suggested practicality to help you justify the rather large wheels.

Unfortunately, Polestar has no plans to produce the Formula 2030, but some of what you’re looking at is headed for cars you can actually buy. So this is the first Polestar penned under the brand’s new design boss, Philipp Römers, who took the role in August.

The study measures 184.3 inches (4,680 millimeters) in length and stands just 53.1 inches (1,350 mm) tall, making it lower than the Polestar 5. For reference, that’s about as low as Audi’s last TT sports car, but the concept’s aggressively bulging rear haunches and low stance make it look a lot meaner and sportier.

It features plenty of cool, subtle details you may not notice at first. The headlights, for instance, sit within a front aerodynamic wing, while a single upright windshield wiper screams motorsport. Polestar has ditched the familiar full-width rear lightbar for separate “Single Blade” lights. The 22-inch wheels, splitter, and diffuser sport variations of the same "Nitrogen" exterior color.

Inside, four individual seats have inflatable cushions and bolsters that adjust around their occupants. Rear-hinged rear doors swing open for pillarless access, while exposed structural components and flax-based composite seat backs really highlight the use of this sustainable material.

The coolest thing about the interior, though, is the fact that the cockpit also physically changes depending on the driving mode. Switch to Performance, and the 12.2-inch center screen lowers into the console while the steering wheel and its attached 20-inch driver display move closer. Choosing Autonomous mode moves the seat and wheel apart.

It features eight physical controls beneath the center screen. You can customize their functions and choose different hardware, including rocker switches or rotary controllers. After years of watching buttons disappear and get replaced by touchscreens, we’re happy to see real controls making a comeback.

Polestar doesn’t say how quick this concept is or how far it can go on one charge—this is purely a design showcase. However, a lot of what you’re seeing here will likely make its way into the upcoming Polestar 2 and its crossover cousin, the Polestar 7. The new 2 is scheduled for 2027 to replace a car that recently ended production after roughly 200,000 sales.

The Polestar 7 will follow a year later, with production planned at Volvo’s Košice plant in Slovakia, using Volvo technology, including cell-to-body battery construction and next-generation electric motors. Shared hardware should help contain costs, while Polestar promises its own driving character with more emphasis on sportiness and driving fun.

Those launches come as Polestar recorded little global growth. It reported 44,790 annual sales through September 2026, up just 0.6% over the same period of 2025. Its record third quarter brought 14,371 sales, but deliveries outside America fell 8%. American inventory sales helped keep the worldwide total positive.

But American sales won’t prop up the brand’s global total soon. Polestar cannot sell model year 2027 and newer vehicles in the United States under the Connected Vehicle Rule, although it can continue selling existing earlier-model inventory and supporting owners.

Europe is therefore now the priority, making the next 2 and European-built 7 especially important. The concept gives those cars an appealing visual direction, but their pricing, capabilities, and execution will determine whether that translates into stronger sales.

Today’s consumers are spending more on things meaningful to their ‘life, health and happiness’, says Siam Piwat CEO Chadatip Chutrakul

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Retailers across Asia are trying to find new ways to boost sales, whether it’s by giving shoppers more reasons to visit a retail location, or by using new technologies to reduce friction for customers, shortening the gap between attention and sales.

Consumers are spending more on things “that are meaningful to their life, health, and happiness,” Chadatip Chutrakul, CEO of Bangkok-based retail mall developer Siam Piwat, said at Fortune’s Southeast Asia 500 Summit in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. “Retail is just a part of it… It’s not [only] about shopping, but creating experiential destinations.”

In October, Siam Piwat announced ICONPHUKET, a retail and entertainment development in Thailand’s Phuket province. Scheduled to open in 2029, the project will include green spaces and health and wellness facilities alongside shops, residences, offices and hotels.

“Shopping malls in Thailand—and I think in Asia—are like a second home to customers,” Chutrakul explained. “So besides shopping, we [also try to] offer everything else.”

Malina Ngai, CEO of global drug and beauty chain AS Watson, agreed that today’s consumers need a reason to choose one particular retailer over another. “Ultimately, the retail game is about relevance,” she said. “It’s not so much about whether you have more products or technology… customers are looking for a brand that they trust.”

Online, social media and influencers are pushing consumers to develop relationships with brands faster.

“[In the past], consumers built a relationship with a brand over [a span of] five years, but that cycle has been cut down to a couple of months now, because people start to adhere to a brand identity very quickly,” Saemin Ahn, managing partner at Rakuten Capital, the venture capital arm of Japanese conglomerate Rakuten Group, said.

Platforms also need to ensure views are turned into purchases. In February, Rakuten Ichiba—the company’s online marketplace—partnered with Google via the YouTube Shopping affiliate program, which allows viewers to tap ‘View Products’ during YouTube videos and navigate straight to Rakuten Ichiba’s webpage to make a purchase.

“In Rakuten Ichiba, there’s about 40,000 to 50,000 merchants that we have to keep happy, and those happy merchants keep the users happy,” Ahn explained.

Physical retailers are also using new tools to help shoppers find what they need. AS Watson is using AI to study how customers shop in different sections of its stores, from supplements to oral and hair care, Ngai said. The aim is to identify where shoppers struggle and change the layout or signage to help them.

“If we know that they spend about 56 seconds on this aisle, and they couldn’t find the product they need, we would have to improve how we arrange our products, put up signages, and work with our suppliers to make it easier for customers to make decisions,” she said.

A Tuskegee Airman’s Legacy of Generational Wealth—and His Granddaughter’s Mission to Expand It

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Julia Lashay Israel bought her first home at 19, never considering it a remarkable achievement.

Only decades later did she begin tracing that sense of possibility to a VA mortgage her grandfather, one of the original Tuskegee Airmen, secured after World War II.

"We never talked about the house. We always talked about the Tuskegee Airmen," Israel tells Realtor.com®.

Her family's experience stands apart from that of many Black Americans. Just 44.4% of Black households owned their homes in the third quarter of 2026, compared with 75.1% of non-Hispanic white households, according to Realtor.com research.

Israel, now a real estate broker and educator, has spent years teaching agents about the discriminatory housing policies that helped create that divide—and how their own practices can perpetuate it.

Ahead of Veterans Day, she shared her grandfather's story at the National Association of Realtors® NXT conference in New Orleans, illustrating what access to homeownership could make possible for generations of a family.

Israel's grandfather Sgt. Morrissey Whitley was just 20 when he entered the military aviation training program in Tuskegee, AL, in 1942—eventually serving in intelligence as a draftsman for the 332nd Fighter Group, according to Israel.

Just a year earlier, the U.S. Army Air Corps had begun admitting Black men after decades of exclusion, following pressure from civil rights organizations.

But the training program remained segregated, and the Tuskegee Airmen faced hostility, unequal treatment, and military officials who questioned whether Black servicemen were capable of flying combat missions.

Despite those systemic obstacles, the Tuskegee Airmen distinguished themselves in combat, helping challenge the racist assumptions that had excluded Black Americans from military aviation.

Whitley made sure his descendants understood their achievements, too.

When Israel told him an elementary school teacher had claimed Black Americans hadn't served in WWII, he assembled a folder of evidence for her to take to class.

But the racial barriers facing Black servicemen didn't end when they returned home.

And in the housing market, those divisions were reinforced by redlining, which restricted where Black families could obtain mortgages—and, ultimately, where they could build wealth.

In 1940, just 22.8% of Black households owned their homes, compared with 45.6% of white households, according to historical U.S. Census Bureau data.

And even while the GI Bill subsequently helped millions of returning veterans purchase homes through government-backed mortgages, lenders often refused to finance properties in predominantly Black neighborhoods.

On top of that, racially restrictive covenants excluded Black buyers from many white neighborhoods.

As a result, just 5.1% of VA-guaranteed loans in an early federal mortgage sample went to Black borrowers, according to a 2026 study published in Nature.

Whitley was among those who secured financing, purchasing a home in Gary, IN, after moving his family from Chicago, according to Israel—a move that she believes was made necessary because of discriminatory lending practices.

"You can't use your VA loan in Chicago," she says. "You have to live where Black people are redlined to live."

During the COVID-19 pandemic, Israel developed a course teaching real estate agents about the history of housing discrimination—including redlining, restrictive covenants, and the industry's role in perpetuating segregation.

She later taught it through Keller Williams' social equity task force, established after George Floyd's killing in 2020. Her training has since reached more than 40,000 agents, according to NAR.

What surprised Israel was how many agents were unfamiliar with that history—or how their own decisions could perpetuate its consequences.

"There's been different buyers, different sellers," she says of the persistence of residential segregation. "The only common denominator is the Realtor®."

Israel has witnessed discrimination firsthand.

"I've had people not sit in closings with us because we were Black," she says, recalling a Minnesota home closing in the mid-2000s.

Black applicants were 7.8 percentage points more likely than white applicants to be denied mortgages, even after accounting for financial characteristics and lender differences, according to a 2026 Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis study.

Israel also worries about agents discouraging buyers from pursuing certain financing programs or directing them toward particular neighborhoods without considering the long-term consequences.

Her courses emphasize the importance of understanding those barriers and earning buyers' trust, particularly when they're navigating a process nobody in their family has experienced before.

During 13 years working primarily with first-time buyers, many of them Black single women, she encountered clients who were hesitant to sign agreements or trust the professionals guiding them.

"A lot of times they are the first, and so if you're the first person who's ever done it, yeah, that's pretty scary," she says.

Israel grew up with a very different understanding of homeownership.

"My grandparents always had a house. My parents always had a house," she says.

In fact, she remembers envying classmates who lived in apartment complexes, where they could play together after school.

Her own children inherited that familiarity. Israel recalls her young son accompanying his grandmother to look at a rental apartment and repeatedly asking, "When's the closing?"

He couldn't understand why someone would pay to live in a home they didn't own.

It wasn't until Israel began working in real estate that she recognized how her family's experience differed from that of many neighbors and fellow churchgoers who rented their homes.

That early exposure can have lasting consequences. Children who grew up entirely in homeowner households were 18.4 percentage points more likely to become homeowners themselves by age 35 than those raised entirely in renter households, according to Realtor.com research.

Housing-related financial advantages can also persist across generations independently of what children earn themselves. Less than half of the persistence in housing capital across generations is explained by children's earnings, according to a 2025 U.S. Census Bureau study examining more than 3.4 million families.

Those advantages have been distributed unequally. Median white family wealth stood at approximately $332,100 in 2025, compared with just $36,800 for Black families, according to a Tax Policy Center analysis of Federal Reserve data.

Israel's own family never had substantial financial resources growing up, despite their history of homeownership.

"We never had a lot of money, but we always had a lot of sense," she recalls.

And when her granddaughter was born, Israel began reconsidering how far the opportunity her grandfather secured had carried the family.

"He didn't leave us any money, but he left us, like, a legacy," she says.

It was that realization that prompted Israel to begin incorporating Whitley's experience into her educational work.

"It's really not about quartz countertops or, you know, the cute fence," she says of the work real estate agents do. "It really is that we're planting seeds for families."

Her own grandfather had planted one more than eight decades earlier. And now, another generation of his descendants is benefiting from the opportunities that followed.

"This little baby is a trust fund baby, and she'll never know that it was because her great-great-grandfather had an opportunity that other people didn't have that set the path of this legacy for her," Israel says.

FDA warns of vision loss risk linked to popular GLP-1 drugs like Wegovy, Zepbound

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration is requiring new safety warnings on all GLP-1 drugs after finding evidence of a potential increased risk of a rare eye condition, the agency said on Monday.

The condition, which can cause sudden vision loss, known as nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION), is rare, and a causal relationship has not been established.

The FDA is requiring the makers of all GLP-1 medications to add a warning about the potential increased risk of NAION associated with use of these medications, it said.

NAION is a condition that can result in sudden decreased vision, including permanent vision loss. It is caused by decreased blood flow to the front part of the ​optic nerve — the underlying cause is not completely understood, however.

NAION cases have been reported for all currently marketed GLP-1 products, the FDA said.

Ozempic and Wegovy maker Novo Nordisk said it "continues to believe the data do not establish a cause-and-effect relationship, and we remain confident in the benefit-risk profile of our GLP-1 medicines."

CNBC has reached out to Eli Lilly, the company behind Mounjaro, Zepbound and Foundayo, for comment.

The FDA decision follows a review of clinical trials, real-world patient data, and published studies.

An analysis of 126 trials involving more than 134,000 patients found 6.4 cases of NAION per 100,000 patient-years among GLP-1 users, compared with 3.8 among patients receiving other treatments or placebos.

Several observational studies also identified an increased risk, supporting the FDA's concerns.

The FDA's class-wide labeling action is "likely to attract attention but should have limited impact on GLP-1 uptake," according to Citi analysts.

It may temporarily increase patient and investor concern, but the rarity of NAION and substantial weight, glycemic, cardiovascular, renal, and sleep apnea benefits should preserve a favorable benefit-risk profile and limit changes to prescribing the medicines, they added.

While GLP-1 drugs have been shown to lead to benefits beyond weight loss and controlling blood sugar levels, many also carry the FDA's strongest safety warning, known as a boxed warning, over a potential risk of thyroid tumors.

Studies have shown that semaglutide, the active ingredient in Wegovy and Ozempic, and tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro, cause thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents. It remains unknown whether the drugs cause these tumors, including a rare form of thyroid cancer, in humans.

Those who were there will never forget the utter chaos of Michael Carrick’s last act at Old Trafford. The 27-year-old was bought for his teenage potential by his compatriot Jose Mourinho in 2018 but has never trained on. No they were not about Dalot but instead about the fundamental truth of what he represents. Namely a steady, grisly and unforgivable drain of footballing standards at United that actually began in the final days of Sir Alex Ferguson and has subsequently never stopped.

It is this that has taken United from Ferguson’s final valedictory title of 2013 to where they are today, the bottom three of the Premier League. It is this that has drained United of all their clout and reputation. It is this that has cost yet another United manager – a seventh – his job.

Diogo Dalot has become the poster boy for the decline under Michael Carrick as Man United seemingly plumb new depths

The outgoing club legend is not a good enough manager for United and his appointment was yet another mis-step by the club's hierarchy

Carrick is not a good enough manager for United. His appointment was an emotional mistake, one of many made by Sir Jim Ratcliffe and the band of misfits and hopefuls who are currently in charge of football operations.

With a better manager – a better coach – in charge of the current team, United would be in the top half of the league. The fact they sit 18th and resemble a Sunday League team that never gets going until they have shaken their hangovers off is squarely down to him.

Nevertheless, United are a football club that exists to win trophies, no matter what the majority owners the Glazers may have said recently. But United will not win the big trophies again until they buy better players and by that I mean better footballers and better people.

It sounds simple, trite even. But it’s a simple truth of our game. Until United can reverse a seemingly addictive habit of paying too much money for too little, the difference made by a manager will only ever be a matter of six or seven places in the Premier League standings. And United needs a much greater improvement than that until this vicious cycle of self-harm comes to an end.

There is so much wrong at the modern Manchester United away from the field. Much of it begins and ends with the hideous hubris and self-regard of Ratcliffe, a man so shot through with misplaced self-confidence that he still insists on being tailed everywhere by a journalist he has hired to write a book on United’s journey back to the top of the mountain. Suffice to say that one won’t be ready for this Christmas.

Despite the financial drain of the Glazers’ stranglehold, United have spent in the region of £1.75bn on their first team in the last ten years.

It takes quite something to do that and take a club backwards. It takes quite something to do that and buy only one player – captain Bruno Fernandes – who can claim to have been consistently effective over his time at the club.

Somehow, though, United have managed it and it is this hideous trail of bad judgment and waste that follows the current team and those that have come before it every time it runs out on to the field.

United are weak off the pitch and even weaker on it. Their football mirrors their off-field strategy. Haphazard, wasteful and inconsistent.

A number of years ago – at a time when previous CEOs Woodward and Arnold were trying to mend a broken club - they enlisted the advice of Ferguson, Sir Bobby Charlton and former captain Bryan Robson to help them work out what exactly a United team should be.

‘Exciting, daring, confident and humble’ were the key words they laid down. Well, what on earth happened to any of that?

United have a huge decision to make – yet again – over a new manager. The field of suitable candidates is not as big as they would like. Nor is United as attractive a proposition as it was. The demands are enormous yet the foundations shake. It’s not a good combination.

A good coach would improve them. Carrick has had two central defenders on his staff in Jonny Evans and Jonathan Woodgate. What on earth have they been doing for the last two or three months?

United need proper talent but they also need proper people, not the kind who would rather moan to TV cameras after a defeat than go home and think about their own part in what has just happened.

Last summer’s strategy seemed to have come straight out of Chat GPT and reflects terribly on the current football director Jason Wilcox. To ‘fix’ the midfield through three signings with the need to ‘fix’ the back four kicked down the road for 12 months is not so much a strategy as an exercise in transfer suicide.

Some think Wilcox should follow Carrick through the door on the back of that and it’s a reasonable point of view. Viewed through the mist of an autumn afternoon on Sunday, Wilcox looked as grey as his recent track record. An alternative take is that the recruitment of players like Bryan Mbeumo and Matheus Cunha is indicative of an uptick in returns, at least in part.

Whichever path you wish to walk, it simply has to lead to an improvement in the one area that will either nourish or starve a football club. With that in mind, the first question a prospective manager should ask is who is buying the players and on the back of what long-term strategy.

This was a more relaxed continuation of the luxuriously louche and wanton glamour of his last runway collection, which itself had been informed by the Californian desert. “With pre-collection I can explore a bit more the casual side of Balmain,” he said.

That meant injecting a dose of dishevelment into Balmain’s appetite for decoration. Sensuously draped dresses in snakeskin and beautiful, borderline-hallucinogenic florals came with cropped flight jackets, while satin tailoring, sheer lurex blouses, and embellished animalier were countered by washed denim and biker leathers. There were micro cardigans over exposed bras, skirts that succumbed into flowerings of asymmetric ruffle, and lavishly embellished pieces whose surfaces seemed weathered by weeks of sun exposure. What blurred in the haze of Tron’s gaze was much distinction between dressing up and down.

This dissolved tension was more than a styling proposition. Tron has little patience for the idea that luxury dressing must conform either to bourgeois standards of propriety, or fashion’s customary cringe of faux-intellectual snobbishness. “For me, this thing with sexiness is also a class thing,” he said of the physical ideals through which notions of elegance are policed. Balmain, in his view, offers a space to articulate sexuality rather than apologize for it.

He recalled meeting a client in Los Angeles while shooting the collection in Joshua Tree. When he mentioned his last show’s desert-and-hallucinogenics theme, she cheerfully volunteered that she had a house there and used to do exactly that. She was accompanied by her muscular husband. “They both wanted to be hot, and that’s it,” Tron observed. This seemed a pretty good expression of the collection’s carefully direct hedonism. Super-long leather boots and Lucite-heeled wedges provided the strut, while the tasseled Zabriskie bag offered easygoing swing.

His instinct is that luxury should enable freedom rather than impose rules. The filament that connects this new Balmain woman and the new Balmain man, in spirit as much as substance, is Tron himself. “I think slowly, I’m finding that line: I’m finding the woman and the man, basically.”
