7 Upcoming Fantasy Shows, Ranked by Hype

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

2026 has been a pretty solid year for fantasy television. So far, the year has seen new seasons of shows like House of the Dragon and Interview with the Vampire, as well as brand-new series like Avatar: Seven Havens and Witch Hat Atelier. However, as fun as those shows are, there are several exciting fantasy shows in the pipeline right now that could potentially blow those hits out of the water.

With release dates across the rest of 2026, 2027, and beyond, several upcoming shows have garnered sky-high hype among fantasy fans, drumming up lots of conversations ahead of their premieres. Not all of the conversations are positive, of course, and there’s at least one upcoming project that’s been talked about for all the wrong reasons, but on the whole, the future of fantasy television does look pretty bright. Without further ado, here are some of the most fascinating upcoming fantasy shows ranked by hype.

Based on J. K. Rowling’s bestselling fantasy books, Harry Potter is an upcoming fantasy series that’s the second adaptation of the novels after the film series. Starring Dominic McLaughlin as Harry, Alastair Stout as Ron Weasley, and Arabella Stanton as Hermione Granger, the show’s first season adapts the first book, Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone, and details Harry’s discovery of the wizarding world and his first year at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry. The ensemble cast also includes John Lithgow as Albus Dumbledore, Janet McTeer as Minerva McGonagall, Paapa Essiedu as Severus Snape, and Nick Frost as Rubeus Hagrid.

HBO has big plans for Harry Potter, with early reports estimating that the new show could span a decade of production. The series has already been renewed for a second season as well. However, the whole project has been largely met with the opposite of hype. Comparisons to the movies (the last of which came out in 2011), negative public opinion of J.K. Rowling, and the idea that the whole project is a naked cash grab have all led to a generally dismal reception from the core fanbase ahead of the show’s release.

Created by Richard Garfield, Magic: The Gathering is one of the most successful game franchises of all time. A trading card game that’s expanded to various other media, the franchise is set across multiple realities and centered on Planeswalkers, dueling wizards with the ability to cross between worlds. While the game has been adapted as comics and novels before, it’s yet to get a movie or TV adaptation.

The Magic: The Gathering series adaptation has been in development since 2019 and has undergone many behind-the-scenes changes, but it finally looks like the show might actually make it to screens. The new show will be a Netflix animated series with Terry Matalas (12 Monkeys) serving as showrunner. The series was previewed at the 2025 Annecy International Animation Film Festival, but it doesn’t yet have a release date. Nonetheless, fans of the franchise are certainly excited for the show’s release and for the upcoming movie adaptation directed by Matt Johnson (Tony).

One of the bestselling romantasy books of the 2020s, Rebecca Yarros’s Fourth Wing is the first book of the Empyrean new adult fantasy romance series. A series adaptation of the books has been in the works at Prime Video since 2023, with Yarros serving as an executive producer on the project alongside Michael B. Jordan, Lisa Joy, Jonathan Nolan, Liz Pelletier, Athena Wickham, and showrunner Meredith Averill. The show, like the book, follows the story of Violet Sorrengail, who joins the Basgiath War College and trains to become a dragon rider, encountering deadly quests, gruelling competition, and unexpected romance. Esther McGregor will star as Violet, with Emilio Sakraya as her rival/love interest, Xaden Riorson.

Fourth Wing is one of the biggest BookTok hits of the romantasy genre, so its series adaptation has a lot of people very excited, and with good reason. Featuring fast-paced action, high-tension romance, and, of course, dragons, Fourth Wing has all the ingredients to become a fantasy TV landmark to rival Game of Thrones. Whether it will actually achieve that potential remains to be seen.

You belong somewhere old enough that the mountains have names and somebody can still tell you who gave them. Middle-earth is for people who think the right thing is worth doing even when it will not be finished in their lifetime.

Mike Flanagan’s The Dark Tower is an upcoming multi-season series adaptation of the fantasy book series by Stephen King. Published between 1982 and 2012, the books revolve around Roland Deschain, a gunslinger on an unending quest across a surreal landscape in search of the titular tower, which sits at the center of a vast multiverse. The books were previously adapted as a 2017 film, which was widely panned by critics, but the series is a complete reboot of the story, and it’s been reported that it will star Sam Strike and Jasper Pääkkönen.

Spanning eight connected novels, one novella, a children’s book, and connections to almost all of King’s celebrated novels, The Dark Tower is the holy grail of Stephen King adaptations. It’s easy to see why the upcoming series has so many people excited, even though it doesn’t have a release date or any concrete details as yet. What little we know about The Dark Tower so far suggests an in-depth adaptation that could prove to be Flanagan’s most ambitious project yet and one of the most fascinating fantasy shows ever made. Or, like the movie, it could just be an expensive failure; only time will tell.

It’s no exaggeration to say that Brandon Sanderson is one of the biggest names in fantasy literature today, and The Stormlight Archive novel series, part of Sanderson’s Cosmere universe, is one of the author’s most popular hits. Currently comprising five novels and two novellas (with plans for a total of 10 novels), it’s an expansive saga set on the battered world of Roshar, which is threatened by magical storms, political tensions, and a looming cataclysm. The books have already inspired a VR experience and a tabletop game, and will soon become an Apple TV series.

As part of Sanderson’s deal with the streaming platform, the author will be personally leading adaptations of his Mistborn novels and The Stormlight Archive, the first as a movie and the second as a TV show, the first season of which will consist of 10 episodes. It’s still early days — the project doesn’t even have a showrunner yet — but it’s easily one of the most hotly anticipated fantasy shows currently in development. With complex character dynamics, intricate worldbuilding, and a fascinating magic system, The Stormlight Archive series has all the potential to become one of the biggest fantasy shows of all time, though, of course, right now, that’s all just speculation.

Developed by Sony Pictures Television, Amazon MGM Studios, and PlayStation Productions, Prime Video’s God of War is the first series adaptation of the video game franchise. Based on the franchise’s Norse mythology era, the show follows Kratos and his son Atreus on a journey to the highest peak of the nine realms, where they hope to spread the ashes of their wife and mother, Faye. Ronald D. Moore serves as the showrunner, and the series stars Dave Bautista and Callum Vinson as Kratos and Atreus, respectively.

God of War fans have been waiting for a live-action adaptation for a long time, and the series is quite highly anticipated as a result. The production has had some significant delays, with original Kratos actor Ryan Hurst suffering an injury during filming, which has prompted a recasting and reshoots. We don’t know when the show will finally arrive, but Prime Video has given it a two-season order, and production is currently expected to end sometime in October 2027.

The second anime adaptation of Eiichiro Oda’s One Piece, The One Piece is a new original net animation show produced by Studio Wit and set to release on Netflix in February 2027. The series is a fresh reboot of the franchise adapted directly from the manga (not the iconic 1999 anime), and it will follow the adventures of Monkey D. Luffy (Mayumi Tanaka), an aspiring pirate who sets out in search of the titular treasure alongside his motley crew, the Straw Hats. Directed by Masashi Koizuka and written by Taku Kishimoto, with character designs by Kyoji Asano and Takatoshi Honda, the show’s seven-episode first season will adapt the first 50 chapters of the manga.

One Piece is one of those anime/manga franchises that’s so big it’s recognizable even to people who’ve never seen an anime before, so it’s pretty understandable why Wit’s The One Piece is so highly anticipated. The new adaptation is expected to fix the classic anime’s pacing issues and elevate the animation style, giving the manga the fully modernized adaptation it deserves and bringing a new generation of viewers to the franchise.

Seven flights across Europe targeted by suspected bomb threats

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Watch: Sniffer dog inspects Ryanair plane in Spain after suspected bomb threat

Seven passenger planes across Europe, two of which departed from the UK, have been inspected following a series of suspected bomb threats.

Two Ryanair flights, one originating from Dublin and the other from Lisbon, were searched after landing in Rome following anonymous tips but no explosives were found, sources at the city's Fiumicino airport told the BBC.

The Dublin flight was escorted by the Italian Air Force until landing. Authorities have also searched planes in Poland, Spain and Bulgaria.

While officially there is no link between them, authorities from various countries will be co-operating to assess whether this was a co-ordinated attack, designed to disrupt airspace.

The UK government described today's incidents as "malicious hoax threats".

In a statement it said it had "one of the most robust aviation security systems in the world, with well-tested processes for dealing with any potential threats, as well as malicious hoax threats, like those we've seen today".

Aviation security expert Paul Mason said bomb threats on passenger planes "don't happen that frequently", which made the latest incidents "unusual".

"It sounds like they've been made in a credible manner across a broad range of airlines," Mason told BBC Radio 5 Live. "That would suggest to me that it's been a very deliberate act by a typical actor rather than a load of coincidental, malicious calls."

He said "there are a lot of processes" that the onboard crew and people on the ground must go through "to validate the credibility of the threat".

A flight may be asked to divert if the credibility of a threat "is of a certain level", Mason added, and in some cases the onboard crew may not even be advised on what the ground operators were dealing with.

Two of the four flights were en route to Warsaw Chopin airport

In Rome, sources at Fiumicino airport told the BBC police bomb disposal experts carried out standard security protocols to ensure the safety of all passengers and crew.

Inspections of the passengers, luggage, and the aircraft yielded negative results, and the alert has since been cleared.

One plane which landed in Murcia, southern Spain - a Ryanair flight coming from Manchester - was also found not to have any explosives on board, Spanish authorities said.

One passenger later said she was unaware of the bomb threat until after the plane landed.

"We weren't told anything during the flight," Sarah told BBC Radio 5 Live. "The plane stopped quite suddenly and then we were kept on the runway for around 30 minutes."

"Then the fire engines turned up, the bomb squad turned up and we were asked to evacuate," she continues.

Sarah said the plane had not been on the ground a minute before it was "surrounded by fire engines and police and a bomb squad". All the passengers were then escorted by police, leaving all their belongings.

"We were only later told that it was an actual threat, a bomb threat to the plane itself," she added.

Two flights heading to Warsaw were also affected, including one from London Heathrow.

The Heathrow flight was originally meant to land at the Polish capital's main airport, Chopin, but was diverted instead to the smaller Modlin airport, briefly forcing its closure.

A Scandinavian Airlines flight from Copenhagen to Warsaw-Chopin landed as planned.

Later on Tuesday, Bulgarian Transport Minister Georgi Peev said there were bomb threats against two planes travelling to the country's capital Sofia.

He told the state-owned Bulgarian News Agency (BTA) the flights were from the northern Italian city of Bergamo and Ireland's capital Dublin.

The flights into Sofia were not diverted and no explosives were found when the planes were checked, Peev said.

Bulgaria's border police said in a statement sent to the BBC that at around 10:35 (08:35 GMT) it had received an email about "the presence of an improvised explosive device on a flight returning from Bergamo".

"Standard procedures for such incidents were immediately activated, and an operational headquarters comprising representatives of various institutions was convened," the statement said.

The border police added that the same email also reported a "similar threat against aircraft arriving from Dublin".

Initial checks found no explosives on board the Crown Iris cruise ship

In a separate development, a bomb alert triggered the evacuation of 1,537 passengers and crew from an Israel-bound cruise ship in Greece, media reports from both countries say.

Initial checks of the Panama-flagged Crown Iris vessel currently docked at a port on the Aegean island of Syros found no explosives, they said citing the Greek Coast Guard. A second inspection is expected shortly.

Tuesday's aircraft incidents come a day after a Wizz Air flight from London Luton made an emergency landing in Budapest, escorted by a Hungarian fighter jet, after a bomb threat.

Wizz Air said later that security checks had been made and nothing was found.

BBC Verify has consulted aviation safety experts about what happens on board a plane when a bomb threat is issued.

Marco Chan, a former pilot for Cathay Pacific Airways and Hong Kong Airlines who is now a senior lecturer in aviation operations at Kingston University, said a bomb threat is considered "genuine until it is resolved, even though most turn out to be hoaxes".

Chan said crews will "quietly search" toilets, galleys and storage areas and will not touch a suspicious item unless it has to be taken to an area on the aircraft designated as the least risky bomb location.

The aircraft's captain will have multiple factors to assess, according to former airline pilot Terry Tozer, an aviation safety commentator.

He said these include what facilities are available at the airport they are being diverted to and how the plane can continue onwards should it turn out to be a false threat.

Chan added that once on the ground, the plane is generally "taken to an isolated stand, passengers are disembarked, and police and bomb disposal teams search it".

Changpeng Zhao says companies need AI-based defenses and an industry alliance to share lessons and freeze stolen funds faster, in his first interview since the Oct. 29 hack.

Binance cofounder and former CEO Changpeng "CZ" Zhao said AI is accelerating cyberattacks and argued companies should invest in AI-based defenses, security personnel, and partnerships with AI security providers.

In his first public interview since the Oct. 29 hack of Binance, Zhao told the Talking Tokens podcast that the threat extends beyond individual companies to the broader crypto industry and global financial system, given crypto's connections to stablecoins, tokenized stocks, real-world assets, and traditional finance.

"The speed of hacking is going to pick up because of AI capabilities [being] so strong now. At the same time, we've got to use more AI to defend. The only way to fight a monster is to kind of become that monster."

In His Own Words, a Czech Alpinist Describes How He Survived a Nightmare on Peru’s Highest Mountain

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Honza Špička on Huascarán (Photo: Courtesy of Matěj Žák)

My name is Jan “Honza” Špička. My climbing partner, Matěj Žák, and I are two alpinists, climbers, mountaineers, skiers, and endurance athletes from Nove Straseci, a small town in the Czech Republic. Although we have known each other since childhood through our families (and the local ice hockey team), our climbing paths only crossed a few years ago. Since then, we have spent three seasons together in Peru’s Cordillera Blanca and two seasons in Argentine Patagonia. We have roped up together a few times on Czech sandstone as well. We are no professionals, though; rather than extreme lines, what we have behind us are moderately difficult routes climbed in our own style: clean and fast.

Our speciality? Long days in the mountains and a fast, lightweight approach. We take nothing on an expedition that we cannot carry on our own backs.

Huascarán is the highest mountain in Peru and the highest point in the entire tropics. Its south summit reaches 6,768m and the north summit 6,655m; the two are separated by a broad col called La Garganta (“the Throat” in Spanish) at roughly 6,000m. Huascarán lies at the heart of the Cordillera Blanca, the most heavily glaciated tropical mountain range in the world, and it gave its name to the surrounding national park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1985. Because the Earth bulges slightly at the equator, the gravitational acceleration on the summit of Huascarán is among the lowest anywhere on the planet’s surface; a person there measurably weighs a little less than at sea level, by a matter of grams. The summit of Huascarán is also the second most distant point on Earth from the planet’s center, right after Chimborazo.

The mountain is named after Huáscar, the Inca ruler who fought his half-brother Atahualpa for several years over control of the Inca Empire. Atahualpa eventually defeated his brother and had him executed. The American mountaineer Annie Smith Peck, then over fifty years old, climbed the north summit in 1908. The higher south summit held out until 1932, when a German–Austrian expedition made the first ascent.

Huascarán, however, also has a darker side to its history. In 1962, a huge mass of ice broke away from the mountain, and the resulting debris avalanche swept down onto the towns of Ranrahirca. Then, on May 31, 1970, an earthquake off the coast triggered a far larger and more extensive avalanche from the north summit, which within minutes buried, among other places, the entire town of Yungay. The earthquake and avalanche combined took the lives of approximately 67,000 people. It was the deadliest single avalanche disaster by death toll in recorded history.

This tragic event unfortunately also carries a strong Czechoslovak mark. It was right here, in the valley between Huascarán’s north summit and Huandoy Sur, that a Czechoslovak expedition was camped—and the whole expedition became a victim of this mountain.

If we take into account the victims of 1970 and all the climbers who have died on its slopes, it is probably the mountain responsible for more human lives than any other in modern history.

The ascent itself, via the Normal Route, is not particularly difficult, but Huascarán’s glaciers are retreating fast, and so today some routes that were relatively straightforward climbs just a generation ago involve significant objective hazards. In the six years that I have lived in Peru, I cannot remember a year when Huascarán did not claim another victim. There have even been years when it allowed no one at all to reach its summit.

The start of our trip began on Friday, July 11, in the evening in our favourite “pollo a la brasa” restaurant. Our whole group, which we called the “Cayesh crew,” met for a shared dinner to load up on a solid dose of protein before what was to be the main climbing objective of the season for all of us: Huascarán Sur. It has to be said, though, that for none of us was Huascarán the center of the universe. We all had it in “one day, maybe, I’d like to, if it comes to it…” mode, but there was certainly no obsession with this “fat one.” I already had three unsuccessful attempts behind me, but even so I wasn’t insisting on “bagging it.” Yet somehow the appetite, the motivation, the hunger for it arrived. And essentially by coincidence it arrived not only for Matěj and me but also for our friends from Peru. In the end we formed three teams of two.

I have known Saul—formally, Freddy Saul Mendoza Lizana—since 2021; he was essentially my first climbing partner here in Peru. Since then we have been through a lot together—in the mountains, on the crags, at home, and in the pub. He was like my Peruvian brother. We were so close that my wife Alča and I were already counting on a little room for Saul for when we one day return to the Czech Republic and settle down somewhere.

Agnieszka is a Polish “machine” with several 8a/5.13b sport routes to her name. She had seen plenty in the mountains, too. Definitely no lightweight.

Alejandro “Chicho” Ugarte Jordan was a professional photographer from Lima. His main interest was unique mountain images captured with his DSLR or his drone. We had known each other since 2022 through the Skiing Peru project, which we were both part of. We often spent time together with our girlfriends in Lima outside the season, too.

Artidoro “Arti” Salas Gaitan was a pure Huaracino—someone who comes from Huaraz. He was born in the town at the foot of the Cordillera Blanca and lived there his whole life. He ran the Big Mountain hostel, well known among climbers. I personally have known him since 2021, when I spent my first season in Huaraz at his hostel.

Our expedition began on the morning of Saturday, July 11, when we loaded the van with heavy packs. Under the watchful eye of—as Matěj called him—our adoptive father Paul, our friend and the owner of the cozy Cayesh Guest House bed and breakfast, we set off for our starting point, the village of Armapampa in the Yungay area. From Huaraz, it was about a two-hour drive. Besides the six of us, Alenka came along, too, having arrived from Lima that same morning, as did Míša, a Czech woman living in Nepal who had just popped over to Peru to see friends. The girls, however, were only going on a day trip to the hut and back down the next day.

On the edge of the beautiful village of Armapampa, we unloaded our gear from the van, ate the last gluten-free cinnamon rolls that Alenka baked for the trip, and shouldered our heavy packs. Matěj took a group photo and we set off towards Refugio Huascarán, our base camp. We reached the refugio at 4,650m spread out over quite a wide time range (Matěj first, at 2 hours 45 minutes, and Saul and Aga last at a little over 6 hours). Who knows where and why they lingered; apparently they were finishing off their avocados. Either way, the trail was easy and there was no rush, so everyone went at their own pace.

There was no one at the hut apart from us, so we enjoyed it immensely. The evening was a very friendly affair: dinner, beer, guitar, and singing in four languages mixed together. And, of course, planning the climb.

This is where the first, relatively pivotal moment came. Aga wasn’t feeling well—to be precise, she felt pretty crap—so she decided to walk down with the girls. That left the five of us Mohicans. Saul immediately arranged with the lads to form a three-person team. At the time, it seemed to me a good idea. Saul is the most experienced of the three, and the team would only be stronger for it. In hindsight, I blame myself that we didn’t tell him to stay with Agnieszka; Aga was his big “project.”

After breakfast at the hut, we packed our rucksacks, took a group photo, and set off. The girls headed down; the lads headed up. None of us had any idea that this would be our last photo together, and that the girls would never see Saul, Chicho, and Arti again. Just writing this sentence brings tears to my eyes.

The second day of the climb up Huascarán was easy going. A few hundred vertical meters led up to the glacier, then a few hundred more traversed over a gently angled and relatively safe glacier to the spot where Camp 1 is set, at about 5,300m. Matěj and I arrived first, and shortly after us Arti and Chicho, with Saul behind them. We pitched the tents, melted water, cooked food, took photos, rested, and fine-tuned the details for tomorrow’s summit push.

Huascarán is normally climbed via the Garganta col, where Camp 2 is placed at 5,950m, and from there climbers make for the summit. All of us, however, were heading for the El Escudo (“The Shield”) route, which is technically harder but objectively safer,and of course more fun. It is a roughly 600-meter long ice face at 60 to 70 degrees. Thanks to its shape, there are no seracs hanging above you, no cornices, and far fewer things that can come down on your head. Unfortunately, there is little room for a Camp 2 there, so the Escudo is usually climbed from Camp 1 straight to the top, with the descent via the Normal Route.

We set our departure time for 10 pm. While the sun was still up, we crawled into our sleeping bags to get at least a little sleep and recharge the batteries a bit. We all knew that brutally hard work lay ahead.

This was it: summit day. Matěj and I got up at 9:30 p.m. so that we could leave at 10. We could hear the lads sometime before 9. In the end, we left more or less together: the lads at about 10:15 pm, we a few minutes after them.

Given the profile of the route over the Escudo, the plan from the start was to climb in pairs. Later, we became a pair and a three. You cannot climb this with five people on one rope, and none of us ever suggested it. The same applies to the descent.

The first part of the route was probably the most dangerous section of the whole ascent. It was a zone of crevasses and seracs of a size you will not encounter in Europe. There were a few sections of steep ice, but it wasn’t outright hard; it was just that something could come down at any moment. This section was shared with the Normal Route, which then branched off to the left towards Camp 2, while we headed right towards El Escudo.

We reach the bergschrund in less than two hours, with the lads following an hour or so behind us. Here we re-tied onto the full length of the 60m rope and racked up all our gear. In the process, I dropped my radio somewhere into the bowels of the glacier, and Matěj set off upward. The terrain wasn’t hard, so we simul-climbed on a single rope. Matěj climbed three to four pitches this way until he ran out of ice screws. On his last two snow pickets, he built a belay and let me through. My section was more hard snow than ice:easier climbing, less fun, but with correspondingly worse protection. I had three snow pickets on my pack, but I wanted to save those for later, for the belay anchors. After climbing some six pitches on just a handful of screws, I topped out over the lip of the Escudo, where I brought up Matýsek. (Editor’s note: “Matýsek” is a Czech nickname for “Matěj.”) By then, we were at 6,300m. It was cold as hell, with the sun still nowhere in sight. I dropped a glove and started feeling properly ill from the altitude. From this moment on, I suffered like an animal. The section from the top of the Escudo to the summit was endless; it wasn’t as steep any more, but it was long, and the soft snow did nothing to help our progress.

Sometime before 9 a.m. on Monday, July 13, though, we stood on the summit of Huascarán Sur. We didn’t really feel much joy, because we knew the worst was still ahead of us: the way down. On top of that, the summit was a completely flat plateau. You could play soccer on it, only you’d have a long way to go for a stray ball. Even so, weakness came over me and I cried. It was my fourth attempt, with my partner and childhood friend, and exactly a month to the day since our dog, Pisco, was poisoned. I cried again just remembering him. But the joy was there, too, although the main part of it was supposed to come down below. And it did come. But only for a moment.

We didn’t linger on the summit. There really was nothing there. After a snack, a little tea, and the summit photo, we headed down. No one had been on the summit for about two to three weeks, so there were no tracks and no trail. Our only guide was the offline mapy.cz app, which traced the Normal Route. But it was the route that was walked an unknown number of years ago, so we treated it as a rough indication only. We re-tied into classic glacier-travel mode, roughly 15–20 meters apart, with the rest of the rope coiled around each of our bodies.

A few minutes from the summit, on the empty, white, flat plateau, I saw something that looked like the windblown hint of a trail. Before I even got there, something happened and I was falling through the air into an icy hell.

A crevasse. With no sign whatsoever of its existence, it had been waiting there silently for its victim. I remember precisely the feeling of flying into the void. It was as if I were falling for an eternity. Suddenly, I felt the rope come tight, and I knew that was Matýsek. But I keep falling. In that instant, I was certain that in a moment, Matýsek would come flying in after me and we’d both be dead.

But a miracle happened—a miracle called Matěj. The rope bit again and I stopped. He bloody well caught me. I was hanging about 12–15 meters down in a crevasse that runs 100m in each direction and could easily be 50m, 100m, or 300m deep. Who knows? Below me, I saw nothing but icy hell. The crevasse was completely covered with a cap of fine snow. Zero chance of seeing it.

To my own surprise, I didn’t panic; I knew exactly what to do. My only connection to our world was the rope I was hanging on and whatever Matěj had rigged up there to keep me in this world. I clipped a Micro Traxion onto my harness as a self-belay and climbed up the vertical ice wall towards the surface as far as I could. I felt so OK that I even took three photos inside the crevasse. When the line of the rope stopped me from climbing any farther, I swung out into space. I ascended the rest on prusiks.

In a little under half an hour, I was out, back on the surface, back in the world. Only now did I breathe out.

Matěj and I fell into each other’s arms and began to grasp what we just survived. It would have taken very little for him to come in after me, and no one would ever have found us. In hindsight, I see it as an enormous miracle and a measure of enormous skill on Matěj’s part. He saved our lives. And maybe it was at this very moment that we realized this really was no joke and that things were getting serious. We continued the descent very cautiously, and we were genuinely scared.

We reached the edge of an ice wall where the Normal Route was supposed to be, but there was nothing there—nothing at all. There was no question of searching for a way through this labyrinth. The wall looked to be about 20m high, with a ramp below it along which we hoped to traverse off left toward the Normal Route.

We rappelled off two snow pickets into a relatively safe spot surrounded by seracs. I remember looking at the leaning towers next to us and thinking that one deep breath would be enough to bring the whole lot down. Still, we felt a certain safety in our little hollow. Finally, we had a bit of time to catch our breath, eat and drink a little, and get in touch with the lads.

Before I could do that, they called me. They said they were about 200 vertical meters below the summit. But it was already late, around 12 to 1 p.m., so their plan was to traverse off towards the Normal Route and descend by it.

I told them to try to find our tracks and our rappel, and to really watch out for crevasses, because I had just fallen into one. We confirmed that everything was OK and that we would meet in the evening at Camp 1, or if need be, at base camp, depending on the time and our energy. If I had known that this was the last time I would speak to them, I would have told them so much more.

A little later, while searching for the route on the map, my phone slipped out of my hand and vanished somewhere below. But I didn’t dwell on it. I knew we had other worries. It was just a shame to lose the photos from inside the crevasse.

Another unpleasant rappel off a 25-meter serac, and we were on the slope leading towards the Garganta. By now the weather had turned and visibility had dropped to a few tens of meters. But we were already in the Garganta, at 6,000m. All we needed now was to find Camp 2, which was just a flat patch where tents stood two days ago. From there, there should be some tracks and a route down.

We wandered about a bit, but eventually we found Camp 2 and there really was a hint of tracks. Shortly before that, the summit shook off its cloud, and we saw the lads above us at about 6,300 or 6,400 m. It even looked to us as though they were closer to the Normal Route than we were. We were really relieved.

The route from Camp 2 to Camp 1 is the most dangerous crux of the climb. It is a zone of avalanches, collapsing seracs, and crevasses. Later, Matěj and I agreed that we both felt as if we were living in some kind of virtual reality, outside our bodies, just waiting for the video game to end. Maybe that was why I didn’t really register the risk.

We arrived back at Camp 1 at sunset. With our last reserves of strength, we packed up and continued down to the refugio. A hot dinner and a bed was, after all, a big draw. And it was only a little under two hours more.

We reached the hut sometime before nine in the evening, after 23 hours on our feet. We asked for something to eat and a beer, then went to sleep. We thought about the lads and somehow had no doubt that they were fine and that they were at Camp 1. After all, we saw them a short way above Camp 2.

In the morning, we woke up at the hut completely wrecked, but suddenly also happy with what we’d achieved. We expected the lads to turn up any moment, and then we’d race down into the valley. The news from the lads, however, was different.

Saul wrote that they couldn’t find the way down and had to bivouac somewhere above Camp 2. But they were OK, he said; they survived the night up there without any camping gear. They were tired, Chicho was apparently feeling ill, but all were OK. They would try to find the descent route. From then on, we were to communicate with Chicho, as Saul’s battery was dead.

We still had no idea that things were bad. But neither did the lads. Because things weren’t bad yet. We managed to download our GPS track onto a phone and send it to the lads as a mapy.cz link. Up above Camp 2, there was occasionally a signal, so we communicated with the lads via WhatsApp.

At 9:10 a.m., Chicho wrote asking us to send our route. At 9:15 a.m., we sent the mapy.cz link and it was delivered.

At 10:16 am, Chicho sent a photo and a video from where they were. They sounded fine; Camp 2 was visible below them. At 10:18 a.m., we sent them a crib sheet of roughly which way to go down, including a sketch drawn onto their photo. This was delivered. At 10:21 a.m., we asked Chicho whether he had our route on mapy.cz. This was the last message delivered to Chicho. After 10:21 am, no further messages were delivered. From then on, there was no communication from them.

Since the weather was beautiful, the lads were fine, and there was no signal lower down towards Camp 2, it never occurred to us that the lads might be in trouble. We were certain they had descended lower, lost signal, had flat phones, and so on—but were descending. There was essentially no reason to think anything else. They werebujacos”—tough guys. They had survived a night without sleeping bags at 6,400m, and now they would just run down to us, to the girls, to lunch, to a beer.

Given these assumptions, we knew we had several hours of waiting before they would appear above Camp 1, which could be seen from the hut. Meanwhile, we tried to contact them by radio and by phone, in case they happened to come into signal. But still, we were completely calm.

When about 3 p.m. came and there was no sign of them, though, it seemed odd to us. It had gone on too long. We decided we would go up and look for them. They were probably tired and frozen, and another night up there would be truly critical.

We ate, brewed tea, and took all the clothing we had. In the meantime, I called a friend, a guide, down in town so that he knew something was going on and could start rounding up help if needed. But there was still a chance alive in us that they were at Camp 1, asleep in the tent, and simply had no way of letting us know. Down in town, a group of volunteers was already getting ready to set out to help. There is no organized professional mountain rescue in Peru.

But there was no one at Camp 1. By then we sensed that things were bad; we just didn’t know how bad. On top of that, a snowstorm was moving in.

The message we sent down was clear: It was official, we needed help. The lads were somewhere up there. We were going up for them. We knew that down in the valley things were already under way and would keep going without us. But we had a different task: to find them and bring them down.

We pack all their sleeping bags as well and set off up into the gale. Matěj created a Garmin messaging group and made our position trackable online. He realized more than I did that we were heading into real trouble.

I have friends up there – my “churaks,” my “hermanos.” I registered nothing.

For the first time in my life, I was faster than Matěj. In my head, I knew they were up there, frozen and exhausted but alive, and that we were their only chance.

In these conditions I would turn around on any climb of my own; I would never go on. But now it wasn’t about me. It was about the lads. The moment we gave up, they would have no chance.

We got as far as above Camp 2, to 6,000m, but there was nothing anywhere. That was shortly before midnight. Just gale, snowfall, a temperature I’d estimate at around minus 30 degrees, and not a trace of the lads.

By this point I knew things were very bad. In this weather, no one survives up here for even an hour. Matýsek was very afraid of going down because of the avalanche danger and pushed for us to stay at Camp 2 and somehow sit it out with all those sleeping bags. I knew that if we stayed, we would be dead within the hour. In the end, I convinced him that we had to go down. That was about 50/50. But staying up there felt to me like 90/10.

The descent was horrific. Avalanche terrain was everywhere we looked, with giant seracs over our heads almost the entire way. Visibility was near zero, and our tracks drifted over with snow. Nevertheless, our guardian angel was on duty again and guided us down. We get back to Camp 1 sometime around 3 a.m. on Wednesday, July 15. There I well and truly collapsed, mentally and physically. We crawled into the tent for a while and waited for the first group of volunteer rescuers, who, as we already knew, were on their way from the refugio.

Even in the tent and in a sleeping bag, 700 vertical meters lower, the weather was terrible, and it was here that Matýsek told me we were one–all. He had saved us above that crevasse, and I had by insisting that we go down.

The first rescue team reached Camp 1 just after first light, sometime after 6 a.m. It was five people in all, three guides and two aspirant guides, and among them my good friend Szymon, who was the only one closer to me than Saul. That was all. We handed over all the information we had and let them go up. We would head for the refugio, where further teams were supposedly bound: one to set up a support base at Camp 2, another at Camp 1. Supposedly lots of people were coming.

Unfortunately, we were in Peru, where everything does not quite work. We met the Camp 1 team below the glacier—five lads, all friends, climbers, and volunteers from Peru, Colombia, Spain, and the Czech Republic. No Camp 2 team ever came, and in fact, there never was one. The five lads up above had packed late in the evening and set out from Huaraz to help friends in need. In a single push they climbed to over 6,000m. But because of the lack of support and the conditions up there, they had to turn back in the afternoon and descend to Camp 1. That essentially ended any realistic chance of finding the lads alive. I personally had known already at Camp 2, which we had climbed to in our attempt to find them, that there was almost no chance left.

From then on, great chaos set in across the whole organization of the rescue and search. As I already mentioned, Peru has no mountain rescue service; there are no rescue helicopters, no rescuers, no system, no protocol, no designated person to take charge of any coordination. Everything happened through the WhatsApp group that Matěj created when we set out for the lads. Everything was done on the basis of volunteers, friends, guides and family members.

Eventually, the police, the high-mountain police (Policía de Alta Montaña), and apparently even the army joined in to help. But their help consisted solely of transporting volunteers by helicopter from the town of Caraz to the refugio at 4,650m, and the first helicopter took off 24 hours after the official call for help. What’s more, it flew up empty, to assess the situation, only to find that another police helicopter had been sitting on the “helipad” by the refugio for several days, badly parked, and that its pilots were afraid to take off in it.

In the end, some transport up did take place, but effectively only on the following days. If someone down below had been able to coordinate it, they could have sent the line of volunteers towards base camp on foot, with a herd of donkeys, on the very first day of the search (July 15), and they would have been there far sooner than waiting until the next day to see whether anyone would get a place on the helicopter.

The entire rescue, and subsequently the search, was run by volunteers and the families. It was they who hired private companies with advanced drones, they who coordinated everything, mainly with the police, they who did everything they could. I myself went back with two volunteers a few days later to a point just above Camp 1 to attempt a further search with drones—without success. The zone where all the clues ended—GPS signals, the location of the last photos, the hint of the last tracks, and so on—is at around 6,500m. Camp 1 is at 5,300m, and most drones simply cannot fly that high and that far. Only the advanced police drones can, and they in turn lacked experienced pilots willing to risk it. Going higher wasn’t possible because of the conditions on the mountain. So, a stalemate. In the end footage from the suspect zone was obtained, but there was not a trace of the lads.

After almost detective-like work by many friends—using the GPS signal from their watch as requested from Garmin headquarters, footage from private and police drones, plus footage from the guides who went all the way up during the search, information from us, and the lads’ last photos—it appears that the lads missed our belay station by literally a few meters, and that while crossing a snow bridge over gigantic crevasse systems it collapsed beneath them.

When I first wrote this report, it had already been more than three weeks since I last saw the lads, since I last spoke to them. Still nothing has been found today, and in my view it will be hard for anything to be found. The scale of everything on Huascarán is utterly absurd: crevasses several hundred meters wide and deep, seracs the size of apartment blocks… Sure, this isn’t the Himalaya, and it doesn’t look like it, either. Huascarán looks like two lazy fat blokes lying side by side. But beware of it. That mountain is mean and hostile, and although on paper it isn’t hard—the Normal Route was reportedly graded only AD/AD+, the Escudo D—it is no game. You are playing for your life at every single step.

Changes to Chile’s Human Rights Institute Risk Leaving Victims Behind

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Chile's President Jose Antonio Kast waves on his inauguration day at Congress in Valparaiso, Chile, March 11, 2026. © 2026 Esteban Felix/AP Photo

Chilean President José Antonio Kast introduced in Congress on October 19 a proposal to change the country’s National Human Rights Institute.

Founded in 2009 as an autonomous state body, the institute has worked to promote and protect human rights in Chile, including by taking legal action on behalf of human rights abuse victims.

If passed, the legislation would weaken some of the tools the institute uses to protect human rights and diminish its independence.

The bill would remove the institute’s eleven-member board of directors, replacing them with a new five-member transitional board that would draft new bylaws for the institute. Unlike the current board, which has four members who were selected directly by civil society groups, the new transitional board would be proposed by the president, approved by the Senate, and would have no members selected directly by civil society. This sits uneasily with the standards set out in the Principles relating to the Status of National Human Rights Institutions (the Paris Principles), which recommend that members be independent from the government and selected through a process that ensures broad civil society representation.

According to a financial report by the Finance Ministry’s Budget Office, the proposal would also reduce the institute’s staff. The number of staff members in the institute’s regional offices outside of Santiago de Chile would be cut in half, and the number of lawyers in the institute’s protection unit, who handle the institute’s legal actions, would be reduced by 70 percent. As a result, people in many parts of Chile could lose access to in-person assistance from the institute.

The proposed legislation would also end the institute’s ability to represent victims in criminal cases—a power it has often used in cases of torture, ill treatment, or human trafficking. That would mean that victims who cannot afford a lawyer would lose legal support and would rely on prosecutors to decide whether to pursue their case and how to move it forward.

The National Human Rights Institute itself has welcomed other parts of the bill meant to improve its transparency and governance but has warned that some aspects of the bill would undermine its work. Chile’s Congress should reexamine these worrying provisions and make sure the institute can protect the rights of all Chileans.
