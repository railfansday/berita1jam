Matt LaFleur surprised Vikings didn't have to give up more in Kirk Cousins trade: 'I was shocked'

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kirk Cousins is back with the Minnesota Vikings and the rest of the NFC North is on high alert. The Vikings have already shown the division what its dominant defense can do, just imagine how the team will perform with a functional offense.

Green Bay Packers head coach Matt LaFleur will get his first look at the Vikings' revamped offense in Week 10. LaFleur is well aware what Cousins can bring to the table, and admitted he was surprised the Vikings didn't have to give up more to bring Cousins back.

The Vikings managed to acquire Cousins on Tuesday for a 2027 third-round pick. With the move, the Raiders can now turn things over to No. 1 overall pick Fernando Mendoza.

LaFleur had nothing but positive things to say about Cousins, calling him "a really intelligent guy" who has been "playing at a high level."

LaFleur then put the focus on the Las Vegas Raiders, saying he was surprised the team didn't get more in exchange for Cousins.

"I was surprised [the Raiders] didn't get more for him, quite honestly. I was shocked when I saw what they traded him for. It was mind blowing to me."

The Packers' coach didn't mention the Vikings in his comments — or the Raiders, for that matter — but the implication was clear: He felt Minnesota should have been forced to part with more in exchange for Cousins.

That shouldn't necessarily come as a surprise. Cousins undoubtedly improves the Vikings in 2026 and makes the team a more legitimate Super Bowl contender. That could come at the expense of the Packers, who are fighting hard to remain in an exceptionally tough NFC North. Given all that, it's understandable why LaFleur would want to see a division rival part with more talent or higher draft picks in any trade.

Whether Cousins draws the start for the Vikings in Week 10 remains an open question. There are already reports suggesting that will be the case, as Cousins is still familiar with the team's offense. LaFleur certainly expects to see Cousins under center Sunday.

Vikings head coach Kevin O'Connell isn't willing to make that commitment yet, saying the team would like that outcome, but wants to make sure Cousins is comfortable.

That sounds like an honest answer from O'Connell, though there could be a fair amount of gamesmanship in there.

Whether of not Cousins takes the field against the Packers in Week 10, LaFleur knows how much the veteran quarterback improves the Vikings. That's not a good thing for the Packers — or any team in the NFC North — which explains why LaFleur wishes Minnesota gave up more in the deal.

The $700,000 Audi Nuvolari Will Be Extremely Rare in the U.S.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Audi has announced the official price for its brutalist supercar in the U.S. and confirmed that owners will have to wait until 2028 to receive their Nuvolari.

Three years after the death of the R8, Audi revealed a new supercar. First shown in June, the Nuvolari wears a brutalist design and borrows the Lamborghini Temerario’s 789-hp twin-turbo V-8, mating it with a trio of electric motors for a total of 987 horsepower. Unlike the R8, though, the Nuvolari will be a limited-production affair, and now we know just how many will make it to the United States, and what they’ll cost when they get here.

The R8, which lasted from 2007 to 2023, was a true series production car, but Audi only plans on building 499 units of the Nuvolari. Of that total, just 182 will reach the United States. Once they do, each of their new owners will have to pony up at least $700,000. That figure doesn’t include the designation fee, which Audi has yet to disclose.

Deliveries won’t start until the first half of 2028, but once owners get their hands on the Nuvolari, we imagine they will be quite happy. That 987-hp powertrain propels the distinctively designed supercar to 60 mph in a claimed 2.5 seconds and to a top speed of 217 mph, making it both the most powerful and fastest production Audi ever.

Owners will have the choice of over 50 gloss paint colors and 10 matte paint colors for the exterior. The carbon-fiber bodywork can also be left fully exposed, which will surely be a pricey option. There are also 13 different colors for the brake calipers available. Given that the Nuvolari has only been shown so far in a matte gray hue, we look forward to seeing what it looks like in more vibrant colors.

Written by Doucouré (Cuties, Hawa) with Olivier Lorelle, Josephine Baker will follow its subject’s rise to international stardom in 1920s Paris and her subsequent work with the French Resistance and the American civil rights movement.

Born in St. Louis in 1906, Baker moved to Paris in 1925 and became one of the defining entertainers of the Jazz Age. Her later activism included appearing alongside Martin Luther King Jr. at the 1963 March on Washington. In 2021, she became the first Black woman inducted into France’s Panthéon.

“I cannot wait to embody Josephine Baker, bringing her fight, her love, her losses, her talent and her heroism to the big screen,” Twigs said when her casting was announced in May. “She lives on in our hearts as a visionary, ground-breaking woman whose story is as powerful as it is relevant today.”

The project has the support of Baker’s sons Jean-Claude Bouillon Baker and Brian Bouillon Baker, along with the Rainbow Tribe, the multiracial family of 12 children she raised at her château in southwest France.

Twigs won her first Grammy in February, taking best dance/electronic album for Eusexua. Her prior screen credits include Alma Har’el’s Honey Boy, Rupert Sanders’ The Crow and David Lowery’s Mother Mary, opposite Anne Hathaway and Michaela Coel.

StudioCanal and Bien ou Bien Productions are producing, with CPB Films, Leyland Films and Dingie as associated producers. StudioCanal is handling worldwide sales at AFM and will release the film theatrically in France, the U.K., Germany, Italy, Benelux, Poland, Australia and New Zealand.

Discount Bank will launch direct Bitcoin and Ethereum trading through its mobile app.

Customers can trade crypto without opening accounts with external exchanges.

Discount Bank will waive account management fees for the first six months.

Israel Discount Bank, a $150 billion bank, has become the first in the country to offer Bitcoin and Ethereum trading through its mobile app. From November 26, customers can buy, hold, and sell both cryptocurrencies without using a separate exchange or wallet.

This puts the bank ahead of rival Bank Leumi, which plans to launch crypto trading in early 2027.

On November 11, Israel Discount Bank announced that its new crypto trading service will go live on November 26, making it the first Israeli bank to offer direct in-app buying and selling of digital assets.

The service will initially support Bitcoin and Ethereum.

Eligible private customers aged 18 and above can trade the two assets directly from their bank accounts, provided their accounts are enabled for securities activity and they meet the bank’s terms.

Users will not need to open an account with a foreign crypto exchange or manage a separate wallet. Bitcoin and Ethereum will be held through bank wallets using Fireblocks’ digital asset infrastructure, while proceeds from sales can return directly to the customer’s bank account.

Discount Bank CEO Avi Levi called the launch “a first step” toward building the bank’s digital currency infrastructure.

One of the key features is the treatment of crypto taxes. Discount Bank said customers will be able to offset capital gains and losses from digital-asset activity against other investments in their portfolio for withholding-tax purposes.

This keeps the tax calculation within the banking and investment account, rather than leaving customers to handle the process separately.

The bank will also waive account management fees for the first six months. However, trading commissions are reported at 2% per purchase or sale.

The launch could give Discount Bank an early lead as Israeli banks compete to attract customers interested in crypto.

Bank Leumi announced a similar plan in August, allowing customers to trade Bitcoin, Ethereum and Solana through its Leumi Trade app. However, the service is not expected to launch until early 2027, as the bank moves ahead with its partnership with Galaxy Digital.

This is not Leumi’s first attempt to enter crypto trading. In 2022, it planned to launch a similar service with Paxos, but the Bank of Israel blocked the move.

10 things to know about RSV in babies, from symptoms to prevention

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The CDC's guide to RSV in babies covers early symptoms, who faces the most risk, when to seek emergency care and the two ways to protect infants.

Most parents have sat up with a baby through a cold, and RSV in an infant often looks like nothing more than that. The stakes are far higher. "RSV was the leading cause of infant hospitalization in the U.S. before the introduction of RSV immunizations," the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said. A family also has less to fall back on once a baby is sick. Widely available antiviral treatments exist for flu and COVID-19, according to the CDC, and RSV is not among them. That makes RSV a serious illness that families do better to head off than to wait out. The useful work happens before a baby gets sick and in the first watchful days of illness, not at the pharmacy counter.

Prevention has become possible, because babies can now be protected before they catch the virus. Most babies still begin the season without that protection. Only 39.6% of U.S. infants were protected by a maternal vaccine or an infant antibody in October 2025, as the season began, according to the CDC. Many families therefore have to rely on spotting trouble early. That is hard when the first signs of RSV look like those of an ordinary cold, and when a baby's age matters more than the baby's health history. Parents of a healthy, full-term newborn have no reason to assume their child is safe from serious illness.

The CDC explained how RSV affects babies and how families can shield them, so parents could understand both what the illness does and how to prevent it. Its guidance covers the quieter signs that show up in the youngest infants, the moments that call for emergency care, and the choice between a vaccine during pregnancy and an antibody for the baby. Many of a family's biggest decisions about RSV come before birth and in a baby's first weeks, when parents and doctors can time protection to the season. Those decisions work best when parents plan them ahead with a doctor. Protection lowers a baby's risk without erasing it, though. Knowing the warning signs still matters for every baby, protected or not.

For small children, a hospital stay for RSV was never a rare event. The CDC estimates that each year in the U.S., before immunizations became available, RSV put 58,000 to 80,000 children under 5 in the hospital. With tens of thousands of young children admitted each year, a family whose child caught the virus faced a real chance of a hospital stay, even though most infections are mild. That outcome is the one parents are working to prevent.

Immunizing babies can reduce that toll without ending it. The CDC projects that maternal vaccines and infant antibody shots together could prevent about 48% of RSV hospitalizations in babies under 1 if uptake matched last season's level. That would be a meaningful cut from the tens of thousands of yearly admissions before immunizations. Many babies would still end up in the hospital, though, and families have good reason to plan around that risk.

The danger is also building this winter. In its December update, the CDC reports that emergency visits and hospitalizations for RSV are rising among children up to age 4. A rising season is not necessarily a worse one. The agency expects this season's national peak in hospitalization rates to be similar to last season's, but only with moderate confidence. That forecast covers people of all ages rather than infants alone. For babies, the size of the benefit depends on how many of them have protection, so the outcome will turn partly on how many families act before the peak.

Hospital visits are climbing now, and families still have time to protect a baby before the season peaks.

Careful households can keep a baby away from coughing relatives and crowded rooms, but they can rarely keep RSV out of a young child's life for good. Most children have had RSV by age 2, according to the CDC. For a baby, then, catching the virus in the first couple of years is a normal part of early childhood, not a stroke of bad luck that only a few families suffer. RSV is common everywhere, with an estimated 64 million cases worldwide each year, according to NIAID. No family can count on its habits or its address to keep a child clear of the virus, and parents should expect their baby to catch RSV sooner or later.

With most children catching RSV, the question parents can act on is how well protected their baby will be at the time of infection, not whether infection will happen. The answer to that question shapes where a family should put its effort. Keeping a newborn home and asking visitors to wash their hands still helps, but those steps can only put off contact with the virus. The bigger difference comes from getting protection in place before the baby is exposed, so that the baby already has defenses when the infection arrives.

Most children catch RSV, but only some become seriously ill. That makes the next question for parents which babies face the greatest danger.

Some babies face RSV with weaker defenses than others because of how they were born or what their bodies already have to manage. Beyond young age itself, the CDC identifies a set of health conditions that make a child more likely to become seriously ill from the virus. In its guidance for doctors, the CDC lists premature infants among the children at increased risk, so being born early carries the same warning for parents and for physicians. What these babies have in common is a body that protects the airway poorly. That can come from weak lungs, a weakened immune system or a condition present from birth that makes it hard to swallow or clear mucus. For a parent, that shared weakness matters more than the name of any diagnosis. A baby who already works harder to breathe has less room to get through a respiratory infection, so even a mild start to the illness deserves closer watching.

A listed condition does not set a baby apart from other infants. Instead, it adds to a risk that every young baby already carries. The CDC's guidance for doctors puts the share of babies younger than 6 months admitted to the hospital with RSV in a given year at 2 to 3 percent, a wider age range than the agency uses for that same share in its advice to parents. The agency attaches no comparable figure to any single high-risk group. Young age is therefore the base risk that can be measured, and a condition on the list adds to it rather than replacing it. The babies with the least room for error are those who carry both risks, such as a premature or medically fragile baby in the first months of life, when the risk from age alone is already at its highest. These families gain the most from protection timed before the season and from quick action when warning signs appear.

RSV in a baby can start with a runny nose, a sign parents see all winter and rarely worry about, according to the CDC. That makes RSV easy to mistake for an ordinary cold at first. A familiar start is not a safe one, and a baby who seems only sniffly at first can be much sicker days later. In the youngest infants, a cold-like start is a reason to keep watching, and the most useful thing a parent can do is notice how those signs change.

Watching a baby for RSV takes days, and a single check is not enough. After a baby catches the virus, symptoms can take 2 to 8 days to appear, according to StatPearls. For parents, that means the days after a possible exposure call for close attention, and so do the first days of illness. A family that knows its baby was near someone sick should treat the quiet days that follow as a time to watch closely, not as proof that the baby escaped.

The signal to act is a baby who gets worse, not simply a baby who is sick. Worsening symptoms are a reason to seek emergency care, so parents should watch the direction of the illness more closely than how it started. Parents who notice that change early can get their baby to care before breathing or feeding trouble takes hold.

With RSV, a calm first day proves nothing, and the days that follow show how sick the baby is.

In the first months of life, a baby with RSV may never get the stuffy nose and cough that parents expect from a respiratory virus. Babies younger than 6 months can show signs of their own, according to the CDC, so parents can't judge a newborn by the rules that fit a toddler. One of those signs is irritability, and a parent can easily put a fussier-than-usual baby down to an ordinary bad day. Another sign runs the opposite way. A baby who becomes less active is also showing a symptom, which means a sleepier, stiller infant is not necessarily getting better. Both are changes in behavior rather than in breathing, and parents can miss them at the age when missing them matters most.

The most serious sign at this age makes no sound at all: the baby stops breathing for a stretch. A pause longer than 10 seconds counts as apnea, long enough for a watching parent to notice and too long to dismiss as a skipped breath. A parent who is listening for a cough can miss it entirely, so looking at how a baby this young breathes is part of the job. Apnea is still only one sign among several, and a baby who breathes steadily but is fussy or listless still needs attention. Together, these signs ask parents to read the whole baby.

Spotting an illness that looks like a mood or a nap is a heavy load for tired parents of a newborn. For the youngest babies, protection in place before the virus arrives may matter more than watching for symptoms, and the prevention schedule is built around this age group.

For a baby under 6 months, the safest moment to act is before any of these signs appear.

Babies and adults respond to RSV in very different ways, and babies get the worse end of it. The CDC reports that infants almost always have symptoms when infected, while adults may carry the virus without any. A baby who catches RSV will nearly always show signs of it, even when those signs stay mild. For a parent, then, the real question is whether anyone recognizes those signs for what they are.

In babies under 6 months, the first clue may be a change in mood or energy rather than a sniffle. Irritability is a symptom at this age, and a baby who is unusually fussy may be sick rather than tired or hungry. Decreased activity counts too, even though a quieter, stiller baby can look like a baby having a calm day. A normal thermometer reading doesn't settle the question either, because a baby can have RSV without a fever. The useful benchmark is the baby's own normal, and any marked shift in behavior counts, whichever direction it goes.

In the youngest babies, RSV can interrupt breathing itself. Pauses in breathing that last more than 10 seconds are a recognized symptom in infants under 6 months, and a parent watching a sleeping baby can notice and count them. This sign looks nothing like a cold. A very young infant can be sick with RSV while showing none of the symptoms parents expect.

Caring for a sick baby also means dropping adult habits at the medicine cabinet. Aspirin should never be given to children, according to the CDC, even though many adults reach for it during their own illnesses.

Spotting RSV in a baby and comforting one both mean setting aside what parents know from their own colds.

Severe RSV in a baby is the same sniffly cold, only deeper in the body than parents expect. A mild RSV cold in an infant can become bronchiolitis or pneumonia, both illnesses of the lungs, according to the CDC. The early, cold-like days are therefore no proof that a baby is in the clear. In the youngest babies, the severe form is common enough for families to plan around: among infants younger than 3 months, 2 to 3 in every 100 are hospitalized with RSV. For a newborn, a hospital stay is a realistic outcome rather than a freak one. A cold in the first months of life calls for closer watching than the same cold in an older child. Parents are not waiting for a new illness to appear. They are watching for signs that the familiar one now involves the lungs.

The youngest babies have been landing in the hospital with RSV less often since protection for infants arrived, though no one has shown how much of the drop the shots explain. Hospitalization rates among babies up to 2 months old were 52% lower than in the seasons before the pandemic in one national tracking system and 45% lower in another, according to CDC. The newborns most likely to need a hospital bed are the group where those drops appeared. The timing matches the arrival of protection, but a match in timing is not proof that the shots caused the decline. Those results also describe all babies of that age across a season and promise nothing for any single child. Even so, they are an encouraging early sign for the infants at greatest risk.

Fewer newborns may need a hospital bed for RSV in the seasons ahead, but some still will, so parents should know what happens once a baby is admitted.

A hospital stay for RSV is about helping the baby get through the illness, not about stopping the virus. Babies admitted with RSV may need extra oxygen, according to the CDC. Hospital staff give it so the baby gets enough air while the airways are inflamed and clogged. Most babies admitted with RSV get better with this kind of supportive care within a few days. For a frightened parent, a typical stay means a few days of close monitoring and breathing support, and then the baby goes home. Doctors and nurses spend those days keeping the baby comfortable and well supplied with air while the baby's body fights off the infection.

That reassurance has limits. Recovery within a few days describes most babies who improve with supportive care, not every infant who is admitted. Some babies need far more support than an oxygen line, and doctors may put them on mechanical ventilation, with a machine doing the breathing for the baby. Ventilation is the most intensive care RSV can require. Families of newborns have the most reason to take that prospect seriously, with RSV putting 2–3 per 100 babies under 3 months old in the hospital every year. Those babies are also the ones with the least time to have built up any protection of their own.

Doctors can usually see a baby through RSV within days, but stays are common enough among newborns that the better outcome is a baby who never needs one, and immunization exists to make that happen.

Once a baby is sick with RSV, parents face one decision that matters more than any other: whether the baby can still be cared for at home. The CDC lists difficulty breathing as a sign that a baby needs emergency care, so a parent who sees a baby straining for air should get help right away. Worsening symptoms are the other signal. A baby whose cold-like illness keeps getting worse instead of easing is no longer following the course of an ordinary cold, and that change is the moment to have a doctor see the baby. The useful question at home is not how sick a baby looks at any one moment. It is whether the baby is laboring to breathe and whether the baby is getting sicker.

Parents who judge a baby's RSV by the thermometer can be misled in both directions. Many infants with RSV do not run a fever, so a normal temperature does not rule out the virus. The youngest babies also need a doctor at a much lower temperature than older children do. For a baby under 12 weeks, a reading of 100.4°F or above is a warning sign, according to the CDC. For older children, a fever counts as a warning sign only above 104°F, and only when medicine fails to bring it down. Parents might watch a temperature calmly in a toddler when the same reading in a newborn calls for a doctor.

A thermometer can tell parents when to worry about a newborn, but a normal reading can never tell them that the baby is fine.

A family can't treat RSV once a baby has it, but it can do a great deal before the virus arrives, and that work is simpler than many parents fear. The aim is to protect every infant, according to CDC guidance, and the CDC recommends two routes to get there: a vaccine for the mother during pregnancy or an antibody for the baby. For parents, the task is to pick the route that fits their timing, not to collect every shot available.

Families have no official best choice to hunt for. The two routes now cover three products, the maternal vaccine and two infant antibodies called nirsevimab and clesrovimab, and the CDC's vaccine advisers prefer none of them, according to CDC guidance. The official basis for choosing is practical: what the parents prefer, which product is available and when the birth falls relative to the RSV season. That leaves the decision in the parents' hands.

The maternal route depends on the due date. Pfizer $PFE's Abrysvo is given between 32 and 36 weeks of pregnancy and only from September through January, according to CDC guidance, so the mother has to be late in pregnancy during the fall and winter for the vaccine to be an option. Families expecting a baby in spring or summer will generally turn to the antibody instead. Mothers who choose the vaccine also accept a small trade-off. The vaccine was associated with a slight rise in high blood pressure disorders during pregnancy. That is worth raising with a doctor, but it is no reason to leave a baby unprotected.

Getting both protections is rare by design. "Most infants will not need both," the CDC said. The exceptions, according to CDC guidance, are babies whose mothers are immunocompromised or living with HIV, and those babies may get the antibody on top of the maternal vaccine. A mother with a weakened immune system may pass too few antibodies to her baby, and the extra shot is meant to make up that shortfall rather than repeat protection the baby already has.

The question to bring to the doctor is whether the baby is already covered, and if not, how to get protection in place before the season begins.

Front Office: Radhi Devlukia

The host of the A Really Good Cry podcast turned to designer Jess Cooney to create a home office-meets-studio made for deep conversations.

Radhi Devlukia isn’t afraid of tears. On her podcast, A Really Good Cry, the whole point is to get personal, and to do so with guests as varied as supermodel Miranda Kerr, dermatologists, hospice nurses, personal finance advisors—and Devlukia’s husband, fellow podcaster Jay Shetty.

Now, two years into the show, Devlukia, a best-selling author, cook, and founder of beverage company Juni, is unveiling a new studio and office space located within her and Shetty’s Los Angeles house. Devlukia turned to designer Jess Cooney, who has a namesake Berkshires-based interiors firm, to craft a space designed to support these really good cries. Cooney had never tackled a podcast studio before but was ready for the challenge, which included building a room that could both serve as a set—technical components, artificial light, and all—as well as a welcoming home office.

From the start, Cooney knew what the space would not include. “We wanted to move away from the tropes usually seen in podcast studios, like styled bookshelves or cluttered backgrounds that detract from the people being interviewed, and also anything logo or merch heavy,” she says. “And toward something that felt warmer and more inviting.”

Instead, she took cues from spaces like fashion designer Bella Freud’s London apartment, Jean Cocteau’s Villa Santo Sospir in the Côte d’Azur, and Paloma Elsesser’s home by Gregory Rockwell. The resulting office, says Cooney, “has this incredible womb-like, feminine atmosphere that cocoons and holds you.”

For Singles Day, World explains why real is the new attractive

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Singles’ Day falls on 11 November each year and has become the world’s biggest online shopping event.

New Harris Poll research on behalf of World finds that 81% of singles would be more likely to use a dating app if every profile were confirmed as a real person.

As millions of singles celebrate Singles Day on November 11, the biggest question in online dating is no longer “are we compatible?” but “are you even real?”

A new survey conducted by The Harris Poll on behalf of World shows that trust has become the most important factor in online dating. Nearly 3 in 4 online daters (74%) say they have suspected that someone they matched with on a dating app was not a real person. Catfishing, AI-generated profiles, bots and romance scams have turned swiping into a guessing game, and singles say they are tired of it.

World’s answer is Proof of Human: a way for anyone to show they are a real, unique person online without revealing who they are.

The survey paints a clear picture of how much doubt singles now bring to every match:

In other words, being real now outranks looks. For a growing number of singles, authenticity is the new chemistry.

Singles are not giving up on dating apps. They are asking for proof. According to the research, 81% would be more likely to use a dating app if every profile were confirmed as belonging to a real person.

They are also willing to do their part. Four in five singles (80%) say they would take steps to prove there is a real person behind their own profile, as long as their matches did the same. Verification, in other words, is becoming a two-way expectation rather than a burden.

World ID is a universal set of human Proofs that lets people show they are a real, unique person online without sharing personal data such as their name, birthday or address. It is built for a world where AI can generate convincing faces, voices and conversations at scale.

The core of the system is the Orb, a spherical verification device. A person stands in front of it, and the Orb uses images of the eyes and face to confirm they are a unique, living human. That check is turned into an anonymous cryptographic credential, a verified World ID, stored in the World ID App. The process does not require a name, email or gender.

The result is a simple promise for singles: the person on the other end of the chat is real.

The largest names in dating are moving in this direction. World first partnered with Match Group, the parent company of Tinder, on a World ID pilot in Japan in 2025. In April 2026, at World’s Lift Off event, the partnership expanded to the United States.

Tinder users who verify with World ID can display a human badge on their profile, signaling to potential matches that a verified, real person is behind it. Verification is optional.

Dating is only one use case. World ID is also being integrated into video calls, document signing, live event ticketing and gaming, with partners including Zoom and Docusign. The common thread is the same one singles describe in the survey: in the age of AI, knowing you are dealing with a real human is becoming essential.
