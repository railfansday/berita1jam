Online Course Tutor Retrieval Architecture: Designing Fallbacks That Keep Answers Grounded

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Short answer: use a staged retrieval design with explicit collections, bounded queries, and traceable source context; make each fallback narrower and more conservative than the previous stage.

An online course tutor is a small production system with an awkward failure mode: a plausible answer backed by the wrong lesson is harder to notice than an obvious error. I have been paged for missed jobs and duplicate deliveries, so I treat retrieval the same way I treat queue work: every stage needs a contract, a timeout, and an idempotent handoff.

The contract starts with the answer the student should see. A question about “the gradient descent lab” should return a lesson section, its course and tenant, and a citation anchor. If the first pass cannot satisfy those fields, it must say so and move to a defined fallback. Silent broadening is how cross-course answers leak into a tutor.

Keep ingestion, querying, and citation as separate observable stages. Ingestion normalizes a representative set of lessons, preserves tenant and access-control metadata on every indexed item, and records a stable document identifier. Querying searches the course collection first, with a bounded top-k and a deadline. Citation assembly checks that returned identifiers still belong to the requesting tenant before the language model sees them.

Fallback one can widen within the same course: a lexical match over titles and headings catches an unusual term that embeddings missed. Fallback two can search a curated, tenant-wide collection, but it should require a stronger confidence threshold and return fewer passages. The final fallback is an explicit “I could not find this in the course material” response, with a path for the instructor to add or correct a source.

That last step matters. A tutor that always produces text has no useful failure signal.

I don't let a timeout silently promote a broader search. In one runbook review, the dangerous path was a retry that reused the old broad query after the course index had recovered; the answer looked fine, but its citation belonged to a neighboring course. The fix was a stage token carried through the trace, a maximum of three retrieval attempts, and a final authorization check against the enrollment snapshot. That sequence takes more lines than a single search call, yet it makes a duplicate delivery and a late response converge on the same decision. The tutor either has an approved source or returns no answer.

I model each retrieval result as a record, not a loose chunk:

The check is deliberately boring. A score is not authorization. A high-scoring chunk from another tenant is still unusable, and a low-scoring chunk should not be promoted merely because the first query timed out.

Give every stage a budget and a reason code. For example, course_vector_timeout, course_low_recall, and tenant_lexical_hit are more useful in a runbook than one boolean called retrieval_failed. The application can then alert on a rising fallback rate without paging on a single hard question.

Evaluation needs representative documents and failure cases: short definitions, long lab instructions, duplicated slide text, misspelled terms, and questions that intentionally have no answer. Measure recall for finding the right section and precision for keeping irrelevant sections out. Also test a query whose only matching passage has a revoked enrollment; the expected result is no citation, not a clever guess.

A practical trace records the collection, stage, query hash, result IDs, scores, elapsed time, and the final citation IDs. Do not log raw student prompts by default. Retain enough context to replay a miss while respecting the tenant's data policy.

The write path should be equally explicit. A collection is created before ingestion, vectors are upserted with the metadata needed for authorization, and deletion removes both the item and its collection membership when a course is retired. These are separate state transitions, so a retry worker can safely repeat a transition keyed by the document ID.

The choice is less about a leaderboard score than about who owns the operational boundary. Pinecone is a managed vector service with a focused API. Weaviate offers an open-source and managed path with a broader schema layer. Postgres with pgvector keeps vectors beside transactional course and enrollment data, which can simplify joins and policy checks. Infrai is another option when a team wants one plain REST API and no SDK to install; its discovery surface is public and self-describing, and its wider backend surface can keep adjacent services behind one key.

The catch is that a broad interface does not remove retrieval design work. Use a direct specialist when you need a feature your evaluation depends on, such as a particular hybrid-ranking implementation, tight database transactions, or a self-hosted deployment mandate. Stick with Postgres when the authorization join is the hard part. Choose a managed vector service when your team cannot own index capacity and recovery. Your mileage may vary because corpus shape and enrollment rules dominate the result.

In a real worker, add the document identity and idempotency key required by the discovered schema, retry 429 responses with bounded exponential backoff, and emit the stage trace before acknowledging the queue message. Never send the credential to a presigned URL or expose it to a browser.

I would not use this sample as a complete ingestion client. It is a control-path sketch: discovery supplies the exact schema, while the tutor owns authorization, retries, and citation policy. That division keeps a provider change from rewriting the answer contract.

Start with a course-scoped collection and a strict citation contract. Add lexical fallback only after measuring misses on representative lessons. Add tenant-wide search only with an explicit policy and a higher threshold. Keep a no-answer outcome, because it is the only fallback that cannot invent a source.

Before launch, replay duplicate upserts and deletes, revoke a student's enrollment mid-query, and inject timeouts at each stage. Confirm that every emitted citation maps to the requested tenant and course. Then watch fallback rate, citation rejection rate, and unanswered-question samples as first-class SRE signals.

Microsoft Expands Protections and Controls for Sovereign Cloud

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Microsoft is introducing new tools within its sovereign cloud offerings designed to give organizations tighter control over where their data resides, who can access it, and how critical systems operate, especially in light of AI becoming a bigger part of enterprise infrastructure.

In a recent announcement, Douglas Phillips, president and chief technology officer of Microsoft Specialized Clouds, outlined updates spanning public cloud, private cloud, and partner environments. The changes target governments, regulated industries, and organizations that need tighter control over sensitive workloads without abandoning cloud-based AI capabilities.

"Digital sovereignty should give organizations greater control and security over the technology they depend on, while continuing to innovate," Phillips wrote.

One of the biggest additions is Microsoft's Sovereign Control Panel, which will enter public preview at Microsoft Ignite. Available through the Azure portal, the tool will give IT administrators one place to manage data sovereignty settings, spot compliance issues, and identify potential risks that could disrupt operations.

Microsoft is also tightening controls over who can access sensitive systems. Its Data Guardian feature will require oversight by personnel based in Europe when Microsoft engineers access certain production systems in the European Union and European Free Trade Association. Another tool, Production Touch Ledger, will keep tamper-resistant records of that access, giving organizations a clearer picture of who accessed their systems and what they did.

Microsoft is also giving customers more control over encryption. External key management with Azure Managed HSM is now generally available, letting organizations keep their main encryption keys on hardware they own or manage outside Microsoft's cloud.

The company is also expanding its Sovereign Private Cloud offerings, giving organizations more options to run systems with or without an internet connection. New capabilities include GitHub Enterprise Local, GitHub Copilot with locally hosted AI models, and additional data services running on Azure Local.

The push for greater operational control comes as Microsoft is also reconsidering how enterprises should secure increasingly autonomous AI systems.

As reported by Redmond magazine, Microsoft is expanding Project Perception to address risks posed by what the company calls "agentic insiders," AI agents capable of accessing sensitive information and performing actions without constant human supervision.

That initiative introduces plans for Guardian Agents, specialized security agents designed to monitor other AI systems, evaluate their behavior and intervene when their actions become risky.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," said Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security.

Project Perception is about limiting what AI agents can do. Microsoft's sovereign cloud expansion tackles a different set of questions: where those systems run, who gets to administer them, and how the data stays protected.

Taken together, the two efforts point to a shift in Microsoft's enterprise AI strategy. The company is putting more weight on oversight, not just automation.

Phillips said AI is making these questions more pressing as organizations roll out intelligent systems across sensitive business processes and critical infrastructure.

The Souls of Animals

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every month, I spend hundreds of hours and thousands of dollars keeping The Marginalian going. For two decades, it has remained free, ad-free, AI-free, fully human and alive thanks to patronage from readers. I have no staff, no interns, not even an assistant — a thoroughly one-woman labor of love that is also my life and my livelihood. For an act of resistance to the tyranny of algorithms, try the Marginalian newsletter—undistracted notes on the search for meaning, free, ad-free, AI-free, fully human since 2006. (Here is an example.)

“They do not sweat and whine about their condition,” Walt Whitman wrote of the other animals, “they do not lie awake in the dark and weep for their sins, they do not make me sick discussing their duty to God, not one is dissatisfied, not one is demented with the mania of owning things.”

Here was “the poet of the body and the poet of the soul” holding up a mirror to us creatures inhabiting an animal body complicated by a soul — that organ of want and worry which we ourselves invented to explain why we make art, why we fall in love, why we yearn to converse with reality in prayers and postulates.

It is daring enough to ask what a soul actually is. Carl Jung knew that it defies the substance we are made of: “The soul is partly in eternity and partly in time.” Virginia Woolf knew that it defies our best technology of thought: “One can’t write directly about the soul. Looked at, it vanishes.” It is doubly daring to question the age-old dogma that the soul is the province of the human animal alone. Even as we have incrementally and reluctantly admitted other creatures into the temple of consciousness, we have denied them souls — denied them, because our tools of communication and computation have failed to probe it, an inner life capable of imagination and play, of love and grief, of dreams and wonder. And yet our very language defies our denial: the word animal comes from the Latin for soul.

In 1991, long before we came to consider the soul of an octopus, long before fMRI and EEG studies revealed not only that birds dream but what they dream about, Gary Kowalski took up this daring question in The Souls of Animals (public library) — an inquiry into the “spiritual lives” (and into what that means) of whooping cranes, elephants, jackdaws, gorillas, songbirds, horses, dogs, and cats. At its center is the idea that spirituality — which he defines as “the development of a moral sense, the appreciation of beauty, the capacity for creativity, and the awareness of one’s self within a larger universe as well as a sense of mystery and wonder about it all” — is a natural byproduct of “the biological order and in the ecology shared by all life.” (There are in this view echoes of Kepler, who believed that the Earth itself is an ensouled body, and of myriad native cosmogonies that regard other animals as sources of more-than-human wisdom and emissaries of the numinous.)

Kowalski — a parish minister by vocation, who spends his days praying with the dying, blessing bonds of love, and helping people navigate moral quandaries — celebrates the soul as “the magic of life,” as that which “gives life its sublimity and grandeur,” and reflects:

For ancient peoples, the soul was located in the breath or the blood. For me, soul resides at the point where our lives intersect with the timeless, in our love of goodness, our passion for beauty, our quest for meaning and truth. In asking whether animals have souls, we are inquiring whether they share in the qualities that make life more than a mere struggle for survival, endowing existence with dignity and élan.

Many people think of soul as the element of personality that survives bodily death, but for me it refers to something much more down-to-earth. Soul is the marrow of our existence as sentient, sensitive beings. It’s soul that’s revealed in great works of art, and soul that’s lifted up in awe when we stand in silence under a night sky burning with billions of stars. When we speak of a soulful piece of music, we mean one that comes out of infinite depths of feeling. When we speak of the soul of a nation, we mean its capacity for valor and visionary change… Soul is present wherever our lives intersect the dimension of the holy: in moments of intimacy, in flights of fancy, and in rituals that hallow the evanescent events of our lives with enduring significance. Soul is what makes each of our lives a microcosm — not merely a meaningless fragment of the universe, but at some level a reflection of the whole.

Half a century after Henry Beston insisted that “we need another and a wiser and perhaps a more mystical concept of animals,” for they are “gifted with extensions of the senses we have lost or never attained, living by voices we shall never hear,” Kowalski writes:

Without anthropomorphizing our nonhuman relations we can acknowledge that animals share many human characteristics. They have individual likes and dislikes, moods and mannerisms, and possess their own integrity, which suffers when not respected. They play and are curious about their world. They develop friendships and sometimes risk their own lives to help others. They have “animal faith,” a spontaneity and directness that can be most refreshing… all the traits indicative of soul. For soul is not something we can see or measure. We can observe only its outward manifestations: in tears and laughter, in courage and heroism, in generosity and forgiveness. Soul is what’s behind-the-scenes in the tough and tender moments when we are most intensely and grippingly alive.

By investigating the inner lives of other creatures, Kowalski argues, we are invariably deepening our own:

As [modern] shamans, we are allowed to examine enigmas like “What makes us human?” and “What makes life sacred?” We can ask not only about the mating behavior and survival strategies of other animals but whether they have souls and spirits like our own. The danger here is that we are often in over our heads. But at least we are swimming in deep water and out of the shallows. In searching for answers to such queries, I have found, we not only enrich our understanding of other creatures, we also gain insight into ourselves.

There is an inwardness in other living beings that awakens what is innermost in ourselves. I have often marveled, for instance, watching a flock of shore birds. On an invisible cue, they simultaneously rise off the beach and into the air, then turn and bank seawards in tight formation. They are so finely coordinated and attuned in their aeronautics it is as though they share a common thought, or even a group mind, guiding their ascent. At such moments, I feel there are depths of “inner space” in nature that can never be sounded. And it is out of those same depths, in me, that awe arises as I contemplate the synchronicity of their flight. To contain such depths is to participate in the realm of spirit.

We have invented no greater expression of our inwardness than music — the language of the soul, with its eternal translation between mathematics and mystery. We know that other animals partake of that language — each spring birds sing the world back to life, each summer cicadas serenade the sun with their living mandolin, and when we set out to tell the cosmos who we are, a whale song joined Bulgarian folk music and Bach on The Golden Record.

Birds, Kowalski observes, sing for reasons beyond the pragmatic — their song is “far from a mechanical performance” and “much more complex than a simple cry of self-assertion.” It is music, which is distinguished from noise by an organizing principle of creative intent, and creativity may be the purest evidence of soul. Kowalski writes:

Surprisingly, many birds are relatively insensitive to pitch. But the best singers employ all the elements of tone, interval, rhythm, theme, and variation in complex and highly pleasing combinations. And what is music if not the deliberate arrangement of sound in aesthetic patterns?

Greatly influenced by philosopher Martin Buber’s I-Thou model of relating, Kowalski admonishes against relying on our own frames of reference in assaying what other creatures are expressing and how it is being expressed:

The tempo of life is faster-paced for birds than for people. This is one of the reasons the individual notes in bird song are so short, sometimes distinguishable only with a spectrograph, and why the compositions of birds last a few seconds at most, compared to an hour or more for a human symphony. It is also why birds sing in the upper registers (just as the pitch on a phonograph record rises when played at high speed). To the birds, with a metabolism continually in allegro, human beings must appear to be lazy and dim-brained creatures indeed. Just as our music reflects the rhythm and intensity of our inner life, the music of birds expresses the flash and flutter of their nervous and high-strung existence.

Examining another subset of the creative impulse — visual art — Kowalski cites Desmond Morris’s famous 1950s studies, which found that non-human primates given pens and paints not only became adept at using them with “a distinct feel for symmetry and balance,” but developed individual styles of drawing. He considers what that indicates:

Art arises from a spiritual longing that all people share: to make our mark on the world and to spend our life energy in a work that rises above the mundane, adding grace to existence. We respond to the light of the world around us by giving expression to our own inner light, and when the two are on the same wavelength, the world seems more brilliant and finely focused.

Insisting that such spiritual longings do not belong to human beings alone, he cites an astonishing case study:

In 1982 Jerome Witkin, a professor of art at Syracuse University and a respected authority on abstract expressionism, was invited to view a collection of drawings by a “mystery artist.” The professor was busy at the time, preparing for a traveling exhibition. Nevertheless, he was sufficiently intrigued to accept the invitation.

“These drawings are very lyrical, very, very beautiful,” the professor said when he saw the portfolio. “They are so positive and affirmative and tense, the energy is so compact and controlled, it’s just incredible.”

“This piece is so graceful, so delicate,” he said of one drawing. “I can’t get most of my students to fill a page like this.”

Only after he had finished his professional evaluation did Witkin learn the identity of the artist: a fourteen-year-old, 8,400-pound Asian elephant named Siri who lived in Syracuse’s Burnet Park Zoo. Siri’s keeper, David Gucwa, had seen her tracing lines with sticks and stones in the dust of her cage. Against the wishes of the zoo’s superintendent, who scoffed at the notion of an artistic elephant, Gucwa had given her pads of paper and charcoal, permitting her to express herself more freely.

When Witkin showed Siri’s drawings to a colleague without context — an expert on children’s drawings charing the university’s art education department — she firmly concluded that they were not done by a child. Witkin himself readily likened them to the work of Willem de Kooning, wishing the painter himself could see Siri’s art.

It was this report of Siri that inspired May Sarton — one of my favorite poets and favorite thinkers — to reimagine these reckonings in a poem. (The footnote of credit in Sarton’s collection is how I discovered Kowalski’s book.)

The drawings were abstract, Delicate, Like Japanese calligraphy. When the painter de Kooning Was shown them, he said, “Interesting. Not done by a child, I think, Or if so, an extraordinary child.” “The artist is an elephant, Sir, Named Siri.”

It had once come about That the keeper noticed Her sensitive trunk Drawing designs in the dust. After an argument With the head of the zoo Who laughed at him, The keeper himself Brought large sheets of paper And boxes of charcoal And laid them at Siri’s feet. For an hour at a time In happy concentration The elephant created designs. Like Japanese calligraphy. What artist’s hand As skillful As that sensuous, sensitive trunk?

Two decades after Iris Murdoch found psychological symmetry between art and morality, locating in both “an occasion for unselfing,” Kowlaski turns to the acts of selflessness and compassion that evince a moral faculty — that fundament of a soul. Pelicans and crows, he notes, have been known to care for blind comrades. Darwin himself reported of a band of monkeys coming to the aid of member seized by an eagle, at the risk of their own lives. But nothing renders such morally tinted actions more vivid and more moving than one nineteenth-century naturalist’s account of a misfire.

Working in an era when “collecting specimens” meant killing creatures, he aimed at a tern but only wounded the bird, which fell helplessly into the sea. Immediately, other terns began circling above “manifesting much apparent solicitude,” until two of them dove down toward their wounded comrade. They lifted him up, one at each wing, carried him several yards, and gently put him down before another two picked him up, and so the group took turns carrying him the entire distance to the shore. The naturalist was so moved by this display of compassion and solidarity that, although he was within shot of the rock on which the wounded tern had been rested, he couldn’t bring himself to finish what he had set out to do.

To witness such a scene is to be stilled with wonder and with humility — which, as Rachel Carson so poignantly wrote, “are wholesome emotions, and they do not exist side by side with a lust for destruction.” A generation after her, and well ahead of our still dawning awakening to the ecological and ethical dignity of other species, Kowalaski reflects:

If we are to keep our family homestead — third stone from the sun — safe for coming generations, we must awaken to a new respect for the family of life.

We are kin to, and must be kind to, all creation. Overcoming speciesism — the illusion of human superiority — will be the next step in our moral and spiritual evolution.

To behold such a display of moral feeling with our own eyes is stirring enough, but to be witnessed back by another creature’s eyes is nothing short of a spiritual experience. In a passage that calls to mind Alan Lightman’s transcendent account of looking into the eyes of an osprey, Kowalski writes:

It is difficult to probe the inward awareness of another being. The realm of what one mystic called “the interior castle” is wholly private and wrapped in solitude. But when we look into another’s eyes — even into the eyes of an animal — we may find a small window into that inner sanctum, a window through which our souls can hail and greet one another.

The act of making eye contact with another being presupposes a conscious self behind either pair of peepers: I see you seeing me, and I am aware that you are aware that we are looking at each other.

Perhaps in the end it is not we who have the power to acknowledge or deny the souls of other creatures but other creatures who confer soul-ness upon us. Kowalski writes:

If by soul we mean our sense of self, our identity as particular persons, then our souls are interwoven with those of other living beings… We know ourselves as human, in part, through our relationships with the nonhuman world.

We are rather unsure of ourselves. What distinguishes our species may be this inward anxiety. While other animals may be endowed with special gifts—acute hearing, keen eyesight, incredible speed — human beings are nothing special. This is both a biological and a moral judgment. Lack of specialization makes us highly adaptable, but it also means we have no fixed form or definite identity. Without many inborn instincts to guide us, we as human beings need models for how to live. We need a sense of our own possibilities and limits, and we find them not only in the artificial rules and restraints imposed by human society but in the lessons for living suggested by biology and the earth itself. We are the younger siblings in life’s family — the perpetual neonates of the animal world. In a fundamental way we need other creatures to tell us who we are.

Out of this arises an urgency more than ethical, more than ecological, but existential — nothing less than examining what we are and why we are here at all:

What profit do we have if we gain the whole world and lose or forfeit our own souls? The human race may survive without the chimpanzees, orangutans, and other wild creatures who share the planet. But we will have attenuated the conditions that are necessary for our own “ensoulment”… And when we look into the mirror there will be less and less to love.

There is a glimmering of eternity about our lives. In the vastness of time and space, our lives are indeed small and ephemeral, yet not utterly insignificant. Our lives do matter. Because we care for one another and have feelings, because we can dream and imagine, because we are the kinds of creatures who make music and create art, we are not merely disconnected fragments of the universe but at some level reflect the beauty and splendor of the whole. And because all life shares in One Spirit, we can recognize this indwelling beauty in other creatures.

Is Your Penis Getting Smaller as You Age? This Common Factor Could Hide One to Two Inches.

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

IT PROBABLY SOUNDS like a nightmare scenario to think your penis is getting shorter. Maybe not even being visible at all. But it can happen as you age, and when it does, doctors refer to it as a buried penis.

Now it's not that your penis is physically getting shorter. It's just that most of it is hidden. More on that below.

CONVINCED YOU LOST an inch? Thankfully, your penis isn’t disappearing; it’s just hidden beneath fat tissue.

Justin Dubin, MD, urologist and men’s health specialist at Baptist Health, says that generally when you can’t see the penis, it’s usually because of low abdominal tissue surrounding the penis.

Essentially, your penis is still its regular size, but it doesn’t extend outside the body. Drogo Montague, MD, a urologist in Ohio tells MH what that looks like. “There is an opening where the penis would normally hang, and the head is usually flush with skin, but there’s no shaft outside of the body.”

Now you don’t just wake up one day with inches of your penis vanished. The effect occurs on a continuum, says Dr. Montague. You might notice at first, for instance, that your penis seems to get shorter and shorter. (Though, this is not the same as the small amount of shrinkage that some men see with age, which is due to skin cells around the penis becoming less elastic.)

“This is a serious condition because it will inhibit urination, intimacy, and personal hygiene,” says Scott Miller, MD, urologist and medical director of Wellstar Urology. “It can also strongly impact self-esteem and overall emotional health.”

MOST CASES OF buried penis are due to one thing: obesity.

While the rates of buried penises haven’t been thoroughly studied, Dr. Montague sees more men in his office today dealing with the issue than he has in the past. His booked-up calendar is no coincidence. The World Health Organization reported that 1 in 7 people is living with obesity. And even though we are living in the golden age of GLP-1s, half of adults worldwide are expected to be overweight or obese by 2050.

The penis itself has two erection chambers. About one-third is inside the body and two-thirds are outside in the penile shaft. When you’re overweight or with severe obesity—and noticing a change in length—what’s changing is basically how much of your penis is inside your body and how much is outside.

“With extreme obesity, it’s like the obese body engulfs the penis. A full buried penis is when the entire penis shaft is buried below the surface of the skin,” says Dr. Montague. So it's still there, but you just can't see it all.

There are a few factors, some starting at birth.

There is a low chance of a baby being born with abnormalities in which the ligaments that attach to the penis are weak. Fortunately, Dr. Dubin says this is usually identified and surgically corrected early on.

Another cause can be “any surgery on your penis” that’s gone wrong, says Dr. Dubin. That includes circumcision, a procedure done on 71.2 percent of American guys are circumcised at birth (or even as an adult). If a doctor does not remove an appropriate amount of foreskin, there is a risk of the penis dropping back into the fatty area surrounding it.

But any procedure that involves the penis runs the risk of scar tissue and possible infection. That includes penile injections and penis enlargement.

“The ironic part is that while a lot of these things try to make your penis look bigger or be bigger, the complication is it can make your penis actually smaller and cause buried penis,” says Dr. Dubin.

Lymphedema can cause swelling in the scrotum. As lymph fluid accumulates, you might see the makings of a buried penis.

Also known as acne inversa, the skin condition can create small and extremely painful lumps under the skin. Lumps usually appear in the armpits, inner thighs, and—you guessed it—your groin. This happens because the body mistakenly attacks hair follicles in the sweat glands, causing pockets of pus and other scarring.

Sometimes when the prostate is removed due to prostate cancer surgery, men may lose about an inch of penile length. “That’s the result of pulling the urethra up to attach to the bladder,” says Dr. Montague. Still, it seems like most penile shortening rebounds as time passes after your surgery, so don't let that stop you from getting your prostate checked.

The condition occurs when scar tissue forms in the penis and it causes a curved erection. The curvature may give the appearance of a shorter length. The issue often arises because of injury to the penis during sex or micro-tears that accumulated from sports or physical accidents affecting the genital area. Fortunately, if diagnosed early, there are drugs and procedures that could straighten out the penis. Read more about it here.

WHEN YOU HAVE a buried penis, certain activities become more difficult. Right away, you might find it hard to pee. Dr. Dubin says guys may not be able to get the stream started and might have poor flow. Buried penis can also cause trouble in the form of dribbling and incontinence.

The area is also tough to clean, which leads to a host of issues that might trigger fungal or bacterial infections or skin breakdown. Buried penis is associated, for example, with a rare skin condition called lichen sclerosus. It’s an inflammatory disorder that causes itchy patches. The skin that hosts these patches becomes very delicate.

Beyond that, penetrative sex remains an ongoing issue. “These men can’t have sex or sex is very difficult,” says Dr. Montague.

Mentally, having a buried penis can cause depression and self-esteem issues. “Most men associate their penis with their masculinity, and they associate fertility with masculinity,” says Dr. Dubin. “These kinds of issues can have strong mental health implications.”

IF YOUR BURIED penis is obesity-related, losing weight will definitely help. But keep in mind that, even after weight loss, you might have excess skin and tissue that keeps your penis buried.

“Sometimes, unfortunately, in very obese people, the damage has already been done, and more aggressive treatment options may be required,” Dr. Dubin. In that scenario, you may need surgery—which is also an option for people who don’t lose weight. Dr. Miller says surgeons would focus on removing the surrounding fat and tissue and repositioning the penis.

“That allows the penis to come outside of the body,” says Dr. Montague. “In most buried penis surgeries, skin and fat are cut out.”

Having a buried penis can be embarrassing to talk about, Dr. Dubin says. It's disturbing to have this happen and it can take a toll on your well-being. If you feel it's getting in the way of living fully, consider talking with a counselor or other mental health professional. It's also crucial to see a doctor if you’re having urinary, hygiene, or sexual health issues because of a buried penis.

Eliminating invasive rats could help islands survive rising seas

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Removing invasive rats could help protect low-lying islands from rising seas by restoring seabirds, boosting coral reef growth, and increasing sand production.

Low-lying atoll islands are usually written off as the first places a rising sea will cover. Around some of them, though, the coral reef is growing fast enough to keep pace with the sea through the end of this century.

One important factor is whether invasive rats have driven away the island’s seabirds.

Where seabirds nest, their droppings feed the reef.

Lagoon reefs there build upward almost twice as fast as reefs beside rat-overrun islands and produce more than twice as much sand.

Native forests produce more than four times as much fine root growth as areas dominated by coconut palms. This can hold the island’s soil in place.

Nick Graham, a marine ecologist at Lancaster University‘s Lancaster Environment Centre, led a team that measured all three factors on 28 islands in the Indian and Pacific Oceans.

The team compared islands as they are today, with and without rats.

“Atoll islands are not static, they are naturally active systems that can change and adapt to their environmental conditions if they are allowed to do so,” Graham said.

Boobies, frigatebirds, terns and noddies feed at sea, up to 620 miles (1,000 kilometers) from the island where they nest. They come home to roost, and the guano they deposit on the island contains nitrogen.

On islands with the largest seabird colonies, their droppings deposited more than 320 pounds of nitrogen per acre (366 kilograms per hectare) each year.

Rats that arrived on ships eat seabird eggs and chicks, and on most atolls the bird colonies are gone. Many islands have also lost their native forest to coconut plantations.

These palms now make up about 60 percent of the forest on Pacific atolls.

The team counted nests on islands in the Seychelles, the Chagos Archipelago and Tetiaroa Atoll in French Polynesia.

Islands with no rats and mostly native forest got the heaviest rain of bird nitrogen. The rest got very little.

Corals near seabird islands photosynthesized more and laid down more limestone skeleton. From that, the team estimated how fast each reef could build upward.

Lagoon reefs beside rat-free islands rose by an estimated 0.23 inches (5.9 millimeters) a year.

This is 1.8 times the rate of reef rise beside rat-infested islands. Ocean-facing reefs showed a smaller and less certain edge.

For the central Indian Ocean, the sea is projected to rise about 0.24 inches (6 millimeters) a year by 2100 if the world warms by 2°C (3.6°F).

The bird-fed lagoon reefs could nearly match that. The reefs beside rat islands, however, are predicted to fall short of even today’s rise of about 0.16 inches (4 millimeters) a year.

A reef that keeps pace with rising seas helps weaken waves before they reach the shore.

“The study shows beautifully how atolls are an intimately interconnected island-reef system, a geo-ecological system,” said Yadvinder Malhi, an ecologist at the University of Oxford and a co-author.

Parrotfish scrape coral and excrete it as sand. On some Indian Ocean reefs they produce up to 85 percent of the new sand.

Around islands with seabirds, parrotfish had more energy available, and the surrounding reefs produced 2.3 to 2.8 times as much sand.

In an interview with Earth.com, Graham was asked what surprised him most.

“The increase in sediment production by seabirds was something that had not been investigated before, and the increase is quite dramatic,” he said.

He put a figure on it for a hypothetical island of about 35 acres (14 hectares), roughly three quarters of a mile long.

With seabirds present, the surrounding reefs would produce about 220 cubic yards (170 cubic meters) more sediment each year than reefs around an island without seabirds.

“That is a really meaningful quantity,” Graham said.

Not all of that sand reaches the beach. The grains on the reef and the grains on the shore match closely, though, which points to a steady supply.

On Tetiaroa, the team measured fine roots in forest plots for two years.

Where native trees such as Pisonia grandis covered more than 75 percent of the ground, each square yard of soil held an estimated 8.2 miles (13.2 kilometers) of fine root.

Where native cover was below 75 percent, the figure was 1.8 miles (2.9 kilometers).

Coconut palms have short, rounded root systems. Native broadleaf trees grow long, dense roots that hold sand in place when storm waves wash over an island.

Seabirds strengthened that effect. With plenty of bird nitrogen in the soil, root growth rose by about 9 percent for every extra 1% of native forest cover. With few birds, more native forest didn’t change root growth at all.

The reef growth and sand figures come from six islands in the Chagos Archipelago, and the root figures from 19 forest plots on five islands of Tetiaroa.

The team inferred the gains from islands that already differ, rather than following one atoll from rat eradication through to a faster-growing reef.

How much reef sand actually reaches an island, and how reef growth changes the scouring of a shoreline, still have to be measured. Island size and local currents also shape what happens to any one atoll.

Jennifer Appoo, a co-author at the University of Seychelles, said the results matter most where seawalls and other engineering are unaffordable or out of place.

Many of the Seychelles’ atolls are uninhabited or sparsely inhabited, she said.

Graham told Earth.com that eradicating invasive predators such as rats should be the top priority for island managers.

It helps the island’s wildlife and the reef around it, he said, and this study shows it can boost the islands’ climate resilience too.

“I think what is key here from a policy perspective is that restoring atoll islands can have major gains in two core areas: biodiversity conservation, and climate adaptation,” he said.

Rats have already been removed from about 1,000 islands worldwide.

Asked by Earth.com which islands he is watching next, Graham said he has just started an Island-Ocean Research Hub at Lancaster.

It will study islands from the tropics to the Arctic, in the Seychelles, the Red Sea, the Mediterranean, the U.K. and Arctic Norway.

The full study was published in the journal Science Advances.

Living far away from family is hard at the best of times, but it becomes particularly difficult around the holidays. If there's one thing that makes it a little easier for me, it's being able to mix and match festive traditions from different countries to create something that's uniquely ours. As an Italian living in France with a British husband, I love bringing together the best of each culture for a rather extravagant Christmas. On Christmas Eve, we have oysters and Champagne, as per French tradition, followed by an Italian feast and plenty of Christmas crackers—although I draw the line at Christmas pudding, preferring a simple pandoro over anything else.

Whether, like me, you spend Christmas far from your family, are planning to chase the sun this December or are driving home for the holidays, one of the great joys of Christmas is making memories and indulging in nostalgic traditions. All over the globe, different countries and cultures celebrate the season in different ways, marking the holiday with long-established rites and rituals or merging heritage to create beautiful new traditions. Below, we run through some of our favourite Christmas traditions around the world, and ask the Traveller editors about their own personal festive rituals. From seafood feasts on Christmas Day in Australia to surfing Santa’s taking to the waves in SoCal, these are the most wholesome traditions for some festive joy this season.

Christmas is not recognized as a national holiday in Japan, but the festive season is still celebrated—albeit slightly differently from how you’d expect. As part of a marketing campaign launched in 1974, a few years after KFC first opened in Japan, the brand came up with the slogan ‘Kentucky for Christmas’, and it stuck. On Christmas Day, many Japanese families tuck into a full feast of fried chicken—a tradition so popular that you often have to order weeks in advance to make sure you’ve got your bucket guaranteed for the big day. The original Christmas meal even came with a bottle of wine. Christmas cake is just as surprising in Japan, too—strawberry shortcake, made with layers of sponge, whipped cream and fresh strawberries, is a festive favourite.

As you might imagine, food is at the heart of Christmas celebrations in Italy. Every region has its own traditions and dishes, enjoyed on Christmas Eve, Christmas Day and throughout the festive season. While northern Italians might tuck into tortellini in broth and other comforting dishes, in the south we go all out. In my family, we have twelve different dishes—mostly fish-based—served for Christmas Eve dinner, before indulging in traditional desserts and an unholy amount of nuts while playing tombola. If there is one tradition that the whole country shares, it's the rather quirky tradition of La Befana – a witch-like old woman who flies through the skies on a broomstick on the night of January 5, leaving gifts for children to discover on the morning of 6 January, known as Epiphany (Epifania in Italian). Well-behaved children receive sweets and presents, while naughty ones get coal – although these days it's usually sweet, sugary charcoal, which is actually delicious.

The real difference between the Scottish festive season and that of much of the rest of the world is Hogmanay. Once Christmas Day is done and dusted, the focus is on New Year’s—or, as the Scots call it, Hogmanay. Street parties in cities such as Edinburgh roll on until the early hours, Robert Burns’ Auld Lang Syne is sung at midnight, and the ‘first-footing’ custom takes place—the first visitor to a home after midnight is believed to bring good luck for the year ahead (along with traditional gifts such as whisky, shortbread or a lump of coal, of course). Traditionally, a tall, dark-haired man is considered the luckiest first-footer.

While much of the Northern Hemisphere is wrapping up against the cold, Christmas in Australia falls in the middle of summer, meaning festive celebrations often revolve around the beach, sunshine and outdoor gatherings. "The traditional Christmas lunch in Australia is usually seafood, a roast ham served cold, lots of salads and then a pavlova for dessert," explains Lauren Burvill, CNT’s senior commerce writer and resident Aussie. "After lunch the classic traditions are to play cricket in the backyard, or if you're lucky, on the beach. It's not unusual to see Santa on the beaches as well, often riding on an inflatable rescue boat from the local surf club. On Boxing Day the country's favourite pastime is to watch the cricket or the Sydney to Hobart yacht race."

Forget snowy landscapes and cosy fireplaces—in Southern California, you might spot Santa Claus swapping his sleigh for a surfboard during the festive season. "I grew up in greater Los Angeles, and though December isn't typically beach weather, something uncanny almost always happens on Christmas," explains Megan Spurrell, Conde Nast Traveler US’s associate articles director. "The temperatures spike to the mid-70s Fahrenheit with shining sun and many families, including my own, end up at the beach. But we also go to look out for a very SoCal figure, known as Surfing Santa. We keep our eyes out at Manhattan and Hermosa Beach, especially near the piers, though I've heard of surfing santas in Malibu and Santa Monica, too. Most are just local dudes with a Santa suit who reliably hit the waves on Christmas morning to entertain their neighbours (and get a surf session in)."

While many Western traditions see Santa leaving lumps of coal in stockings for badly behaved children, in Austria it's the terrifying Krampus who comes to punish them. As the folklore goes, the Krampus is a fearsome horned figure with a long, snake-like tongue, who accompanies Saint Nicholas on 5 December, otherwise known as Krampusnacht, the eve of Saint Nicholas Day. While Saint Nicholas rewards well-behaved children with sweets and small gifts, Krampus is said to frighten or punish those who have been naughty. Today, the tradition is celebrated with Krampuslauf parades, during which people dressed in elaborate horned masks and furry costumes take to the streets, particularly in Alpine towns and villages. The folklore even inspired a Christmas comedy horror film in 2015, starring Adam Scott and Toni Collette.

In Peru, Christmas Eve, known as Nochebuena, is the main event, with families gathering for a festive dinner and celebrations that often continue well past midnight. "We alternate years of spending Christmas in Southern California with my mom and sisters, and going to Lima, Peru, with my husband's family" says Megan. "Though Peru has plenty of traditions tied to Christmas, perhaps my favourite is the sheer fact (shared in many Catholic and Latin American countries) that the whole affair begins at midnight on the 24th. It can be torture waiting until midnight proper to dig into the feast of turkey and arroz arabe that sits out waiting, but the entire city explodes in fireworks when the clock strikes 12. And then the party doesn't stop until the wee hours of the morning, sometimes at a family member's house or at the city's bars and clubs. It almost feels like a preview of New Year's Eve, just a few days earlier."

Dumplings are a major part of Polish cuisine day to day, and Christmas is no exception. The traditional Christmas Eve supper, known as Wigilia, begins when the first star appears in the sky, with families gathering around the Christmas table for a feast that traditionally includes 12 meatless dishes. "My favourite part of seasonal eating has never been the Great British Christmas Roast, but instead 'Polish Christmas', celebrated by my family on Christmas Eve, a haphazard tribute to the Polish side of my family tree," says Olivia Squire, Traveller's features editor. "When I was a child, my grandmother used to make pierogi (Polish dumplings filled with mashed potato and cottage cheese) from scratch, which in more recent years has been taken over by my mum and I and usually involves a lot of swearing and tears. It's all worth it when we sit down to eat them alongside a vegetarian version of bigos (hunter's stew—an insult to my meat-loving ancestors), breaded fish and holy wafers, soundtracked by Carol's at King's, my English dad's addition."

Forget spending a fortune on Christmas presents—in Germany, the tradition of Schrottwichteln puts a rather amusing twist on exchanging gifts, with friends and family swapping unwanted, bizarre, or downright useless objects instead.

"For gifts, we follow the German tradition of Schrottwichteln (literally 'junk Secret Santa'), where everyone brings something completely useless, unwanted or gloriously ridiculous, often something they were given once and never knew what to do with. The more pointless, tacky or absurd, the better," Karin Mueller, Traveller's visuals director, tells us. "Our children have grown up with this ritual, and what began as a cosy dinner has become the highlight of the year. They never miss it. Now that they’re young adults, they bring their partners along, and it’s turned into this wonderfully chaotic, joyful, very-London celebration that I’m pretty sure they’ll keep going long after we stop hosting."

Across England, Christmas is celebrated with familiar traditions, from exchanging gifts to tucking into mince pies and singing Christmas carols. But each county and neighbourhood has its own customs, too. In some seaside towns, brave locals take part in a Boxing Day dip—sprinting into the freezing sea to shake off the Christmas excesses, often while raising money for charity. There's also the age-old custom of burning a Yule log, traditionally associated with good fortune and protection from misfortune. In Cornwall, some communities gather in local pubs for a traditional Cornish ‘shout’, joining together to sing folk songs and sea shanties.

"In the hazy betwixmas period, the days of lounging on the sofa and gorging on Terry’s chocolate orange are punctuated by one magical evening at a local pub for a Cornish 'shout'", explains Lucy Bruton, CNT's social media manager. "A shout refers to the community coming together to sing sea shanties and folk songs in the pub, and the Lerryn (where my family live in Cornwall) shout is a particularly special one to me. The tiny pub feels packed to the eaves with people of all ages belting out some of my favourite Cornish songs – from "Trelawney" to "Lamorna" and, of course, “Cornwall My Home”. It’s dark, cosy and a real slice of the magic of Cornwall."

After a Democratic Victory in Midterm Elections, Trump Focuses on Touting His Ballroom: ‘This Is a Special Structure’

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After a midterm election that saw the Democrats take control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate on Tuesday night, President Donald Trump spent Wednesday ignoring the results and instead stumping for his $400 million plan to build a ballroom for the White House.

Trump’s ballroom has been controversial from the start, not only because he demolished the White House’s East Wing to make room for it but also because he said it would include a military complex beneath the structure. A series of challenges, both from past presidents and US courts, have questioned whether Trump even has the authority to undertake such a project. And yet, he has largely stuck to his guns, once even labeling the ballroom “THE GREATEST OF ITS KIND!” Related Articles In Fiery Court Filing, Democratic Lawmaker Says Kennedy Center's Closure Is Meant to Hide Its Failure Under Trump Former Presidents Obama, Clinton Mock Trump's White House Interiors With Comparisons to 'Saddam Hussein' and 'Liberace's Bathroom'

Whether Trump would be able to fully proceed with the ballroom plan under a Democratic-majority House and Senate remains an open question. The ballroom has been the subject of a lawsuit brought by the National Trust for Historic Preservation, which aimed to halt its construction. In September, the Supreme Court ruled that it could go forward, saying the National Trust didn’t have the legal grounds to bring the suit.

But Trump seemed totally unbothered Wednesday as he spoke during a ceremony intended to mark the raising of the final beam on the structure. “This is a special structure,” he said, according to the New York Times. “It’s going to be not only one of the biggest, but it’ll be the most beautiful ballroom, I think, anywhere in the world.”

Regarding his repeated efforts to add to or amend preexisting structures in Washington, D.C., he said, “You know, it was sort of mentioned a little bit during the campaign: ‘Why does Trump spend time on the White House?’ Well, first of all, it’s very little time. Second of all, I like it. It’s good. For me, it’s relaxing.”
